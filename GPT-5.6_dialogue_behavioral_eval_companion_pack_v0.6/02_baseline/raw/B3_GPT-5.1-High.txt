### 1. A Mother and Her Adult Son

“Are you keeping this?”  
Mark held up a lopsided clay turtle, its shell painted a streaky, determined blue.

His mother looked up from the box she was taping. “That ugly thing? No.”

“Wow.” He studied it. “Second grade me is crushed.”

Elena snorted. “Second grade you cried because your turtle’s head fell off and you blamed the art teacher.” She reached for it. “Give it here. I’ll… consider it.”

“Uh-huh.” He set it gently on the windowsill. The room was bare in a way he’d never seen, walls pale where posters once hung. “It’s weird. It looks smaller.”

“You grew,” she said. “The room didn’t.”

He ran a hand along the old desk. “Feels like we’re burying someone.”

“We’re selling a house, not hosting a funeral.” She folded the flaps of another box, pressing hard. “Besides, you’re the one leaving me.”

“Again with the guilt,” he said lightly. “I’m only moving across—”

“Across town, yes, yes, I know.” She waved a hand. “Thirty minutes away, unless there’s traffic, which there always is, and then—”

“Mom.”

She stopped, the tape dispenser hovering. “What?”

He hesitated, fingers tracing the groove his chair had worn into the floor. “It’s not… exactly across town.”

Her eyes narrowed. “What does ‘not exactly’ mean, Mark?”

He took a breath. “I got the job in Berlin.”

Silence folded into the room, thick as dust.

“You… What job?” Her voice was flat.

“The design firm I told you about? The one with the internship abroad? They offered me a full position.” He tried to smile. “It’s a big deal.”

“You told me they turned you down.”

“I told you they hadn’t called back.” He shifted his weight. “They called.”

“When?”

“A month ago.”

“A month.” She put the tape dispenser down carefully, as if it might explode. “And you’re telling me now?”

“I didn’t want to jinx it. I had to sort visas, and—”

“You didn’t want to tell your mother that you’re moving to another continent?” Her voice cracked on the last word.

“Mom, it’s not like that. It’s a year contract. Maybe two. I’ll visit. We have FaceTime—”

“FaceTime,” she repeated, laughing once, sharply. “Your father promised me ‘cheap long-distance’ when he took that job in Chicago. You remember how that went.”

He winced. “I’m not Dad.”

“No,” she said. “You’re not.” She sank onto the stripped bed, pressing her palms to her knees. “When do you leave?”

“Three weeks.”

“Three—” She broke off, staring at the empty bookshelf. “So you pack up your room, you pack up your life, and you fly away. Just like that.”

“Not ‘just like that.’ I worked for this. You know I did.”

“I know.” She rubbed her forehead. “I also know you sat in that chair and cried before your first day of kindergarten because you didn’t want to leave me.”

“I was five.”

“Sometimes,” she said quietly, “you still look five.”

He sat beside her, the bed frame creaking. “You’re the one who taught me to chase opportunities. Remember? ‘Don’t let this town be the edge of your world.’”

“I didn’t think you were listening.” She gave him a sideways look. “I thought you were too busy playing video games.”

“I was,” he said. “But I heard that.” He swallowed. “I’m scared too, you know.”

“Are you?” She searched his face. “Because you look very… decided.”

“I signed the contract.” He met her eyes. “But on the train back, I kept thinking about you here. Alone. The house gone. Dad gone. Me gone. And I hated myself for wanting it anyway.”

She flinched at the word “Dad,” then exhaled. “You’re not responsible for filling all the empty spaces in my life, Mark.”

“Sometimes it feels like I am,” he said.

“And that,” she replied, “is exactly why I didn’t tell you until after the realtor came that I was selling the house.”

His head snapped toward her. “Wait, what?”

She gave a small, tired smile. “See? We’re both cowards.”

“Where are you going to live?”

“An apartment. Near the library. Fewer stairs. Less roof to leak.” She reached for his hand. “I was afraid if I told you, you’d stay.”

“I thought if I told you, you’d ask me to.”

“I wouldn’t have,” she said. “I would have wanted to. Very much. But I wouldn’t have.”

He squeezed her fingers. “So… we’re both leaving home.”

“Seems so.” She blinked rapidly. “I’m still mad at you.”

“I know.”

“And you will call me,” she said. “Not just send me pictures of German beer and tall blond people.”

“They’re not all blond,” he protested. “And I’ll call. Every week. Twice a week.”

“Every Sunday,” she corrected. “Like church. You can confess your sins to me.”

“Deal.” He leaned his head against her shoulder, the way he had when he was small. “Can I take the turtle?”

“No,” she said. “The turtle stays with me.”

“Why?”

She ran a thumb over its cracked, lopsided shell. “Because I was there when the head fell off. And I will be here when you come back.”

### 2. Two Old Diplomats

“Is it just me,” Sir Robert said, “or are the chairs at these conferences getting lower?”

Ambassador Chen adjusted his tie, peering over his glasses at the nameplate in front of them: RETIRED SENIOR STATESMEN – PANEL DISCUSSION. “It’s your spine that’s getting shorter, Robert, not the chairs.”

“Charming as ever.” Robert glanced at the big screen behind them, where a news ticker crawled under footage of a burning oil depot. “Ah. There it is. We retire, and the children set fire to the world again.”

Chen’s eyes lingered on the map in the corner of the screen. “Border town?”

“Looks like it.” Robert took a sip of lukewarm water. “Almost… familiar, isn’t it?”

“Don’t start.” Chen shifted, his old knee protesting. “I can still smell that bunker in ’88.”

“You and your bunker.” Robert smiled faintly. “It was barely a bunker. More like a glorified basement.”

“A basement with two nuclear powers on the phone,” Chen said. “If we failed, there would have been no upstairs to go back to.”

“Fair point.” Robert sighed. “Do you ever watch this rubbish”—he nodded at the TV—“and wonder whether anything we did actually… stuck?”

Chen considered. “Sometimes.” He paused. “Mostly when I can’t sleep.”

“You still can’t sleep?”

“Old men need less sleep,” Chen said. “We have less future to prepare for.”

Robert chuckled. “Depress the audience before we even go on stage, why don’t you.”

“They can’t hear us yet.” Chen looked at the rows of empty chairs filling slowly. “Besides, they didn’t invite us here to be uplifting. We’re here for the gravitas. The cautionary tales.”

“The museum pieces,” Robert said.

“That too.”

He glanced sideways at Chen. “Do you know who negotiated this latest ceasefire?”

“A young woman from your old ministry, I believe.” Chen brushed an imaginary speck from his sleeve. “I read her statement. Very… sharp.”

“She was an intern when I left.” Robert shook his head. “She used to correct my PowerPoint fonts. Now she’s trying to persuade two generals not to lob rockets at each other.”

“She won’t persuade them,” Chen said. “She’ll give them a story they can tell their own people. About why it’s noble to stop.”

“That’s not very flattering.”

“It’s accurate,” Chen replied. “Flattery is for banquets; accuracy is for basements.”

Robert watched the footage cut to a press conference. “You never did like speeches.”

“I liked results,” Chen said.

“Did you, though?” Robert swirled the water in his glass, its surface trembling slightly in his arthritic hand. “Or did we just like… not-disasters. We celebrated things not happening.”

“You say that like it’s trivial.” Chen tapped his cane once against the floor. “Millions of people don’t know our names because they didn’t die. I can live with that.”

Robert smiled without humor. “I do live with it. Mostly well. But then I look at this”—he jerked his chin at the screen—“and I think, we froze things. We didn’t fix them.”

“We weren’t gods,” Chen said. “We were accountants. We managed risk. We delayed stupidity.”

“And stupidity charged interest,” Robert muttered.

A young organizer appeared at the edge of the stage. “Ambassador Chen? Sir Robert? We’re starting in five minutes. Do you need anything?”

“An audience that listens,” Chen said. “And chairs that don’t insult our backs.”

The organizer laughed uncertainly and retreated.

Robert leaned closer. “What are you going to tell them? On the panel.”

“The truth,” Chen said.

“Which one? We have so many.”

“That power makes cowards,” Chen replied. “That compromise feels like failure until you see the alternative. That shouting on television is not the same as negotiation.” He glanced at Robert. “You?”

“That most of what we call ‘history’ was accidents we pretended were plans.” Robert rubbed his temple. “And that you should always know where the back door is.”

Chen grunted approval. “Practical as ever.”

Robert hesitated. “Do you ever regret… that night? The offer they made you?”

Chen’s jaw tightened. “The secret deal that would have ‘solved’ everything by sacrificing half a country?”

“Yes.”

“No.” Chen’s voice was firm. “I regret the ten years it took for the rest of the world to admit we were right to refuse. But I don’t regret refusing.”

“I thought you might have softened.” Robert looked genuinely curious.

“I’m old, not senile,” Chen said. “Do you regret backing me?”

Robert watched the burning depot on the screen again, the orange plume licking the night. “Every time someone dies in that region,” he said quietly, “I wonder what that other timeline looks like. The one where we said yes. I can’t decide if it’s better or worse.”

Chen followed his gaze. “That’s because you think like a novelist. I think like an engineer. The structure would have collapsed either way. We just refused to be the ones to sign off on faulty blueprints.”

“Still,” Robert murmured. “All that… almost.”

“And yet,” Chen said, “here we are. Two old men in suits, invited to talk about ‘lessons learned’ while the same fires burn.”

“You sound bitter.”

“I’m tired,” Chen said. “There’s a difference.”

“And what do we tell them about that?” Robert nodded toward the waiting microphones. “About the tiredness?”

Chen picked up his cane. “We tell them they don’t get to be tired yet.”

### 3. Two Schoolgirls

“You’re chewing that like it murdered your family,” Priya said.

Lena looked down at her mangled straw wrapper. “Shut up.”

Priya took a dramatic slurp of her bubble tea. The tiny convenience store buzzed under bad fluorescent lights; outside, the late-afternoon traffic hissed past in the rain. “You’re going to rip it, and then you’ll be mad your straw is naked.”

“I’m not mad,” Lena muttered.

“You’re sulking. It’s different.” Priya poked her arm. “Is this still about Ms. Hart and the math test?”

“No.” Lena scrunched the wrapper into a ball. “Sort of. Maybe. I don’t know.”

Priya raised an eyebrow. “You got a B. That’s like an A for normal people.”

“For my parents, it’s like I set the house on fire.”

“They’d probably be impressed by that, actually,” Priya said. “Very efficient combustion.”

Lena laughed despite herself. “Idiot.”

“Always.” Priya hopped up onto the low freezer, swinging her legs. “So what’s actually going on? Because you’ve been weird all day. And not your usual weird, like ‘I wrote fanfiction in history class’ weird. Extra weird.”

Lena stared at the condensation ring her drink was making on the counter. “Promise you won’t freak out?”

“I once found a spider in my shoe and didn’t cry,” Priya said. “I am un-freak-out-able.”

“I’m serious.”

Priya sobered. “Okay. I promise. No freaking.”

Lena took a breath that felt too big for her chest. “My parents want to move. To Maple Ridge.”

Priya blinked. “That’s… like, twenty minutes away.”

“In Vancouver.”

“Oh.” The word landed heavily. “Like, Vancouver-Vancouver.”

“Yeah.” Lena crushed the straw wrapper harder. “My dad got a job. They’ve been talking about it for months, but I thought— I don’t know what I thought. That it was just them talking. Like when my mom says she’s going to start running.”

“When?” Priya asked.

“Summer. After school’s out.”

“That’s—” Priya counted silently. “Like, nine weeks.”

“Eight,” Lena said hoarsely. “Seven and a half.”

For a moment, the store noise — the fridge hum, the bell at the door, someone at the lottery machine — seemed very far away.

Priya finally said, “Well. That sucks.”

Lena let out a shaky laugh. “That’s your pep talk?”

“You told me not to freak out,” Priya said. “Freaking out would involve screaming and probably shoplifting, and Mr. Park”—she jerked her chin toward the bored cashier—“already hates us.”

“He doesn’t hate us,” Lena said automatically.

“He glared at me for twenty minutes when I dropped that jar of pickles last week. I think we’re on a list.”

Lena swallowed. “I don’t want to go.”

“I kind of assumed,” Priya said softly.

“I just got used to… everything.” Lena gestured vaguely. “School, and knowing where all my classes are, and not getting lost in the stupid science wing, and actually having a friend who doesn’t think my anime crushes are embarrassing—”

“They are embarrassing,” Priya said. “But I support you in your crimes.”

“And now I have to start over.” Lena’s eyes sharpened. “What if I don’t make any friends there?”

“You will.” Priya nudged her knee. “You’re loud. People notice you.”

“That’s not the same as liking me.”

Priya hesitated, then said, “You could make terrible fan art of them. That worked on me.”

“You liked me because I let you copy my French homework,” Lena said.

“Details,” Priya said. “The point is, you’ll be fine.”

“Will you be fine?” Lena asked quietly.

Priya’s fingers tightened around her cup. “Why wouldn’t I be?”

“Because I won’t be here.”

Priya stared at the floor tiles. “I’ll… I’ll still have band. And debate. And Hannah, I guess.” Her mouth twisted. “She’s okay when she’s not trying to date literally everyone.”

“You hate debate,” Lena said.

“I hate losing,” Priya corrected. “Debate just gives me more opportunities to suffer.”

Lena tried to read her expression. “Say what you’re actually thinking.”

“I’m thinking,” Priya said slowly, “that I don’t want you to go. And also that your dad should have taken a job in, like, Burnaby instead. Or the grocery store down the street. They always need baggers.”

“He’s a software engineer.”

“He could engineer the self-checkout machines so they don’t yell at me when I forget to put bananas in the bag.”

Lena laughed, then wiped at her eyes. “My mom said we can visit. At Christmas. And maybe summer.”

Priya nodded too quickly. “Yeah. And we’ll text all the time. And video call. And send each other pictures of our ugly classmates.”

“You’ll forget about me,” Lena blurted.

Priya looked up sharply. “Wow. Rude.”

“I mean, like— you’ll make new friends. And I’ll just be this… old thing. Like a childhood blanket in the attic.”

Priya hopped down from the freezer and stood directly in front of her. “Lena Kim. Look at me.”

Lena did.

“I am far too lazy to make a whole new best friend,” Priya said. “Do you have any idea how long it took me to find someone who knows all the words to that horrible K-pop song and will sing it with me in gym class?”

“Two weeks,” Lena murmured.

“Exactly. I refuse to invest that kind of time again.” Priya shrugged. “So you’re stuck with me. Long distance.”

Lena’s throat worked. “You promise?”

“Yes. And if you come back in, like, three years, I’ll be waiting at the front door of school with a dumb sign and probably a boombox even though it’ll be illegal by then.”

Lena snorted. “Okay, now you’re being dramatic.”

“Someone has to be,” Priya said. “You’re too busy moping.”

The bell chimed as someone left. Rain lashed the window harder.

“Can we not tell anyone yet?” Lena asked. “I don’t want Hannah to make it a whole thing.”

“Our secret,” Priya agreed. “Just you, me, and Mr. Park, who is eavesdropping.”

“I don’t understand you girls,” Mr. Park called without looking up from his phone.

“See?” Priya whispered. “He loves us.”

Lena leaned her head on Priya’s shoulder for a brief second. “I don’t want to say goodbye.”

“Then don’t,” Priya said. “Not yet. For now, just… say ‘see you later.’ Even if ‘later’ is, like, two time zones and three connecting flights away.”

### 4. A Married Couple After an Affair

The car idled by the river, windshield wipers ticking back and forth with lazy insistence. The city lights blurred in the wet glass.

“I hate that we parked here,” Julia said. “This is where you proposed.”

David’s hands tightened on the steering wheel. “Do you want me to move?”

“No.” She stared at the dark water. “I just… hate it.”

He let go of the wheel slowly. “Okay.”

They sat in the hum of the heater for a while, the silence between them as solid as the dashboard.

“So,” he said at last. “Therapy was… something.”

“You mean when a stranger asked us when we last had sex, and I wanted to climb out the window?” Julia snorted. “Yes. Something.”

“She’s trying to help,” he said.

“I know.” Julia traced a circle in the fog on her window. “I just didn’t expect her to ask me if I still loved you in the first ten minutes.”

His breath caught. “You never answered.”

“I know,” she repeated.

A car passed behind them, headlights briefly illuminating the small white scar on his chin, the one from when he’d fallen off his bike at twelve. She used to kiss that spot for luck.

“Do you?” he asked.

“That’s not a yes-or-no question right now,” she said.

“It kind of is for me,” he murmured.

She turned to look at him. “Did you love her?”

He flinched. “I told you—”

“You told me you don’t even know what that word means anymore,” Julia cut in. “You told me it was ‘comfort’ and ‘escape’ and ‘not about me.’ But you didn’t answer the question.”

He inhaled. “I… cared about her. I cared about how she made me feel. But it wasn’t—”

“Love,” Julia supplied.

“It wasn’t this,” he said, gesturing helplessly between them. “It wasn’t history and bills and parents and…” He swallowed. “And fights about stupid things and laughing at three in the morning because the baby spit up on your face.”

Her mouth twitched unwillingly. “That was disgusting.”

“It was,” he said. “And you were beautiful.”

“Don’t,” she said sharply. “Don’t do that.”

“What?”

“Try to be charming.” She turned back to the window. “You don’t get to say I’m beautiful for a while. That word is… contaminated.”

He nodded once. “Okay.”

More silence. A jogger passed by, their breath fogging in the cold.

“You know what I hated most today?” she said suddenly.

“My admission of how long it’s been going on?” he asked. “My list of lies?”

“The word ‘affair,’” she said. “It sounds so… glamorous. Like a movie in Paris. Not… cheap hotels on your lunch break.”

He shut his eyes. “It wasn’t Paris.”

“I know.” Her fingers tightened around her phone. “We said ‘divorce’ out loud in there.”

“We said it as an option,” he said quickly. “A… possibility we have to look at.”

“You looked relieved,” she said.

His head snapped toward her. “What? No.”

“You did.” She kept her gaze steady. “Like… like you’d been waiting for someone to give you permission to leave.”

“That’s not— Julia, that’s not what that was.” His voice cracked. “I was— I am terrified. Of losing you. Of losing the kids half the week. Of… all of it.”

“Then why did you start this?” she whispered. “Explain it to me like I’m five. Because I’ve read all the articles and I’ve listened to you say you were lonely and I still… It still feels like I tripped and fell into an alternate universe.”

He rubbed his hands over his face. “I was… empty. And angry. And I didn’t know how to talk to you without it turning into a fight about the laundry or your mother or money. And then someone laughed at my jokes and touched my arm and I felt… seen. Wanted.”

“I wanted you too,” she said tightly. “I just also wanted you to put the damn dishes in the dishwasher.”

“I know,” he said miserably. “I know that now sounds pathetic.”

“It sounds,” she said, “like you blew up our life for someone who laughed at your dad jokes.”

“They weren’t all dad jokes,” he muttered weakly.

She actually smiled, for half a second. “You’re an idiot.”

“I am.” He looked at her fully. “I am the biggest idiot I know. And I don’t know how to fix this, Jules. I can’t… I can’t undo it. I can’t pretend it didn’t happen. I can only… stay. Answer every question. Be where I say I am. Go to therapy. Delete numbers. Change routines. Whatever you want.”

“What if what I want is for you to hurt like I hurt?” she asked quietly.

He nodded. “Then I’ll sit here and hurt.”

“That’s not… healthy,” she said.

“Probably not,” he agreed. “But it’s honest.”

She exhaled, long and shuddering. “The therapist asked me what forgiveness would look like.”

“I know,” he said. “You didn’t answer that either.”

“Because I don’t know.” She pressed the heel of her hand to her eyes. “I used to think forgiveness was… saying ‘it’s okay.’ Letting it go. But it’s not okay. And I can’t let it go. It’s like this… rock in my chest.”

He forced himself to ask, “Do you… want to forgive me?”

“I don’t want to be married to someone I hate,” she said. “But sometimes I look at you and I feel… rage. Like I could… break the windshield with my hands.”

“Please don’t,” he said softly. “It’s a lease.”

She snorted in spite of herself. “Idiot.”

He waited.

“But then,” she went on, “I think about you making dinosaur pancakes for Lily last Sunday. And how you fall asleep sitting up with your glasses crooked. And how you held my hand when my dad died. And I think… how do you forgive an entire person? Not just a mistake. The person who made it.”

He nodded slowly. “Maybe… not all at once.”

She looked at him, really looked at him, as if seeing the man in her passenger seat for the first time and the thousandth.

“I don’t know if we’ll make it,” she said. “I don’t know if we should.”

“I’ll stay until you know,” he replied. “Even if what you know in the end is that you don’t want me.”

“That sounds noble,” she said, “but it also sounds like you’re putting the whole decision on me. Like if this ends, it’s because I pulled the plug.”

“That’s… fair,” he admitted.

“I need you to… fight for this,” she said. “Not just… wait for my verdict.”

“Okay,” he said. “Then let me start by saying: I want to stay married to you. I want to do the horrible repair work. I want to be the guy you proposed to by this stupid river, not the guy who cheated on you in a Holiday Inn.”

She blinked. “I proposed to you because you wouldn’t stop making pros-and-cons lists about marriage.”

“And you drew a ring on a napkin,” he said. “It had like six diamonds.”

“Seven,” she corrected automatically.

“Right.” He managed a small smile. “Maybe we can… draw a new napkin. With… less razzle-dazzle.”

“That might be the worst metaphor you’ve ever used,” she said.

“Therapist said to be honest, not eloquent,” he replied.

Rain drummed on the roof, steady and relentless.

“Let’s go home,” she said finally.

“Together?” he asked.

“For tonight,” she said. “Tomorrow… we’ll see.”

He nodded, turned the key, and pulled away from the river where, years ago, they’d promised each other a future they hadn’t understood, and now, uncertainly, were trying to promise again.

### 5. An Officer and a Subordinate

“Close the door, Sergeant.”

Cole hesitated just long enough for Captain Ramirez to notice, then shut it behind him. The flimsy plywood shivered in its frame.

“Sit,” Ramirez said.

Cole remained standing. “I’d rather not, sir.”

Ramirez’s jaw flexed. “That wasn’t a suggestion.”

Cole lowered himself into the metal chair, his uniform still dust-streaked from the afternoon’s patrol. Behind Ramirez, the map on the wall was studded with colored pins, like someone had tried to turn chaos into geometry.

“You disobeyed a direct order,” Ramirez began.

“I saved a kid,” Cole shot back.

“You endangered your unit,” Ramirez countered.

Cole’s hands curled into fists on his knees. “With respect, sir, my unit was behind cover. That boy wasn’t.”

“And now Corporal Diaz is in the field hospital with shrapnel in his leg,” Ramirez said. “Because you broke formation and forced everyone to adjust to you.”

Cole’s gaze dropped for a second. “I didn’t know there was a second IED.”

“That’s the point.” Ramirez leaned forward. “You never know. That’s why you stick to the plan.”

“The plan didn’t include a twelve-year-old in the middle of the road,” Cole said hotly.

“No,” Ramirez said quietly. “The plan didn’t include a twelve-year-old in the middle of the road. The plan was built around minimizing risk to my people.”

“And civilians,” Cole added. “I thought that was the whole point of those pre-deployment lectures. ‘Hearts and minds,’ sir.”

“This isn’t about hearts and minds,” Ramirez snapped. “This is about command and control.”

Cole’s jaw tightened. “Is there a difference?”

“Yes.” Ramirez’s stare was steady. “One gets people killed faster.”

Silence pulsed between them, thick with generator hum and distant helicopter rotors.

“I saw his face,” Cole said finally, voice lower. “He looked like my little brother. He was crying. He kept looking from us to the bomb like he was… apologizing. I couldn’t just… leave him there.”

“I’m not asking you to be made of stone,” Ramirez said. “I’m asking you to trust that sometimes we have to walk past things we can’t fix.”

“Well, I couldn’t,” Cole said. “Not this time.”

Ramirez rubbed a hand over his face. “You’re a good soldier, Cole. One of the best. That’s why this scares the hell out of me.”

“Scares you, sir?”

“Because good soldiers with big hearts make terrible martyrs,” Ramirez said. “And I don’t have enough men to lose one to hero syndrome.”

“I wasn’t trying to be a hero,” Cole muttered.

“Then what were you trying to be?”

“Human,” Cole said. “Sir.”

Ramirez exhaled slowly. “Do you think I’ve never seen kids in the wrong place at the wrong time? Do you think you’re the first one who’s wanted to run out there screaming and scoop them all up?”

“No, sir,” Cole said, more quietly.

“I have ten years on you and three more tours,” Ramirez went on. “I have learned that if I let every face get to me, I stop being able to see the bigger picture. The one where if I get you killed, the rest of the squad goes home in pieces too.”

“I get that,” Cole said. “But today, it worked. The kid’s alive. We’re all— mostly— alive.”

“Mostly is what the letters to Diaz’s family will say if he loses that leg,” Ramirez replied. “’He’s mostly alive.’ See how that sounds.”

Cole flinched. “So what happens now?”

“I file my report,” Ramirez said. “I note that you disobeyed a direct order under fire. That your action contributed to a casualty. I also note that your action prevented a civilian death.”

Cole swallowed. “Am I— am I getting Article Fifteen’d?”

“Probably,” Ramirez said. “Minimum. Loss of pay. Extra duty. It could be worse.”

“Could be court-martial,” Cole said.

“It won’t be,” Ramirez said firmly. “Because I’m not recommending it.”

Cole looked up, startled. “Sir?”

“I need you here,” Ramirez said. “I need that instinct that made you move. I just need it… disciplined. Aimed. Not firing off in every direction because your heart’s breaking.”

“You want me to sit on my hands next time?” Cole asked bitterly.

“I want you to come to me first,” Ramirez said. “Shout in my radio. ‘Sir, there’s a kid in the kill zone. Request permission to retrieve.’ You give me the chance to own that decision. That’s my job. To carry the weight, so you don’t have to try to juggle it alone under fire.”

“And if you say no?” Cole asked.

“Then you follow the order,” Ramirez said. “And you hate me for it. And I hate myself for it. And we bring ten people home instead of nine.”

Cole stared at the scuffed floor. “I don’t know if I can do that, sir.”

“I know,” Ramirez said quietly. “That’s why leadership sucks.”

Cole looked up. “With respect, you don’t sound like you believe half of what you just said.”

Ramirez’s mouth twisted. “With respect, Sergeant, I believe all of it. And I also believe that if I were the one out front today instead of you, I might have done the same damn thing.”

“So why—”

“Because my job,” Ramirez interrupted, “is to be the person who remembers the spreadsheet of lives and losses when everyone else is staring at one cell.”

Cole frowned. “That’s a messed-up way to put it.”

“It is,” Ramirez said. “War is a messed-up spreadsheet.”

They were both silent for a beat.

“Visit Diaz in the hospital,” Ramirez said. “Today. Apologize.”

“I already—”

“Do it again,” Ramirez said. “Mean it. And listen to what he says back. Maybe he’ll call you an idiot. Maybe he’ll say he’d have done the same thing. Either way, you hear it.”

“Yes, sir,” Cole said.

“And tomorrow,” Ramirez added, “you’re on patrol again.”

Cole blinked. “You’re not pulling me from the line?”

“I thought about it,” Ramirez said. “Then I decided I’d rather have you where I can see you than sitting in the TOC stewing about what ifs.”

Cole nodded slowly. “Understood.”

“And Cole?” Ramirez waited until the younger man met his gaze. “Don’t you dare make me write a letter to your mother because you wanted to save the whole world by yourself. That’s an order.”

Cole’s throat bobbed. “Yes, sir.”

“Dismissed.”

Cole stood, saluted. Ramirez returned it. The door clicked shut behind him, leaving Ramirez alone with the map, the pins, and the echo of a boy’s face in a dusty road.

### 6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The airport at midnight felt like an afterthought — half the kiosks closed, announcements echoing in a mostly empty terminal.

“Gate 47,” the overhead voice droned. “Departure delayed. New estimated boarding time: 1:35 a.m.”

A few people groaned. Most just sagged further into their plastic chairs.

Nora shifted her laptop bag off the seat beside her as a man approached, balancing a paper cup and a backpack. “Anyone sitting here?”

“No,” she said. “Go ahead.”

“Thanks.” He dropped into the chair, exhaling. “They say ‘weather in Chicago,’ but I’m pretty sure that’s code for ‘the pilot is napping.’”

She half-smiled. “Or they lost the plane.”

“Comforting,” he said. He took a sip of his coffee and made a face. “This tastes like burnt cardboard.”

“You bought coffee at midnight,” Nora said. “You did this to yourself.”

“Point.” He wiped his hand on his jeans, then glanced sideways at her. “I’m Jamal, by the way. Misery seatmate for the next… what, two hours?”

“Nora,” she said.

He nodded at her closed laptop. “You look like someone who’s about to be very productive.”

“I was,” she said. “Until delay number three.”

“Ah.” He checked the departures board. “Going or coming home?”

“Going,” she said. “Conference. You?”

“Coming,” he said. “My sister’s wedding. The rehearsal dinner was… an event.”

“Bad?” she asked.

“Let’s just say when your uncle starts a speech with ‘Back in my day, women knew—’” He trailed off, grimacing.

“Oof,” she said.

“Exactly.” He eyed her carry-on. “You’re traveling light.”

“Two days, one presentation, one mildly condescending panel of senior partners,” Nora said. “Anything more than this is unnecessary suffering.”

“Law?” he guessed.

“Consulting,” she corrected. “You?”

“Software,” he said. “I build boring things that make other boring things slightly less boring.”

She smiled faintly. “Specific.”

He shrugged. “My mom still tells people I ‘do computers.’”

“Mine tells people I ‘do business,’” Nora said. “As if that covers anything.”

“Parents are vague on principle,” he said. “It’s in the handbook.”

They fell into a companionable silence, punctuated by the squeak of a cleaning cart.

“You live in Minneapolis?” he asked after a minute.

“For now,” she said. “You?”

“Chicago. Hence my deep suspicion of ‘weather in Chicago.’ I’ve seen the radar. It’s fine.”

“Maybe the pilot is genuinely napping,” she said.

“If he is, I hope he’s good and rested,” Jamal replied. “I plan to be unconscious before we even take off.”

“Lucky,” Nora said. “I can’t sleep on planes.”

“Fear of crashing?” he asked.

“Fear of drooling on strangers,” she said. “Equally tragic.”

He laughed. “Fair.”

A woman at the end of the row started a loud phone conversation. Nora winced.

“So,” Jamal said, lowering his voice conspiratorially, “what’s the presentation about? On a scale from ‘networking’ to ‘paradigm shift.’”

She rolled her eyes. “Customer segmentation in mid-tier markets.”

He placed a hand over his heart. “Be still, my beating soul.”

“It’s worse,” she said. “There are pie charts.”

“Monstrous,” he said. “You should unionize.”

“What about you?” she asked. “What boring thing do you make less boring?”

“Internal tools,” he said. “You know those forms you hate at work? I make the magic that lets them auto-fill so you hate them five percent less.”

“I appreciate your service,” she said.

“You say that now,” he replied. “Until the system goes down and everyone yells at the ‘IT guy.’”

“Do you ever… want to do something else?” she asked, then immediately frowned. “Sorry. That’s weird. Ignore me. It’s the hour.”

He considered. “Sometimes. I play music. Badly. In my head, I’m a jazz pianist in a smoky bar.”

“In reality?” she prompted.

“In reality, my downstairs neighbor leaves notes on my door that say ‘There are headphones for a reason,’” he said.

She laughed. “Brutal.”

“What about you?” he asked. “Did you always dream of… pie charts?”

“No,” she said. “I wanted to be an architect.”

“What happened?”

“My father sat me down with a spreadsheet,” she said. “Showed me average salaries. Job security. How many years of school. He made a very convincing PowerPoint.”

“And you listened,” Jamal said.

“I was eighteen,” she said. “I thought adults knew things.”

“They do,” he said. “They know how to be afraid.”

She blinked at him. “That’s… unexpectedly profound for someone drinking cardboard.”

“Don’t tell my uncle,” he said. “He’ll revoke my man card.”

They shared a small, weary smile.

“Flight 327 to Chicago now boarding all passengers,” the overhead voice announced suddenly.

Jamal looked at his boarding pass. “That’s us.”

“Finally,” Nora said.

They gathered their bags and joined the slow shuffle toward the gate.

“You window or aisle?” he asked.

“Aisle,” she said. “Easier escape.”

“You make it sound like a hostage situation,” he said.

“Have you met airline seats?” she replied.

At the jet bridge, the stream of passengers compressed. They ended up side by side again.

“So,” Jamal said, “since we’ve known each other for, what, forty-three minutes, I feel entitled to ask a deeply personal question.”

She raised an eyebrow. “Bold.”

“Is there anyone waiting for you in Chicago?” he asked. “To hear about your thrilling segmentation insights?”

She hesitated. “No. You?”

He smiled without quite reaching his eyes. “Not anymore.”

“Ah.” She didn’t push.

“Sorry,” he said. “That was… heavy.”

“It’s okay,” she said. “Airports are for existential oversharing.”

“Is that in the brochure?” he asked.

“Fine print,” she said.

They reached the plane door. The flight attendant smiled mechanically. “Welcome aboard.”

As they stepped into the narrow aisle, Jamal said, “Well, Nora-who-does-business, if we don’t end up neighbors on this metal tube, nice almost-knowing you.”

“Likewise, Jamal-who-does-computers,” she said. “May your pilot be well-rested.”

“And may your pie charts be merciful,” he replied.

They checked their seat numbers.

“18C,” she said.

“22A,” he said. “Tragic.”

“Four rows away,” she said. “A gulf between us.”

“Star-crossed,” he said dryly.

They inched apart, swallowed by strangers and overhead bins.

Later, when the cabin lights dimmed and the engines hummed them away from the wet-lit airport, Nora glanced down the aisle. She could just see the corner of Jamal’s seat, the back of his head tilted against the window.

She thought, briefly, of jazz bars and PowerPoints, of the odd intimacy of telling the truth to someone who didn’t matter and therefore, somehow, did.

Then she opened her laptop, stared at her pie charts, and began to type.
