## Scene 1: A mother and her adult son

The attic smelled like old cedar and summer dust. Mark held up a high school trophy, smeared with gray.

“Remember this?” he said. “Most Improved. Which is, you know, a nice way of saying ‘used to be terrible.’”

Elena laughed, kneeling by a box of photo albums. “You were not terrible. You were... figuring it out.”

“That sounds like a very mom thing to say.”

“It is a very mom thing to say,” she replied, sliding a photo free. “Look—your first suit. You hated the tie.”

“I hated that the tie hated my throat,” Mark said. “You look so young in this picture.”

“So do you,” she said softly. Then, after a pause, “Do you really have to take the desk? I like it here.”

“You said we should sell it,” he said. “Less to move.”

“True.” She picked up a porcelain figurine and turned it in her fingers. “Funny how things feel heavier when you’re trying to decide if you love them.”

He set the trophy down. “You okay with all this? The house going, I mean.”

Elena’s eyes drifted to the low window. “I raised you in these rafters and hallways. I’m okay.” She gave a small smile. “I’m sixty-four, not ancient.” She placed the figurine in a keep pile. “I think I’m more okay than you are.”

“That obvious?”

“You’ve been grumpy since breakfast.”

“I’m not grumpy.”

“You told the kettle it was boiling ‘too loudly.’”

He winced. “I did say that.”

She reached for a box labeled Mark—Childhood. “You’re worried I’ll be lonely,” she said. “Or that you’re leaving me.”

“I already left,” he said. He sat on the beams, legs dangling. “I moved out ten years ago.”

“Moving out is not the same as letting go,” Elena said. “Your father never quite did either.”

He glanced over. “You miss him?”

“Sometimes I miss the version of him we never quite reached,” she said. “He had plans for this attic. A train set, remember?”

“Yeah,” Mark said. “He nailed the first bit of track and then the hammer fell through the insulation.”

“He swore in three languages.” She laughed. Silence settled, warm and heavy. “You’re thinking about the last argument.”

Mark exhaled. “I said things. About how he should’ve shown up more. I told him to stop promising trips. I said I didn’t need him.”

Elena closed the album. “He knew you needed him. And he knew you were brave enough to say the opposite.” She looked at him. “He also knew how to forgive. It was one of his good skills.”

“He died before I could use that,” Mark said. “Before I could let him.”

“You’re using it right now,” she said. “On yourself.” She nudged the trophy with her toe. “Most Improved is a perfect award for living.”

He snorted. “That’s corny.”

“I’m allowed to be corny. It’s the law.” She stood up, brushed dust off her jeans. “Help me with that trunk?”

He lifted it, grunting. “What’s in here? Bricks?”

“Letters,” she said. “From when I was twenty. To no one important.”

He rested the trunk on a beam. “Do I get to read them?”

“Not yet,” she said, smiling. “Someday. When you’re most improved.”

He smiled back, then grew quiet. “When you move into the apartment, do you want me to build your bookshelves?”

“I’d like that,” she said. “And you can bring the kettle. It behaves better away from the attic.”

He nodded. “Deal.”

They filled the silence with the soft sound of paper slipping into boxes, the easy percussion of shared history—tiny sounds in a big, echoing room.

“Mom,” Mark said, not looking at her, “thanks for not making this sad.”

Elena tied a box with twine. “Oh, sweetheart,” she said, “this is the good kind of sad. The kind that makes space.”

He picked up the trophy again, squinting at the engraved plate. “Maybe I’ll take this after all.”

“Good,” she said, hefting the box labeled Keep. “It suits you.”

“Because I used to be terrible?”

“Because you keep getting better,” she answered, and together they trudged down the narrow stairs.

## Scene 2: Two old diplomats

Rain tapped the awning of the café overlooking the river. Ambassador Salim stirred his tea, eyes on the current. Across from him, Ambassador Hartley unfolded a paper napkin as if it were a treaty draft.

“You always pick places near water,” Hartley said. “You know I can’t swim.”

“I assume you can drink,” Salim replied. “Besides, it keeps us honest. Rivers are relentless. You can’t negotiate with gravity.”

Hartley snorted. “You tried, once.”

“Twice,” Salim corrected. “And I lost both times.”

They smiled. The waiter set down their plates—almond cake for Salim, soup for Hartley.

“Do you remember Vienna?” Hartley asked.

“I remember the chandelier that looked like a galaxy,” Salim said. “I remember pretending not to see you rewriting our clause with a fountain pen under the table.”

“It needed a soft verb,” Hartley said. “Shall should’ve been may.”

“Shall saves lives,” Salim said.

“May gets signed,” Hartley countered. He blew on his spoon. “How many years have we been having this argument?”

“Long enough to turn shalls into shalls-we-dance,” Salim said, tapping the edge of his cup. “You retired first. Coward.”

“Strategist,” Hartley corrected. “I left before I became someone I used to despise.”

Salim leaned back. “And did it work?”

Hartley watched a gull dart under the awning. “Depends on the day.”

“On mine,” Salim said, “I wake, read the news, and reach for the phone to call a minister who hasn’t needed me in five years.”

“You still teach?”

“I do,” Salim said. “Students with bright eyes and sharper questions. Last week, one asked me how to negotiate with a person whose career depends on not agreeing.”

“What did you tell them?” Hartley asked.

“I said, ‘Offer them a different victory.’” Salim toyed with a crumb. “And make sure they can claim it without lying.”

Hartley chuckled. “You always were kind.”

“You always were clinical,” Salim replied. “Between us, we made a decent surgeon.”

“If only we’d operated on the right patient,” Hartley said. He leaned forward. “Do you regret Oslo?”

“Which part?” Salim asked. “The airport, the handshake, the flash bulbs? The clause you turned to may?”

“The part where we knew it would unravel,” Hartley said softly. “And did it anyway.”

Salim took a sip. “Sometimes I think it’s our job to buy time.”

“For what?” Hartley asked.

“For the children who speak both languages,” Salim said. “For the seeds we never see sprout.”

Hartley wrapped his hands around the soup bowl. “Yesterday a young attaché told me we were naïve. He said the world is teeth.”

“And what did you say?”

“I said even teeth smile.”

They sat a while, listening to the river and the café’s low hum.

“Do you still keep the chessboard by the window?” Salim asked.

“I do,” Hartley said. “The bishop has a crack. I tell guests it’s from a tense gambit. It’s actually from the cat.”

“Liar,” Salim said.

“Diplomat,” Hartley corrected.

A bicyclist splashed past. Hartley wiped a raindrop from the table’s edge. “If you could change one word in your career,” he asked, “what would it be?”

Salim considered. “I would change ‘later’ to ‘now’ more often.”

“And you?”

Hartley smiled. “I would swap a few ‘never’s for ‘not yet.’”

Salim raised his cup. “To the soft verbs.”

Hartley clinked his spoon against it. “And to rivers, relentless and honest.”

They ate in companionable silence, each thinking of letters they’d written and lives they could not count, the rain writing its own preamble on the awning overhead.

## Scene 3: Two schoolgirls

The art room smelled like glue sticks and acrylics. Hannah sat cross-legged under the big window, scissors clicking, while Maya sprawled on her stomach drawing lightning bolts.

“So,” Hannah said, “for the zine launch, do we call it a ‘launch’ or a ‘birthday party’? Because if it’s a birthday party, we get cake.”

“If it’s a launch, we get to yell ‘ignition,’” Maya said. “Cake versus ignition. The struggle is real.”

“Why not both?” Hannah suggested. “Ignition cake.”

Maya drew a rocket with confetti coming out of it. “People will ask why we called it ‘Spiral.’”

“We could say ‘because we’re not linear people,’” Hannah said. “Or because of all the weird stairs in this school.”

“Or because last semester was a spiral and we survived.”

Hannah glanced at her. “Are you okay about—”

“I’m fine,” Maya said quickly. “I mean, not fine-fine. But drawing helps.” She added a tiny parachute to the rocket. “You know what else helps? Not telling Mr. Peters every time we use the good markers.”

Hannah grinned. “We live dangerously.”

“Obviously.” Maya held up a poster. “Okay, listen. ‘Spiral: a zine for people who doodle in the margins, think too loud, and believe in snacks.’”

Hannah clapped. “Perfect. Snacks are important.”

The door creaked. They both froze, then relaxed when no one came in.

“Do you think he read it?” Maya asked after a moment.

“Who?”

“Elliot,” she said, voice small. “The poem.”

“Oh,” Hannah said. She fiddled with the blunt edge of her scissors. “He... liked it.”

“Did he say that, or did he say ‘cool’?”

“Both,” Hannah admitted. “But he said ‘cool’ in italics.”

Maya groaned into her sleeve. “It was too much.”

“It was brave,” Hannah said. “And if he doesn’t know what to do with brave, that’s a him problem.”

“He’s nice,” Maya said. “He always hands the ball to the next person in P.E. I just—ugh.” She sat up. “Can we put a page in the zine that just says ugh in fifty fonts?”

“Yes,” Hannah said. “Right after the page that’s a list of ways to get through Mondays.”

“Number one, lattes,” Maya said.

“We are fourteen,” Hannah reminded her.

“Number one, illegal lattes,” Maya said, then dropped her voice. “Number one, cinnamon hot chocolate that pretends to be a latte.”

“Number two, having a friend who sits under the window with you and makes you laugh even when your feelings are a humidifier.”

“That’s disturbingly accurate,” Maya said, smiling. “Number three, blasting that song with the screaming violin.”

Hannah nodded seriously. “Necessary.”

“But seriously,” Maya said, “what if nobody shows up?”

“They’ll show up,” Hannah said. “We bribed them with stickers.”

“And snacks.”

“And ignition cake,” Hannah said.

Maya sat back, hair fanning like an ink spill. “Do you ever think about next year? Like, high school. What if we get split up?”

Hannah’s scissors paused. “Then we will text so much the Wi-Fi cries. And we’ll meet on Saturdays to make issue two.”

“Spiral: The Sequel,” Maya said.

“Spiral: Now With More Snacks,” Hannah added.

Maya looked at the poster, then at Hannah. “Thanks for not telling me to chill.”

“If anyone ever tells you to chill, I will throw a metaphor at them,” Hannah said.

“A sharp one,” Maya said.

“Obviously,” Hannah replied.

They taped their posters on the wall. Their reflections in the window looked like two characters in a comic, speech bubbles full of bright, messy words.

“Ready?” Maya asked.

“Ignition,” Hannah said.

“Cake,” Maya added.

They grinned, and the art room felt like a small, sturdy planet they were building together, page by page.

## Scene 4: A married couple after an affair

The kitchen was too clean. The counters shone the way gym floors do before a game no one wants to play. Laura folded the dish towel again even though it was already folded. Daniel stood near the fridge, hands in his pockets like they could hide him.

“So,” Laura said, the word stretched thin.

“So,” Daniel echoed.

“I made tea,” she said, nodding toward two mugs. Steam rose between them like something trying to leave.

Daniel reached for his cup, then didn’t. “I don’t know how to begin.”

“You began already,” Laura said. “In a hotel. In lies.”

He flinched. “I know.”

“Do you?” she asked, and her voice gentled. “Do you know what I’ll look for now when you’re late? Do you know what the chairs at that café feel like on the backs of my legs?”

He shut his eyes. “I’m sorry.”

“I know,” she said, rubbing her temple. “I believe you’re sorry. I just don’t know if you’re sorry about the right things.”

“What are the right things?” he asked.

“That you took something from me without asking,” she said. “That you wrote a story with my name in it and didn’t let me read it.”

He sat. The chair scraped too loud. “I was lonely.”

“I was too,” she said. “You didn’t ask me to be less lonely with you.”

“I thought you were tired of me,” he said, a small, miserable laugh. “That thing you do with your mouth when I tell a joke? I decided it meant contempt.”

“It means the joke needs work,” she said, and for a second they both almost smiled. Then Laura’s face changed. “Who is she?”

“Her name doesn’t help,” he said.

“It helps me to picture a person instead of a shadow,” Laura said. “It helps me to know whether she wears perfume I’ll smell at the grocery store.”

He swallowed. “Her name is Alyssa. We met at the conference in March. It was—”

“Simple,” Laura finished for him. “Easier than the work here.”

“It felt like oxygen,” he said, ashamed. “I don’t know why.”

“Because we forget to open windows,” she said flatly. “Because we confuse inconvenience with love failing.”

“I ended it,” he said. “I told her. It’s over.”

Laura stared at the kettle. “Are you asking me to forgive you?”

“I’m asking what you need,” he said.

She looked at him, really looked. “I need the password to your email,” she said. “I need you to answer questions when I wake at three a.m. I need you to go to therapy even when you’re ‘busy.’ I need—space.” Her voice wavered. “And I need you not to tell me to hurry up and heal.”

“I can do those,” he said quickly. “All of them.”

“You can offer,” she said. “Doing is different.” She took a breath. “Also, I need you to know I don’t know yet. About us.”

He nodded, tears bright. “Okay.”

“And I need to say this,” she added, fingers gripping the edge of the counter. “There was a moment today where I remembered your hands on our baby’s back when she had a fever, and I thought, there is goodness. There is also this other thing. Both are true. I hate that both are true.”

“I hate it too,” he said. “I wish I could make it simple.”

“We tried simple,” she said. “We called it later. Later we’ll talk. Later we’ll take a trip. Later we’ll be brave.” She exhaled. “Later arrived without us.”

The tea went cool. Outside, a delivery truck rattled past, and the kitchen clock clicked.

“Will you stay tonight?” Daniel asked.

“Yes,” she said. “In the guest room. I don’t want you to be far. And I don’t want you near.”

He nodded. “Okay.”

“One more thing,” she said, voice steady. “You don’t get to be the hero of this story yet.”

He met her eyes. “Understood.”

They stood, a river of tile between them. Laura picked up her mug, tasted it, grimaced. “It’s cold.”

“I’ll make a new pot,” he said, reaching for the kettle.

She watched him fill it. The sound of water was ordinary, almost kind. It made room for a future that wasn’t promised and a present that, stubbornly, still existed.

## Scene 5: An officer and a subordinate

The briefing room was all fluorescent honesty. Lieutenant Avery sat stiff-backed, jaw set. Captain Ruiz closed the door soft as a secret.

“Relax your shoulders,” Ruiz said.

“They’re relaxed,” Avery lied.

“Okay,” Ruiz said, taking a seat. He slid a notepad across the table. “After-action, frank and clean. Ready?”

“Yes, sir,” Avery said.

“Yesterday’s patrol,” Ruiz began. “You called an audible at checkpoint three. Why?”

Avery’s mouth was dry. “The alley felt wrong. The vendor at the corner always whistles. He didn’t.”

“You trusted your read,” Ruiz said. “Good. What did you miss?”

Avery stared at the map pinned to the wall. “I tunneled. My focus went narrow. I didn’t see the van idling two blocks down.”

“And?”

“And we were late to intercept,” Avery said. “Sanchez had to sprint. He almost ate pavement.”

Ruiz waited. “What else?”

Avery flinched. “I raised my voice on comms.”

“You yelled,” Ruiz said, not unkind. “At your people.”

“I was scared,” Avery admitted.

“Of what?” Ruiz asked.

“Of something happening I couldn’t un-happen,” Avery said quietly. “Of being the reason someone didn’t go home.”

Ruiz let that sit. The hum of the lights was too bright. “Okay,” he said. “Here’s what I saw. You made a call based on pattern recognition. That’s leadership. You also choked the net with adrenaline. That’s fixable.”

Avery nodded, a quick jerk.

Ruiz leaned forward. “You know what my first captain told me after I blew a stop at a checkpoint and made everyone do a dumb little dance around my mistake?”

“What?” Avery asked.

“He said, ‘You’re allowed to be afraid. You’re not allowed to be alone.’” Ruiz tapped the notepad. “You didn’t have to carry the whole moment by yourself. You have NCOs. You have me. You have a team.”

Avery’s throat worked. “I didn’t want to look weak.”

“Looking stubborn is worse,” Ruiz said. “You ever seen a pilot yank the stick harder and call it courage? That’s how you stall.”

Avery rubbed a hand over his face. “Sanchez won’t look at me.”

“Sanchez is nursing a bruise on his pride, not his body,” Ruiz said. “You will apologize for the yell. Not for the call.”

“Yes, sir,” Avery said.

“Now, what would you do differently?” Ruiz asked.

“Breathe,” Avery said. “Delegate. ‘Sanchez, eyes left. Park, that van’s yours. Miller, pulse comms with HQ.’ Not try to be all of them.”

“Good,” Ruiz said. “And?”

“Use plain language,” Avery added. “Not ‘possible anomaly’ at checkpoint three. Say, ‘Alley usual vendor missing, proceed with caution.’”

Ruiz smiled a little. “You’re improving yourself in real time. That’s the job.”

Avery stared down at his hands. “When do you stop hearing the what-ifs?”

Ruiz took a breath. “You don’t. You teach them to sit in the back seat and shut up while you drive.”

Avery laughed once, surprised. “Copy that.”

“Also,” Ruiz said, voice softer, “you call me at 2200 when your brain is chewing you alive. I’ll tell you about the time I dropped a map in a river and tried to pretend it was a new tactic.”

Avery looked up. “You?”

“Me,” Ruiz said. “And I still got promoted.”

A knock came at the door. Sanchez poked his head in. “Captain? LT? We’re, uh, heading to chow.”

Ruiz gestured. “Give us a minute.”

Sanchez hesitated, then said to Avery, “Hey, LT. You owe me laps.”

“I owe you an apology,” Avery said. “And laps.”

Sanchez squinted, then grinned. “I’ll take both.” He vanished.

Ruiz stood. “There. Human problem, human solution.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Last thing,” Ruiz said, hand on the door. “Next time you feel the tunnel coming, say it out loud. ‘I’m narrowing. Talk to me.’ It’s not weakness.”

“It’s flying the plane,” Avery said.

“Exactly,” Ruiz replied. “Chow time.”

As they walked out together, the lights hummed the same, but Avery’s shoulders eased a fraction, the hallway no longer a gauntlet but a corridor with people in it.

## Scene 6: Two people who barely know each other

The elevator jolted, then sighed into stillness between floors. The overhead lights kept humming defiantly. Hannah looked at the panel, then at the stranger with the green scarf.

“Did it just—”

“Yeah,” he said. “It did.”

She pressed the alarm button. A tinny voice crackled. “Maintenance. We see you’re between ten and eleven. Stay calm.”

“I’d rather levitate, but fine,” Hannah said.

The man laughed. “I’m Leo.”

“Hannah,” she said. They both shifted their grocery bags. A carton of eggs regarded them judgmentally.

“So,” Leo said, “what are you making?”

She looked down. “Omelets for dinner. It was a day.”

“Ah,” he said. “The omelet day. Classic.”

“What about you?” she asked.

He lifted a bag with a bunch of parsley sticking out. “I got ambitious and bought parsley. No plan. Just vibes.”

“Parsley is ninety percent vibes,” she said.

Silence settled, companionable and a little weird. The elevator hummed like a faraway bee.

“Do you live on ten?” Leo asked.

“Twelve,” Hannah said, nodding upward. “You?”

“Eleven,” he said, nodding in place. “Between.”

“Fitting,” she said. “You ever get stuck like this before?”

“Not since Rome,” he said.

She blinked. “Casual.”

“I was a student,” he explained. “Tiny pensione elevator. No air. A nun and a cat were also involved.”

“Please be joking.”

“Not even a little,” he said. “The cat was named Franco. He judged us all.”

Hannah smiled in spite of the day. “What were you studying?”

“Architecture,” he said. “The universe laughed and now I do project management.”

“The universe likes transferable skills,” she said. “I did theater. Now I do training videos for software.”

“Do you miss it?” he asked.

“Yes. No. Sometimes,” she said. “I miss the feeling of being someone else. I don’t miss notes that say ‘more sparkle.’ What about you? Do you miss arches?”

“Every time I see a strip mall,” he said. “But I also like getting dinner and not worrying about rent.”

“Low bar, high peace,” she said.

The panel crackled. “Maintenance again. We’re working on it.”

Hannah glared at the speaker. “That’s comforting.”

Leo shifted, then said, “Can I tell you something weird?”

“Please,” she said. “We’re in an elevator. It’s the law.”

He took a breath. “I was on my way to break up with someone.”

“Oh,” she said. “Do you want me to say ‘I’m sorry’ or ‘good for you’ or ‘want an omelet’?”

He laughed, then rubbed his neck. “Maybe none of the above. We met six months ago. She’s great. I’m... not present. And it’s not fair.”

“You sound like you’ve rehearsed that,” Hannah said gently.

“I have,” he admitted. “Out loud. In the produce aisle.”

“What did the cucumbers think?”

“They applauded,” he said. He looked at the floor numbers. “Now I’m here, delaying.”

“Maybe the elevator knew,” she said. “Builds suspense.”

“What about you?” he asked. “Why was it a day?”

Hannah hesitated. “My mother sent me a picture of a dog she wants to adopt. I said I was too busy to visit this weekend. She said, ‘I understand,’ which is Mom for ‘I do not understand.’ I feel like a heel.”

“Are you too busy?” Leo asked.

“I could make time,” she said. “I just—didn’t want to.” She looked at her hands. “And that feels uglier somehow.”

“Or honest,” he said. “Honesty isn’t always pretty.”

“Tell that to my mother’s thumbs-up emoji,” she said.

The elevator shuddered, then started to move with a low groan. Both of them grabbed the rail, then laughed, a little breathless.

“Look at us,” Leo said. “Vertical again.”

The doors slid open at eleven. He held them with his foot. “Do you want to—”

“Trade parsley for two eggs?” she finished.

He grinned. “Deal.”

They swapped items, a tiny ceremony in the fluorescent light.

“Good luck,” she said, nodding toward whatever waited down his hall.

“You too,” he said. “Text your mom a dog meme.”

“I will,” she said.

They stepped out into their separate corridors.

“Hey,” he added, turning back. “If you ever want to rehearse lines for being someone else—”

“Eleven-B?” she guessed.

“Eleven-B,” he confirmed.

“Okay,” she said. “Twelve-D.”

They smiled, strangers plus one story, and let the doors close. The elevator exhaled and rose, carrying with it a whiff of parsley and the lightest possible version of what might come next.
