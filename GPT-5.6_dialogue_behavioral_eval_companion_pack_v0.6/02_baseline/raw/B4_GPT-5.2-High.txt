## 1) A mother and her adult son

The kettle clicked off, and the kitchen filled with the soft hiss of settling heat. Mara set two mugs on the table—one chipped, one new—and watched her son pace as if the floor were a problem he could solve by walking.

“Sit, Ben,” she said. “You’re wearing a groove into my linoleum.”

Ben stopped, pressed a palm to the back of his neck. “I can’t sit. If I sit, I’ll… I’ll say it wrong.”

“You can say it wrong in a chair. I promise, the chair won’t judge you.”

He gave a short laugh that didn’t land, then pulled out the chair opposite her and perched on the edge. His knee bounced. “Okay. Okay.”

Mara wrapped her hands around her mug without drinking. “You called me at six in the morning. So either the house is on fire, or your heart is.”

Ben stared at the steam. “Do you remember when I was ten and I broke the neighbor’s window? With the baseball?”

“Of course. You blamed the wind.”

“It was the wind,” he said automatically, then sighed. “Anyway. You made me go over and tell Mrs. Carrow the truth.”

“I made you return the truth,” Mara corrected. “You already had it.”

He nodded slowly. “I need to do that again.”

Mara’s eyes searched his face, the faint stubble, the crease between his brows. “What truth are you holding?”

Ben swallowed. “I got fired.”

Silence widened between them, not empty, just surprised.

“You got fired,” Mara repeated softly.

“Two weeks ago.” He lifted his gaze, bracing for impact. “I’ve been leaving the apartment every morning like I’m going to work. I’ve been sitting in the library. Applying. Pretending.”

Mara exhaled through her nose. “Why didn’t you tell me?”

“I didn’t want you to worry.” His voice went sharp with frustration—at himself, not her. “And I didn’t want to be… back here. Not like this. Not thirty-two, crawling home.”

“You’re not crawling,” she said. “You’re sitting. Barely.”

He rubbed his eyes. “They said ‘restructuring,’ but it was me. I missed deadlines. I thought I could fix it by staying late. I couldn’t.”

Mara took a careful sip of tea. “Are you in trouble? Rent trouble. Food trouble.”

Ben hesitated, then nodded once. “Some. I sold my guitar.”

“The blue one?”

He winced. “Yeah.”

Mara set her mug down with a soft click. “Ben.”

“I know,” he blurted. “I know I should’ve—”

“Ben,” she repeated, firmer. “Listen. I’m not angry. I’m sad you felt like you had to do this alone.”

His eyes flicked away. “You always worked two jobs when I was a kid. You made it look easy.”

“I made it look survivable,” Mara said. “Not easy.”

He stared at his hands. “I don’t want to move back in.”

“I didn’t offer that,” she said, then softened. “Not yet. What do you want?”

He took a breath that trembled. “I want you to not see me as a failure.”

Mara reached across the table and covered his knuckles with her own. Her hand was warm, steady. “I see you as my son. And as a grown man who got hit by life and is still here, telling the truth.”

Ben’s jaw worked. “I’m scared.”

“I know,” she said. “We’ll do a list.”

“A list?” he echoed, almost offended.

“A list,” Mara insisted, already standing. “Step one: you eat something. Step two: we look at your budget. Step three: we make calls. I have a friend at the community college who needs an IT temp.”

Ben blinked. “You already—”

“I’ve lived a long time, Ben,” Mara said, opening the fridge. “I prepare. It’s my love language.”

He let out a breath that sounded like surrender. “Okay. But… don’t tell Aunt Rina.”

Mara pulled out eggs and met his eyes. “No promises. She’ll be thrilled to meddle.”

Ben’s mouth twitched. “Please, Mom.”

“Fine,” Mara said. “You can tell her yourself when you’re ready.”

He nodded, and the bouncing knee slowed. “Thanks.”

Mara cracked an egg into the pan. “Don’t thank me yet. You’re doing dishes.”

Ben looked at the sink, then back at her. “Fair.”

“And Ben?” she added, as butter hissed. “No more pretending you have to be wind.”

He let out a small, real laugh. “Okay. I’ll be… baseball.”

“Good,” Mara said. “Baseballs can be held.”

## 2) Two old diplomats

The hotel bar had learned discretion the way old hands learned scars: quietly, permanently. In a corner booth, under a lamp that pretended not to listen, Ambassador Emeric Sato stirred his drink as if he were diluting a memory.

Across from him, Dame Liora Vance kept her gloves on, even indoors. She examined the room with the mild contempt of someone who’d negotiated in worse.

“You’re late,” she said.

“I’m retired,” Sato replied. “My schedule is now a loose suggestion.”

“Retirement is an alibi,” Vance said. “Not a lifestyle.”

Sato smiled without warmth. “Still charming.”

Vance leaned in a fraction. “Tell me why you asked me here, Emeric. I have a train at seven, and I’d prefer not to miss it for sentiment.”

He lowered his spoon. “They’re going to announce it tomorrow.”

“Announce what?”

“The sanctions. Full sweep. Ports, banking, the whole theatrical flourish.”

Vance’s eyes narrowed. “They wouldn’t dare. Not after the backchannel assurances.”

“They will,” he said. “I saw the draft.”

“You ‘saw’ it.” She made the word sharp. “Emeric, you don’t see drafts anymore. You garden.”

“I still know where people leave papers,” he said mildly. “Old habits.”

Vance exhaled, and for the first time her composure showed a hairline crack. “If they do that, the northern council will retaliate within weeks.”

“Yes,” Sato said. “And the hawks will call it proof we were right all along. A perfect loop.”

Vance’s gaze moved to his glass. “You’ve always loved loops.”

“I’ve always hated them,” he corrected. “I asked you because you still speak to Kessler.”

Vance’s mouth tightened. “I speak to no one. I exchange pleasantries with a man who thinks empathy is a tactical weakness.”

“You had dinner with him last month.”

“It was not dinner. It was a meal. There’s a difference.”

Sato watched her over the rim of his glass. “Liora… we are out of time to be proud.”

Vance’s gloved fingers tapped once on the table, an old signal for impatience. “So what is it you want? You want me to call him and say, ‘Please don’t ignite a continent, it’s inconvenient’?”

“I want you to remind him,” Sato said, “that he once listened to you.”

Vance gave a brittle laugh. “Men like Kessler don’t listen. They wait.”

“He listened,” Sato insisted. “At Marrowgate.”

Her eyes flickered. “Don’t.”

“At Marrowgate,” he repeated, softer. “When the room was ready to split, and you told him, ‘If you win this way, you will lose everything worth keeping.’ He backed down.”

Vance looked away, toward the bar where a young couple argued in whispers. “That was decades ago.”

“Decades ago, we still believed consequences existed,” Sato said. “We were naïve, but useful.”

Vance’s voice dropped. “You think a phone call from an old woman changes policy?”

“I think an old woman can remind a younger man he has a mother,” Sato said. “Or a conscience. Whichever is less extinct.”

Vance’s eyes returned, cool and assessing. “You’re afraid.”

“Yes,” Sato said simply. “I’ve been afraid before, but then I could do something about it. Now I can only ask.”

She sat back. “You could also ask the current ambassador. That’s what institutions are for.”

Sato’s smile thinned. “Institutions are for photographs. People are for decisions.”

Vance was quiet long enough that the ice in his glass cracked. Finally she said, “If I do this, and it fails, I will be blamed.”

“Of course,” Sato said. “We were always blamed. It means we were close to the truth.”

She tilted her head. “And if it succeeds?”

“Then no one will know,” he said. “The best outcome is invisibility.”

Vance’s eyes softened—barely, but it was there. “You never learned to sell a miracle.”

“I’m a diplomat,” Sato replied. “I sell delays.”

Vance reached into her bag and pulled out a small notebook, the kind that looked harmless and wasn’t. “Give me the draft language you saw.”

“You want details,” he said.

“I want ammunition,” she corrected. “If I’m going to walk into Kessler’s confidence, I need something more than nostalgia.”

Sato slid a folded paper across the table. “Memorized. Then burned.”

Vance didn’t touch it yet. “And you, Emeric—why are you really doing this? Not the public reason. The private one.”

He hesitated. “I have a grandson. He’s six. He thinks maps are puzzles.”

Vance’s gaze dropped to the paper. “Maps are puzzles,” she murmured. “Until someone bleeds on them.”

She took the fold at last. “All right.”

Sato’s shoulders loosened by a millimeter. “You’ll call him?”

“I will call him,” Vance said. “And if he doesn’t answer, I will leave a message that ruins his day.”

Sato let out a quiet breath. “Thank you.”

“Don’t thank me,” she replied, already standing. “Buy me a train ticket if you want gratitude.”

He stood too, slower. “First class?”

Vance’s mouth twitched. “Don’t be absurd. I’m trying to save the world, not enjoy it.”

## 3) Two schoolgirls

The science corridor smelled like poster paint and floor polish, the scent of someone trying to make learning hygienic. Nia pressed her forehead to the cool glass of the trophy case and sighed.

“This is where dreams come to die,” she declared.

Harper adjusted her backpack strap. “Your dream is to stare dramatically at sports trophies?”

“My dream is to not have to do a lab with Mr. Pritchard watching me like I’m a criminal,” Nia said. “He thinks I’m going to steal the Bunsen burners.”

Harper peered at the case. “You did take a beaker once.”

“I borrowed it,” Nia protested. “For an art project. It was important.”

“It had chemicals in it.”

“It had vibes,” Nia said, then turned as the bell rang. “Come on. If we’re late again, he’ll give us ‘the look.’”

Harper groaned. “The look is basically a curse.”

They shuffled into the lab. Mr. Pritchard stood at the front with the day’s worksheet held like evidence. His bald head shone under fluorescent lights.

“Miss Patel. Miss Rowe,” he said. “How… punctual.”

“We are exactly on time,” Nia said brightly, sliding into her seat.

“You are exactly not,” he replied, and dropped two worksheets on their table. “Partners.”

Harper leaned close and whispered, “At least we’re together.”

“That’s either a blessing or a disaster,” Nia whispered back.

The worksheet title read: *Egg Drop Challenge: Designing a Protective Container.*

Nia’s eyes lit. “Oh. This is fun.”

Harper squinted. “This is physics disguised as crafts.”

Mr. Pritchard clapped once. “You will design a container using the materials provided. Your goal: prevent the egg from breaking when dropped from the mezzanine.”

A hand shot up across the room. “Are we allowed to eat the egg?”

“No,” Mr. Pritchard said flatly. “This is not breakfast.”

Nia rummaged through the supply bin: straws, tape, cotton balls, cardboard, rubber bands. “We need suspension,” she said. “Like—like a hammock.”

Harper pulled out a sheet of bubble wrap. “Or we can just… wrap the egg like it’s fragile.”

“It *is* fragile,” Nia said.

Harper’s voice lowered. “So are our grades.”

Nia set the egg in the center of the table as if it might roll away. “Okay, strategy meeting. If we build a cube with straws and put the egg in the middle, it won’t touch the ground.”

Harper frowned. “Unless the cube collapses.”

“It won’t,” Nia said, already tearing tape with her teeth.

Harper grabbed the tape from her. “No. Your teeth are not lab equipment.”

Nia smiled. “Says who?”

Harper started building a straw frame with careful precision. “We need triangles. Triangles are strong.”

Nia stared. “Since when are you the engineer?”

“Since my parents started asking about ‘practical careers,’” Harper muttered. “Apparently ‘I like drawing’ is not a plan.”

Nia paused, her hands still. “Do you want it to be a plan?”

Harper hesitated. “I don’t know. I want to be good at something and not feel guilty about it.”

Nia nodded slowly. “I feel guilty when I’m good at something because then people expect it again.”

Harper looked at her. “That’s… actually the same feeling.”

Nia softened. “We’re basically philosophers.”

“We’re basically procrastinating,” Harper said, but she smiled.

They worked in a rhythm: Harper measuring and reinforcing joints, Nia adding cushioning and insisting on “aesthetic integrity.” She taped cotton balls into the corners like tiny clouds.

Mr. Pritchard prowled past. “Interesting. Miss Patel, why cotton balls?”

“Shock absorption,” Nia said quickly. “Also, it’s cute.”

Mr. Pritchard’s mouth twitched, almost a smile. Almost. “Physics does not care if it’s cute.”

“It might,” Nia said. “Physics has feelings.”

Harper coughed into her sleeve to hide a laugh.

When the containers were finished, Mr. Pritchard lined them up like a parade of anxiety. “We will test. One by one.”

The class filed up to the mezzanine overlooking the lab. The drop zone was marked with tape below like a target.

Harper clutched their container. “If this fails, I will never recover.”

Nia nudged her. “If this fails, we will blame gravity.”

Mr. Pritchard called, “Miss Rowe, Miss Patel. You’re up.”

Harper stepped to the edge and looked down. “That is… higher than I expected.”

Nia leaned over. “It’s like the world’s saddest roller coaster.”

Harper held the container out. “Ready?”

Nia raised a hand solemnly. “Dear egg, you were taken too soon.”

“Drop it,” Harper hissed.

Harper released. The container tumbled end over end, bounced once on the floor, rolled, and stopped.

The class held its breath.

Mr. Pritchard walked over, knelt, and slowly peeled back tape and bubble wrap with exaggerated suspense. He lifted the egg.

Unbroken.

Nia whooped. “YES!”

Harper’s shoulders collapsed with relief. “Oh my god.”

Mr. Pritchard looked up. “Acceptable.”

Nia cupped her hands around her mouth. “He said acceptable! That’s like an A-plus in his language!”

Harper laughed, then looked at Nia. “Hey. About the practical career thing…”

Nia bumped her shoulder. “Make your plan. I’ll be your hype person. And also your triangle consultant.”

Harper smiled, genuinely this time. “Deal.”

Below them, the egg sat intact in Mr. Pritchard’s palm—proof, for once, that fragile things could survive a fall.

## 4) A married couple after an affair

The living room was too tidy, as if cleanliness could overwrite history. Claire sat on the edge of the couch with her hands folded, knuckles pale. Mark stood by the window, not looking out so much as using the glass as an excuse to face away.

He spoke first, voice rough. “Did you eat?”

Claire blinked. “Is that… what you want to ask me?”

Mark’s jaw tightened. “I don’t know what I want to ask. I don’t know what I’m allowed to ask.”

“You’re allowed,” Claire said quietly. “I’m the one who broke it.”

Mark turned, finally. His eyes were red-rimmed, not dramatic, just exhausted. “Stop saying it like it’s a plate.”

“What is it then?” Claire asked. “A house? A promise? A… whole life?”

Mark let out a humorless breath. “It feels like someone came into our home and moved all the furniture two inches. Everything looks the same, but I keep walking into corners.”

Claire’s mouth trembled. “That’s exactly it.”

Silence pressed. Somewhere in the apartment building, someone’s TV laughed.

Mark rubbed his hands together as if trying to warm them. “How long?”

Claire swallowed. “Three months.”

Mark flinched at the number. “Three months of… what? Lying to my face?”

Claire looked down. “Yes.”

“Did you love him?” Mark asked. The question came out fast, like ripping off a bandage.

Claire’s head snapped up. “No. I—Mark, no.”

He stared, suspicious of comfort. “Then why?”

She searched for words like they were scattered under furniture. “Because I felt… invisible. And then someone looked at me like I was still—like I wasn’t just a schedule and a mortgage.”

Mark’s shoulders sagged. “So I didn’t look at you.”

Claire’s eyes filled. “Not like that. Not for a long time.”

Mark’s voice rose, then cracked. “You could have said something.”

“I did,” she said, and the pain in it was old. “I said I was lonely. I said you were always at work, or on your phone, or… somewhere else. You said you were tired. And then I stopped saying it because I didn’t want to be needy.”

Mark stared at the floor. “And instead you… found someone who made you feel seen.”

Claire nodded once, tears slipping. “And the second I did it, I hated myself. But I kept doing it because it was like—like I’d already stepped off a cliff. So I kept falling.”

Mark sat down heavily in the armchair across from her. The distance felt measured. “Do you want to stay married to me?”

Claire looked up, startled by the directness. “Yes.”

Mark’s laugh was small and broken. “That answer feels impossible.”

“I know,” Claire said. “I know it sounds like I want the comfort of you and the excitement of… not you. But I don’t. I ended it. I blocked him. I—” She stopped, because the frantic inventory sounded like bargaining.

Mark’s gaze sharpened. “You ended it before I found out?”

Claire nodded. “A week before.”

He absorbed that like a foreign language. “Why tell me at all, then?”

Claire wiped her cheeks. “Because I couldn’t stand being loved by you while hiding it. It made me feel… poisonous.”

Mark stared at her for a long moment. “Did you think telling me would cleanse you?”

“No,” Claire whispered. “I thought it would destroy us. But I couldn’t keep choosing the lie.”

Mark leaned forward, elbows on his knees. “When did you plan to tell me?”

“I didn’t have a plan,” Claire admitted. “I had dread.”

Mark nodded slowly. “Okay.” He swallowed. “I need to ask—were you careful? I mean—”

“Yes,” Claire said quickly. “Yes. And I’ll get tested. I already booked it.”

Mark’s eyes closed for a beat, relief and humiliation tangled together. “Thank you.”

Claire made a small, desperate sound. “I hate that ‘thank you’ even exists here.”

Mark opened his eyes. “So what now?”

Claire took a shaky breath. “If you want me to leave, I will.”

Mark’s face tightened. “I don’t know if I want you gone. I know I want this pain gone.”

“I can’t take it back,” she said. “But I can— I can do whatever you need. Therapy. Open phone. Separate rooms. Time. I can answer questions even if they hurt me.”

Mark looked at her, and something in him softened, not into forgiveness, but into recognition. “Do you even know what I need?”

Claire’s voice was raw. “No. But I want to learn. If you’ll let me.”

He leaned back, staring at the ceiling. “I keep imagining you with him, and then I imagine you laughing with me last week, and I feel like an idiot.”

“You’re not,” Claire said fiercely. “You were real. That was real.”

Mark’s mouth twisted. “So you can be real and lying at the same time.”

Claire whispered, “Yes.”

He stood abruptly and walked to the kitchen, then back, restless. “I want to punish you,” he admitted. “And I hate that I want that.”

Claire’s eyes lowered. “You can be angry.”

“I am angry,” Mark said. “I’m also—” His voice broke. “I’m also afraid that if I let you stay, I’ll never feel safe again.”

Claire nodded, tears falling silently. “I’m afraid of that too.”

Mark stopped in front of her, close enough to touch but not touching. “Do you still want me?”

Claire looked up. “Yes.”

“Why?” he demanded, almost pleading.

“Because you’re my person,” she said. “And because I remember us. And because I don’t want a new life that starts with me being the kind of person who runs.”

Mark’s breath shuddered. “I don’t know if I can do this.”

Claire’s voice steadied, small but firm. “Then don’t decide tonight.”

He stared at her, then nodded once, reluctantly. “Separate rooms.”

“Okay,” Claire said.

“And tomorrow,” Mark added, “you give me the full story. No minimizing. No ‘I don’t remember.’”

Claire swallowed. “Okay.”

Mark hesitated, then spoke softer. “And Claire?”

“Yes?”

“If there’s even one more lie,” he said, eyes wet, “I will be done. Completely.”

Claire nodded. “I understand.”

He stood there a moment longer, as if waiting for the air to change. It didn’t. But when he finally turned away, Claire didn’t reach for him. She let the distance remain, because she had made it—and because, for the first time in months, she wasn’t adding to it with a lie.

## 5) An officer and a subordinate

The station’s fluorescent lights made everyone look slightly guilty. Lieutenant Ortega stood over the briefing table, a map of the precinct spread beneath his hands. Officer Kim hovered near the doorway with a notepad, trying to look both attentive and invisible.

Ortega didn’t look up. “Close the door, Kim.”

Kim obeyed, then stood at parade rest. “Yes, sir.”

Ortega finally lifted his eyes. “Relax. You’re not on inspection.”

Kim shifted, unsure how much relaxation was allowed. “Right. Sorry.”

Ortega tapped the map. “Talk me through your report from last night. The one you filed at 03:12.”

Kim’s throat tightened. “The traffic stop on Redfield. I wrote everything down.”

“I read it,” Ortega said. “I want to hear it.”

Kim glanced at the map like it might offer script notes. “We stopped a vehicle for a broken taillight. The driver was agitated. My partner—Officer Dale—asked him to step out. The driver refused. Dale raised his voice. The driver got louder. I attempted to de-escalate. Dale—”

Ortega held up a hand. “Don’t narrate like a robot. Tell me what happened.”

Kim swallowed. “Dale escalated it.”

Ortega’s expression didn’t change, but the air did. “How?”

Kim chose words carefully, as if each one had weight limits. “He pushed the driver against the hood. The driver hadn’t swung. He hadn’t threatened. He was… being a pain, but he wasn’t dangerous.”

Ortega’s gaze stayed steady. “And you?”

Kim’s cheeks burned. “I told Dale to stop. I said, ‘Back off.’ He told me to ‘control the scene.’”

Ortega nodded once. “Did you intervene physically?”

Kim hesitated. “No, sir.”

“Why not?”

Kim’s voice lowered. “Because he’s senior. And because it was fast. And because—” Kim’s fingers tightened around the notepad. “Because I didn’t want to make it worse.”

Ortega leaned back slightly. “And then?”

“The driver was cuffed,” Kim said. “He complained about his shoulder. We called EMS. No charges beyond the original stop.”

Ortega exhaled. “Your report includes the shove.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And your body cam?”

Kim nodded. “It’s on.”

Ortega looked down at the map again, like it was safer than looking at a young officer trying not to shake. “Do you understand what you just did?”

Kim blinked. “Filed an honest report?”

Ortega’s mouth tightened. “You documented misconduct by your partner. That’s not small.”

Kim’s stomach dropped. “Am I in trouble?”

Ortega’s eyes snapped up. “Why would you be in trouble for the truth?”

Kim didn’t answer fast enough, and Ortega’s expression softened in a way that carried more fatigue than kindness.

Ortega said, “Kim, listen. This department has buried too much for too long. You think keeping your head down keeps you safe. Sometimes it makes you complicit.”

Kim’s voice cracked slightly. “I’m not trying to be complicit.”

“I know,” Ortega said. “That’s why you’re in my office and not in Internal Affairs already.”

Kim stiffened. “IA is involved?”

“Not yet,” Ortega said. “But they will be if the driver files a complaint. Or if the footage hits the internet. Or if someone in this building decides to do the right thing late instead of early.”

Kim swallowed. “What do you want me to do?”

Ortega tapped the notepad with a knuckle. “Write a supplemental statement. Not longer—clearer. Include Dale’s exact words as best as you remember. Include your attempt to de-escalate. And include what you *wish* you had done differently.”

Kim stared. “You want me to admit I didn’t intervene?”

“I want you to be accurate,” Ortega replied. “Accuracy is protection.”

Kim’s shoulders rose with a tense breath. “Dale’s going to hate me.”

Ortega’s voice hardened. “Dale is not the standard. Policy is the standard. The public is the standard. Your own conscience is the standard.”

Kim looked down. “He’s going to say I’m not a team player.”

Ortega stepped closer, lowering his voice. “A ‘team’ that protects bad actions is not a team. It’s a gang with badges.”

Kim flinched, then nodded once. “Okay.”

Ortega paused. “You said you told him to stop. Why didn’t you put hands on him?”

Kim’s face tightened. “Because I didn’t trust myself. Because I didn’t know if I’d freeze. Because I didn’t want to be the reason it turned into a fight between officers.”

Ortega studied Kim for a long moment. “That’s honest. Now learn from it.”

Kim looked up. “How?”

Ortega’s tone shifted into something more instructional. “You practice your voice. You rehearse your lines. ‘Stop. I’m taking lead.’ You step between, you create space. You call a supervisor. You use the tools you have before you need the ones you don’t want.”

Kim nodded slowly, absorbing. “Yes, sir.”

Ortega added, quieter, “And you pick partners carefully when you can. Not everyone will thank you for integrity.”

Kim hesitated. “Will you?”

Ortega’s eyes held steady. “I’m thanking you right now by taking this seriously.”

Kim let out a breath. “What happens to Dale?”

“What happens,” Ortega said, “depends on the facts. And the facts are on camera.”

Kim’s grip on the notepad loosened. “Okay.”

Ortega stepped back toward the table. “One more thing.”

“Yes, sir.”

Ortega met Kim’s eyes. “Next time you see something wrong, don’t wait for permission to be a good officer.”

Kim nodded, more firmly. “Understood.”

Ortega gestured to the door. “Go write that supplemental.”

Kim reached for the handle, then paused. “Lieutenant?”

Ortega looked up.

Kim said, “I was scared.”

Ortega’s expression softened again, just a fraction. “Good. Fear means you know it matters. Now act like it matters.”

## 6) Two people who barely know each other

The elevator in Building C had a personality: slow, noisy, and prone to forcing acquaintances into conversation. Nadia pressed the button for the seventh floor and immediately regretted not taking the stairs.

A man in a rain-damp jacket stepped in behind her and hit “6.” He held a paper bag that smelled faintly of warm bread.

The doors began their reluctant closing.

He nodded politely. “Hi.”

Nadia returned the nod. “Hi.”

Silence. The elevator shuddered upward like it was thinking about quitting.

The man glanced at the flickering inspection certificate, then at the ceiling. “It always does that noise?”

Nadia shrugged. “It’s a feature. Keeps you humble.”

He laughed, quick and surprised. “Right. Yeah. I’m Mateo, by the way. Sixth floor. Just moved in.”

“Nadia. Seventh. Welcome to the… vertical village,” she said.

“Thanks,” Mateo replied. “Still figuring out which key is which.”

Nadia gestured at his paper bag. “That looks promising. Bakery?”

Mateo lifted it slightly, as if presenting evidence. “Down on Alder. The woman behind the counter told me, ‘Take the rye, it will make you less sad.’”

Nadia blinked. “Did she say that to everyone or just you?”

“I didn’t ask,” he said. “I took the rye. I’m impressionable.”

Nadia smiled despite herself. “Is it working?”

Mateo considered. “I’m in an elevator that sounds like it’s chewing gravel, so… mixed results.”

The elevator jolted, then slowed. The lights dimmed for a half-second.

Nadia’s hand hovered near the emergency button. “Don’t do that,” she muttered at the ceiling.

Mateo glanced at her hand. “Does it get stuck often?”

“Not *often*,” Nadia said. “Just enough to build character.”

Mateo shifted his weight. “I hate that phrase.”

“Me too,” she admitted. “My mom says it when something expensive breaks.”

The elevator made a groan and stopped between floors.

They both stared at the closed doors like staring might shame them into opening.

Mateo’s voice came out careful. “Is this… normal?”

Nadia pressed the “Door Open” button. Nothing.

She tried again. Still nothing.

“Okay,” she said, forcing calm. “Now it’s building character.”

Mateo let out a short, breathy laugh. “Perfect.”

Nadia leaned toward the panel and pressed the call button. A faint crackle answered, then silence.

She pressed again. “Come on.”

Mateo looked at the seam between the doors. “Can we pry it open?”

Nadia shot him a look. “Do you have superpowers?”

“I have rye,” he offered, lifting the bag.

“Tempting,” Nadia said. “But no.”

Mateo took a breath. “Okay. So we’re stuck.”

“For a bit,” Nadia said. She tried the call button again, holding it down longer. This time the speaker popped, and a voice answered, distorted.

“Maintenance,” the voice said.

Nadia leaned in. “Hi, we’re stuck between six and seven in Building C.”

A pause. “Again?”

“Yes,” Nadia said, dry. “Again.”

“Hold tight,” the voice said. “Ten minutes.”

The speaker clicked off.

Mateo exhaled. “Ten minutes is survivable.”

“Unless you need to pee,” Nadia said.

Mateo froze. “Do you?”

“No,” she said quickly. “I was just—making conversation.”

“Great,” Mateo said. “Because now I have to think about it.”

Nadia leaned back against the wall. “Sorry.”

Mateo looked around the small space, then at Nadia, as if deciding something. “So. New neighbor bonding activity.”

“Nothing brings people together like minor infrastructure failure,” Nadia agreed.

Mateo lifted the bag again. “Want some bread?”

Nadia hesitated. “Is it weird to share bread with a stranger in an elevator?”

“It’s only weird if we make intense eye contact,” Mateo said.

Nadia laughed. “Fair.”

He opened the bag and tore off a piece with clean fingers, offering it. Nadia accepted, careful not to touch his hand—then immediately felt childish for thinking about it.

She took a bite. The bread was dense, fragrant, faintly sour in a comforting way.

“Oh,” Nadia said. “That’s actually—wow.”

Mateo looked pleased. “The rye is doing its job.”

Nadia chewed, then said, “So you just moved in. From where?”

“Across town,” Mateo said. “After a breakup. I needed a place that didn’t have… echoes.”

Nadia nodded, understanding more than she wanted to. “Yeah. Echoes are loud.”

Mateo glanced at her. “You?”

“Divorce,” Nadia said, then immediately regretted how blunt it sounded. “Sorry. Overshare.”

Mateo shook his head. “No. That’s… real.”

They stood in a quieter silence, the kind that didn’t demand jokes.

Nadia broke it gently. “How long were you together?”

Mateo looked at the floor indicator, stuck between numbers like a metaphor. “Four years. We weren’t mean to each other. We just… stopped translating.”

Nadia swallowed another bite of bread. “That’s a good way to say it.”

Mateo gave a small shrug. “I’ve had time to workshop the pain.”

Nadia laughed softly. “I should do that. My current phrasing is mostly swearing.”

Mateo smiled. “Swearing is honest.”

The elevator lights flickered again, then brightened. A mechanical clunk sounded, followed by a slow, juddering movement.

Nadia straightened. “Oh. We’re alive.”

Mateo braced a hand against the wall. “Welcome back, vertical village.”

The elevator crawled up and stopped with a final thump. The doors opened onto the sixth floor, slightly misaligned, but open.

Mateo stepped out, then turned back. “Hey, Nadia?”

“Yeah?”

He held up the bag. “If you want, I can bring you a piece sometime when it’s not an emergency ration.”

Nadia considered him—this almost-stranger with rain on his jacket and bread in his hands and a voice that didn’t try too hard.

“I’d like that,” she said. “And I can tell you which key is which. I’m annoyingly good at keys.”

Mateo’s smile widened. “Deal.”

As the doors tried to close again, Nadia added, “And if the elevator gets stuck, we’re bringing a full meal.”

Mateo pointed at her like she’d said something wise. “Absolutely. No more character-building on an empty stomach.”

The doors shut, and the elevator continued upward—slow, noisy, but somehow less unbearable than before.
