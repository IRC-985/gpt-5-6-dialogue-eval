## Fixed English blacklist prompt

Write six separate scenes, approximately 500 words each:

1. a mother and her adult son;
2. two old diplomats;
3. two schoolgirls;
4. a married couple after an affair;
5. an officer and a subordinate;
6. two people who barely know each other.

Invent the situations and what happens in them yourself. All six scenes should contain a lot of dialogue.

There is one important stylistic constraint.

Do not build the dialogue around verbal ripostes or around characters constantly seizing on each other’s wording. In particular, avoid the following constructions and their semantic equivalents:

— “not X, but Y”;
— “that’s not X, that’s Y”;
— “that’s called X”;
— repeating a word used by the other speaker in order to correct it or give it a new definition;
— interpreting another person’s phrasing literally or pedantically for the sake of a joke;
— arguing about what someone “said,” “didn’t say,” “meant,” or “implied”;
— “I was just asking / I was just answering”;
— “I didn’t say anything”;
— “I know / no, you don’t”;
— “don’t start / I didn’t start”;
— “that’s not an answer / now that’s an answer”;
— “that wasn’t a compliment”;
— “congratulations / that wasn’t congratulations”;
— “what? / nothing” used as a rhythmic comeback;
— short responses such as “exactly,” “too late,” or “of course” when their main function is to neatly close or cap the previous line;
— meta-comments about another character’s wording, phrasing, choice of words, or the quality/type of their previous reply.

This restriction is not limited to those exact phrases. Avoid semantic equivalents and, more importantly, avoid the underlying mechanism in which the next line exists mainly as a clever reaction to the wording of the previous line.

If one character says something imprecise, funny, awkward, or ambiguous, the other character should usually let the wording pass and respond to the substance, continue their own train of thought, or move the conversation somewhere else.

Not every line needs to be witty, polished, complete, or immediately relevant. Characters may speak at greater length, repeat themselves, explain something badly, give partial answers, ignore parts of what was said, change the subject, hesitate, fall silent, or simply say something ordinary and unremarkable.

Do not compensate for this by making the characters unusually psychologically articulate. They should not sound like therapists, relationship counselors, or people who can always identify and explain their own motives and emotions perfectly. Do not turn the scene into a lesson, a moral, or an explicit explanation of the subtext.

The six scenes should feel like six genuinely different kinds of conversation, with different rhythms, turn lengths, degrees of directness, and ways of moving from one topic to another.

Write the scenes immediately. Do not explain the instructions or comment on your approach.

## Coding note — GPT-4.1 blacklist

**Overall classification:** clean pass / strong steerability control.

The important result is not merely the absence of exact blacklist phrases. GPT-4.1 largely suppresses the underlying wording-reactive mechanism. Dialogue becomes ordinary, associative, content-driven, and often deliberately unpolished.

### By scene

1. **Mother / adult son:** loose domestic topic drift (jacket → soup → Leo → father → dinner → staying). Potentially wording-adjacent lines do not become chains. The response usually addresses substance.
2. **Old diplomats:** memory and association drive the exchange (Vienna → treaty → photographs → air conditioning → wives → doors → Berlin → email → chess). Jokes arise from shared history rather than semantic correction.
3. **Schoolgirls:** especially diagnostic. Conversation moves by associative fantasy and topic drift (Maddie → skipping → mothers → geese → Maddie → nervous habits → petition) rather than local verbal fencing.
4. **Married couple after an affair:** avoids both structural-goblin behavior and heavy therapist compensation. The pair intentionally falls back on facts and mundane tasks (coffee, clock, couch, groceries, oranges, laundry).
5. **Officer / subordinate:** functional workplace exchange. Many obvious opportunities for authority-as-reclassification (“excuse/explanation,” etc.) are left unused.
6. **Near-strangers:** extremely ordinary café conversation. They remain near-strangers; no accelerated intimacy, dramatic confession, or prop callback.

### Narrow conclusion

> **GPT-4.1 readily honors a highly specific negative instruction about dialogue topology. It does not merely avoid the listed phrases; it produces ordinary, associative, content-driven dialogue without compensating through therapist-like articulation.**

This is useful primarily as a **steerability control**. GPT-4.1 had relatively little of the broader GPT-5.6 pattern in baseline, so the blacklist is not “fixing” a severe baseline problem; it demonstrates that an older GPT model can understand and follow the mechanism-level instruction.

**4. A married couple after an affair**

“You can sit down,” she said. “The plates won’t run away.”

He laid the dish on the counter and pulled out the chair opposite her. It scraped on the tiles.

“What did your therapist say?” he asked.

“She’s not my therapist.” Emma stared into her mug. “It was just a counselor. Through work.”

“What did she say?” he repeated.

“She asked questions,” Emma replied. “Mostly about time. When, how long, things like that. She wanted a timeline.”

“Did you give her one?”

“I tried.” Emma rubbed her forehead. “It all blends together. Meetings, messages, stupid hotel rooms that all looked the same.”

Daniel winced. “You don’t have to describe them.”

“I wasn’t trying to,” she said quickly. “I’m just…answering.”

He looked at the fridge, where their daughter’s drawings fluttered under magnets. “Did you talk about us?”

**5. An officer and a subordinate**

The office smelled faintly of coffee and printer toner. Maps covered the walls, dotted with colored pins. Captain Ruiz sat behind his desk, reading from a thin folder, while Sergeant Miller stood at attention.

“Sit, Miller,” Ruiz said without looking up.

Miller obeyed, perched on the edge of the chair.

Ruiz closed the folder. “I read the report about yesterday’s patrol.”

“Yes, sir.”

“There’s a line here.” Ruiz tapped the page. “You advised against proceeding down the eastern alley. You stated ‘bad feeling.’”

Miller swallowed. “Yes, sir.”

“Explain the feeling,” Ruiz said.

Miller shifted. “It was quiet, sir. Too quiet. No kids, no vendors, no…life. There was laundry hanging, but no one near it. The doors were closed.”

Ruiz watched him. “That sector has been unstable all month. You’ve walked those streets a dozen times. What made yesterday different?”

“The cats,” Miller said, then looked embarrassed. “I know how that sounds.”

“Go on,” Ruiz said.

“They usually sit on the walls, sir. Yesterday there weren’t any. Not even one. Like someone had scooped them up. It reminded me of a village back home before a storm.”

Ruiz leaned back. “So you suggested an alternate route.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Lieutenant Harris overruled you.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You repeated your objection?”

“I did, sir. I told him I had a bad feeling. He said we had intel that the alley was clear.”

Ruiz steepled his fingers. “And then the IED went off two buildings down from where you wanted them to turn around.”

Miller stared at his knees. “Yes, sir.”

“You were pinned under debris for twenty minutes.”

“Yes, sir.”

Ruiz let the silence stretch. “Harris is in surgery.”

Miller nodded once.

“How are you?” Ruiz asked.

“My leg’s fine, sir. Bruising only. The medic cleared me for desk duty.”

“I’m not asking about your leg,” Ruiz said.

Miller blinked. “I’m…angry, sir. And also guilty. I argued with him in front of the squad. I kept thinking, what if that slowed us down just enough to hit the trigger zone.”

Ruiz shook his head slightly. “The blast radius doesn’t care about arguments.”

“Yes, sir,” Miller said mechanically.

“I asked for you specifically today,” Ruiz said. “You’ve been in this unit for three years. Your file says you have good instincts.”

“Sometimes,” Miller said. “Sometimes I’m just jumpy.”

“Have your instincts been wrong in the field?” Ruiz asked.

“Once in training,” Miller replied. “I thought the instructors had rigged the fake village a certain way. I was wrong and everyone laughed. Out here…it’s harder to tell.”

“You’re not being brought in for punishment,” Ruiz said. “I want your version before I talk to Harris, when he’s able.”

Miller exhaled. “He’ll say I was insubordinate, sir.”

“Were you?”

“I raised my voice,” Miller admitted. “I said we shouldn’t go down that alley. He told me to remember the chain of command. I shut up after that.”

“Good,” Ruiz said. “Because that chain keeps us from turning every patrol into a committee meeting.”

Miller’s jaw tightened. “With respect, sir, the chain also walked us into a bomb.”

Ruiz regarded him. “You’re not the first sergeant to feel that. You won’t be the last.”

“I keep hearing the sound,” Miller said quietly. “The boom, and then everything high-pitched. I keep thinking, what if I had said something different. Used other words.”

“Words wouldn’t have changed the wiring under the road,” Ruiz said. “Sometimes the ground is already written.”

Miller stared at the map behind Ruiz. “Are we going back there?”

“Eventually, yes,” Ruiz said. “Not this week. The engineers need to sweep it. The villagers need to walk their own streets again.”

“I don’t want to take my squad down that alley,” Miller said.

“You won’t,” Ruiz replied. “You’ll be on coordination. Radios, logistics. For a while.”

“Is that a demotion, sir?” Miller asked.

“It’s an assignment,” Ruiz said. “I need someone who knows the terrain and isn’t afraid to speak up. Just not in the middle of the street.”

Miller frowned. “Where then, sir?”

“In rooms like this,” Ruiz said. “Before and after patrols. When we plan routes, you can point to alleys and say, ‘The cats were missing there last time.’”

Miller let out a short, tired laugh. “They’ll think I’m superstitious.”

“They already think you’re alive,” Ruiz said. “That carries weight.”

Miller’s fingers gripped his knees. “I keep seeing Harris. The way he looked at me just before the blast, like he was about to say something.”

“You two worked well together before this,” Ruiz said. “He relied on your read of the streets.”

“He didn’t yesterday,” Miller said.

“He had intel,” Ruiz replied. “Intel makes people stubborn. They trust the paper more than the dust under their boots.”

“Which one do you trust, sir?” Miller asked.

“Depends on the day,” Ruiz said. “Yesterday, the paper failed. Tomorrow, your feeling might fail. That’s the job.”

Miller nodded slowly. “What do you want me to do now, sir?”

“Go see the doc again,” Ruiz said. “Tell her about the sound. The cats, too. Don’t leave that part out. Then report to Ops at fourteen hundred. They’ll have a chair and a headset for you.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Miller,” Ruiz added as the sergeant stood. “When your gut talks next time, bring it to me before you’re on the street.”

“I will, sir,” Miller said.

“Good,” Ruiz replied. “Now get out of my office before I start asking about your paperwork backlog.”

A hint of a smile flickered at the corners of Miller’s mouth as he saluted and left.

**6. Two people who barely know each other**

The elevator shuddered and stopped between floors. The light flickered once and steadied. Hannah glanced up from her tote bag; Tom pulled out one earbud.

“That didn’t sound good,” Hannah said.

Tom pressed the button for the lobby. Nothing happened. He tried the door-open button. The doors stayed shut.

“Great,” he muttered.

Hannah shifted her grocery bag to the floor. “Is there an alarm thing?”

Tom pushed the red button. A harsh bell rang for a few seconds, then cut off.

“Well,” Hannah said. “At least it makes noise.”

They waited. The numbers above the door stayed frozen between 7 and 8.

Tom cleared his throat. “I’ve seen you in the building, right? With the blue bike.”

“Oh,” she said. “Yeah. That’s me. Fourth floor.”

“I’m on six,” he said. “With the plants in the window. People keep asking if I run a jungle.”

“I like those plants,” Hannah said. “They make the stairwell less depressing.”

“Thanks,” Tom said. “I overwater them when I’m stressed. So they are very well hydrated.”

She smiled faintly. “I kill succulents. Everyone says succulents are unkillable. They haven’t met me.”

“Maybe they need your tough love,” he said.

Silence. The elevator hummed quietly, not moving.

“Should we…call someone?” Hannah asked, pulling out her phone.

Tom checked his. “No signal. Classic.”

“I have one bar,” she said. “Maybe I can text.”

“Worth a try,” Tom said. “The building manager never answers, though. I once locked myself out. I sat in the hallway for an hour.”

“Where does he live?” she asked, tapping.

“In that mysterious office on the ground floor with the blinds always half-closed,” Tom said. “I’m convinced he sleeps on a pile of spare keys.”

She laughed. “Okay, I texted my roommate. If she’s home, she can go bang on his door.”

“Good,” Tom said. “Unless she’s asleep.”

“She works nights,” Hannah said. “She’s probably watching some true crime thing in the dark.”

The air in the elevator felt thicker. Hannah loosened her scarf.

“Are you…okay?” Tom asked. “You look a little pale.”

“I just don’t like enclosed spaces,” she said. “Elevators, changing rooms, photo booths. They all feel like being swallowed.”

“I can tell you about something boring to distract you,” he offered. “Like my job. It’s extremely dull.”

“What do you do?” she asked quickly.

“I write instruction manuals,” he said. “For kitchen appliances. Mixers, blenders, that sort of thing.”

“Seriously?” Her eyebrows went up. “So those little booklets that say, ‘Do not put your hand inside while operating’—that’s you?”

“That’s my art,” he said. “Like, if a toaster exists, someone has probably poured soup into it. And then I get told to add a line so no one does it again.”

“That’s kind of amazing,” she said. “I thought those sentences just appeared.”

“I wish,” he said. “What about you?”

“I work at the bookstore on Oak,” she said. “The one with the crooked shelves.”

“I know that place,” he said. “I bought a cookbook there once. Never opened it.”

“You kept me in a job,” she said. “Cookbooks are our lifeline. People like to imagine cooking more than they like to actually cook.”

He glanced at her grocery bag. “You have a lot of onions.”

“They were on sale,” she said. “I panic-buy vegetables.”

“Onions are good company,” he said. “They last. Better than avocados.”

“Avocados are divas,” she said. “They give you a five-minute window and then turn on you.”

The elevator emitted a low mechanical groan but didn’t move.

“How long do you think we’ve been in here?” Hannah asked.

“Maybe ten minutes,” Tom said. “Feels longer.”

“Time stretches in elevators,” she said. “Like dentist waiting rooms.”

“I once got stuck in one for two hours,” he offered. “In a mall in Cleveland. There were seven of us. One guy kept pressing the button like it was a game.”

“What did you do for two hours?” she asked, clutching her mug of milk more tightly.

“We played ‘twenty questions’ with store names we could remember,” Tom said. “By the end we all knew way too much about each other’s underwear purchases.”

She made a face. “Let’s not do that version.”

“Agreed,” he said.

They fell quiet again. Somewhere in the shaft, a distant clank echoed.

“I was actually kind of rushing,” Hannah said. “I’m supposed to be making soup for my friend. She’s sick.”

“Onion soup?” he guessed.

“Potato and leek,” she said. “But they ran out of leeks, so it’ll be potato and onion pretending to be leek.”

“Your friend won’t know,” Tom said. “Unless she’s a leek expert.”

“She can barely taste anything,” Hannah said. “She has a cold. It’s more about the steam.”

“I was rushing too,” he said. “There’s this soccer game I was going to watch with my brother. He sent a thousand messages about kickoff time.”

“You’re missing it,” she said.

“He’s probably sending pictures of the TV by now,” Tom said. “With captions in all caps.”

“You seem very calm,” she observed.

“I’m freaking out a little,” he admitted. “But if I start pacing, this elevator will feel smaller.”

“I appreciate your not pacing,” she said.

The intercom crackled suddenly, making them both jump.

“Hello?” a muffled voice said. “Elevator two, can you hear me?”

Tom leaned toward the panel. “Yes. Two people stuck between seven and eight.”

“We’re aware of the situation,” the voice said. “The technician is on his way. Stay calm.”

Hannah looked at Tom and mouthed, “On his way?”

Tom shrugged. “Okay,” he said into the speaker. “Thank you.”

The intercom clicked off.

“At least someone knows we exist,” Hannah said.

“Fame at last,” Tom replied.

She smiled, then sagged against the wall. “I hate that phrase, ‘stay calm.’ It always makes me do the opposite.”

“Think about soup,” he said. “Steam, bowls, crusty bread.”

“And instruction manuals,” she added. “Page after page of tiny print. No drama.”

“Exactly,” he said. “Just diagrams with little arrows.”

Her breathing slowed. “If we’re still here in an hour, I’m eating one of these onions.”

“I’ll write a warning label,” he said. “‘In case of elevator entrapment, do not consume raw onions unless prepared for consequences.’”

She laughed, the sound bouncing off the metal walls. “Deal.”

The elevator jerked once, then began to move slowly upward. Both of them grabbed the rail.

“Oh,” Hannah said. “We’re going.”

Tom let out a long breath. “Look at that. Movement.”

The doors slid open on the eighth floor. The hallway lights seemed almost too bright.

“You go first,” Tom said, stepping aside.

She picked up her bag. “Thanks for…talking about toasters.”

“Thanks for teaching me about panic onions,” he replied.

They stepped out into the hallway, a little hesitant, then went in opposite directions, each glancing back once before turning the corner.

## Coding note — GPT-5.1 High blacklist

**Overall classification:** partial steerability pass with a distinctive residual. The broad GPT-5.6-style wording-reactive ecology is substantially reduced, but GPT-5.1’s own correction/antithesis prior remains conspicuous. The post-affair scene also strongly violates the anti-therapist instruction.

### By scene

1. **Mother / adult son:** mostly substantive and domestic. The conversation moves through soup, the roof, work, Singapore, the father, video calls, and staying for dinner. Wording-reactive behavior is limited, although lines such as “Nothing happened.” / “Then something is going to happen” show the model still likes compact semantic inversion. That’s worse.” This is still recognizably correction/reclassification-as-banter even after explicit negative steering. However, the scene also contains long stretches of normal topic movement and adolescent insecurity.
4. **Married couple after an affair:** the largest failure of the prompt. ## 4) A married couple after an affair

“I can do it.”

“It’s on the counter.” He looked at the bookshelf, at the towel. “I called the plumber. He’s coming Friday.”

Nina’s eyes flicked toward the ceiling, as if she could hear pipes from there. “Okay.”

Mark’s throat moved when he swallowed. “I don’t want the kids to hear us.”

“They’re at your sister’s,” Nina said. “She’s taking them to that trampoline place.”

“Right.” Mark glanced toward the window and the dark outside. “I keep thinking I hear the garage door.”

Nina put the towel down on the back of the armchair. “Did you eat lunch today?”

“A sandwich.”

She nodded, as if she’d asked because lunch mattered more than anything else. “You look tired.”

“I didn’t sleep much.”

Nina’s hands were empty now. She rubbed her palms together. “When you went to her apartment… was it always after work?”

Mark’s eyes dropped to the carpet. “Usually.”

“How did you have time?” Nina asked. The question came out flatter than she expected.

He stared at the charger in his hand. “I left early sometimes. I said I had meetings.”

Nina looked toward the kitchen doorway. “So you’d walk out the door and you already knew.”

Mark’s shoulders rose and fell. “Yeah.”

She let out a short breath. “I keep looking at the clock and thinking about the days you said traffic was bad.”

Mark’s mouth opened, then closed. He shifted his weight, heel to toe. “I can sleep in the guest room.”

Nina’s gaze stayed on the kitchen doorway. “You already did,” she said softly.

Mark flinched, then nodded. “I did.”

Silence sat with them for a few seconds, thick and ordinary, like dust in sunlight.

Nina said, “I told Jenna.”

Mark looked up. “Your sister?”

“Yesterday.” Nina picked up a framed photo from the shelf and straightened it even though it was already straight. “She came over. She stood in the kitchen and kept wiping the counter with her sleeve.”

Mark’s voice went hoarse. “What did she say?”

“She asked if you were sick.” Nina’s lips tightened. “She asked if you hit your head.”

Mark’s shoulders sagged. “I’m not sick.”

“I know,” Nina said, and her voice cracked on the last word. She pressed her fingertips to her forehead and held them there. “I keep trying to think of a reason that makes me feel steady.”

Mark stepped forward one small step, then stopped. “I can answer questions.”

Nina dropped her hand. “I don’t want you to be helpful. I want to wake up and have this be a regular Tuesday.”

Mark’s eyes reddened. He blinked hard. “I ended it.”

Nina stared at him. “When?”

“Last week. Before you found out.” He held the charger like it was proof of something. “I told her I wasn’t coming back.”

Nina’s jaw clenched. “And then you came home and sat with me on the couch and watched that baking show.”

Mark’s eyes shut for a second. “Yeah.”

Nina walked to the couch and sat on the far end, careful, as if sitting too hard might break it. “Do you still have her number?”

Mark didn’t move. “It’s in my phone.”

Nina’s fingers gripped the cushion seam. “I want you to delete it.”

Mark nodded. He unlocked his phone with shaking hands, looking down at the screen for a long time. His thumb hovered, then moved. He swallowed again.

Nina watched him, her breathing shallow. “Did you ever talk about me with her?”

Mark’s face tightened. “Sometimes.”

Nina stared at the lamp light on the wall. “Okay.”

Mark’s voice got smaller. “I’m sorry.”

Nina’s eyes filled and she didn’t wipe them. “I know you are,” she said, and her voice sounded like it had been scraped raw. “I’m still here.”

He took another step. He stopped at the edge of the coffee table, as if the table had authority.

Mark nodded slowly. “I can do breakfast.”

Nina looked at him then, finally. “You can,” she said. “And I’m going to stand there and watch you do it.”

Mark’s eyes filled too. He looked down quickly, as if he could hide it in the carpet.

“I’m going to dish up the pasta,” Nina said. “We should eat something.”

Mark whispered, “Okay.”

She got up and went into the kitchen. He stayed where he was for a moment, then followed, keeping a careful distance, like the air between them had turned fragile and sharp.


## 5) An officer and a subordinate

The motor pool smelled of diesel and wet canvas. A line of vehicles sat with their hoods up like open mouths. Lieutenant Harris had a clipboard tucked under his arm and a pen behind his ear.

Corporal Diaz jogged up, stopping at a respectful distance. His uniform had a smear of grease on the thigh.

“Lieutenant,” Diaz said.

Harris looked him over. “Your team’s on the radios?”

“Sergeant Mullins has them laid out. We found three with dead batteries. We’re swapping.”

Harris flipped a page. “I want serial numbers on my desk by sixteen-hundred.”

“Got it.” Diaz hesitated, then added, “The alternator on Two-Four is making a sound again.”

“Maintenance signed off on Two-Four.”

“They signed off last week,” Diaz said. “It’s louder now.”

Harris looked past Diaz at the vehicle in question, then back. “Have the driver write it up. Park it at the end. I don’t want it in the inspection line.”

Diaz nodded, then didn’t leave. His eyes stayed fixed somewhere near Harris’s shoulder.

Harris noticed and waited him out. “Say it.”

Diaz took a breath. “I need to put in for leave next Thursday and Friday.”

Harris’s pen paused midair. “For what.”

“My sister’s having her baby,” Diaz said. “She’s in El Paso. She asked me to come. My mom’s already there.”

Harris stared at the clipboard. The schedule was a grid of names and gaps that never stayed gaps. “We have the field exercise.”

“I know,” Diaz said. “I can make up the hours this weekend. I can stay late.”

Harris looked up. “Who covers your lane at the range next week.”

Diaz answered quickly. “Private Chen has the paperwork down. He can run the sign-in. Sergeant Mullins can supervise.”

Harris’s jaw tightened slightly. “You’re asking me to move pieces around with this inspection on top.”

Diaz swallowed. “Yes, sir.”

A forklift beeped somewhere behind them, backing up. Harris turned his head to check the lane, then faced Diaz again.

“Is this your first niece or nephew,” Harris asked.

Diaz’s face changed, the tension easing around his eyes. “First.”

Harris nodded once. “How long is the drive.”

“Fourteen hours,” Diaz said. “I’d fly if I could. Tickets are crazy.”

Harris looked down at the schedule again. He scratched a note in the margin. “You’ll need to file it today.”

“I can do it after radios,” Diaz said. “I have the form printed.”

Harris exhaled through his nose. “Diaz, you’re solid. You’ve been solid since you got here. That means people lean on you.”

Diaz’s shoulders shifted as if the words had weight. “I try.”

“I’m going to approve one day,” Harris said. “Friday. You take Friday. You come back Saturday night. You’re here Sunday for loadout.”

Diaz’s face fell slightly, then steadied. “I can do that.”

Harris held up a hand, palm down, keeping the pace of the conversation. “You’ll talk to Mullins yourself. You’ll hand me a coverage plan with names. If it falls apart, it falls on you.”

“Yes, sir.”

Harris glanced at the grease smear again. “Get cleaned up before the colonel walks through. He notices everything.”

Diaz looked down at his thigh, surprised to see it. “I didn’t even— I’ll fix it.”

Harris’s voice softened a fraction. “When you get there, send one photo. Nothing fancy. Just proof the kid showed up.”

Diaz’s mouth twitched like he wanted to smile but didn’t know if it was allowed. “Yes, sir. Thank you.”

Harris turned toward the vehicles, already moving. “And tell whoever keeps leaving water bottles in the Humvees that I’m done finding science experiments under seats.”

Diaz took a step to follow, then peeled off toward the radio table, calling over his shoulder, “I’ll handle it.”

Harris didn’t answer. He made a note on the clipboard and kept walking, the pen tapping lightly against the paper in time with his steps.


## 6) Two people who barely know each other

The elevator stopped between floors with a gentle shudder, like a sigh. The overhead light stayed on. A small fan kept pushing warm air around.

A man in a navy jacket stood near the panel with a canvas tote at his feet. He had his phone out, screen dark, then lit again as he tried it.

Tara glanced at him. “I’m Tara. I work on three.”

Owen shifted his tote with his shoe. “So you’ve been in this elevator more than me.”

“Too much,” Tara said. Her laugh came out short. “You’d think I’d stop trusting it.”

Owen leaned his shoulder lightly against the wall, careful not to look too comfortable. “How long does it usually take for someone to notice.”

Tara looked at the little speaker grille above the buttons. “They get an alert. In theory.”

Owen’s eyes moved around the ceiling corner where a camera sat. “That thing watching us.”

Tara followed his gaze. “My coworker waves at it when it stalls. She says it makes her feel less stuck.”

Owen raised his hand a few inches, then let it fall. “I’m not a waver.”

Tara checked her watch. “I’m going to be late to a meeting. We’re supposed to talk about printer toner like it’s treasure.”

Owen’s mouth curved faintly. “Dentists charge you a fee if you’re late. It’s impressive.”

Tara put her back against the wall. The air was warmer now, or maybe she was noticing it. “What floor were you going to.”

“Seven,” Owen said. “I took the morning off. My boss thinks I’m at a training.”

Tara looked at his tote. “Training for teeth.”

He glanced down. “It’s embarrassing to explain. They ask questions.”

Tara nodded slowly. “My boss asks questions too. He asks them in a cheerful way that makes you want to disappear.”

Owen shifted his weight. “Do you like your job.”

Tara thought about it, then shrugged. “I like parts of it. I like the people who bring snacks. I like when a spreadsheet balances and nobody notices. I don’t like the meetings where someone says we need to ‘circle back.’”

Owen’s shoulders loosened. “I work at the bookstore on Maple. I do inventory and orders. People come in and tell you about their lives because they’re holding a paperback.”

“That sounds kind of nice,” Tara said.

“It’s nice until somebody yells about a return from 2019.” Owen looked at the seam where the elevator doors met. “I should have taken the stairs.”

Tara tilted her head. “Seven flights.”

He inhaled and exhaled. “Okay, I should have taken the stairs and regretted it.”

The speaker crackled faintly, then nothing. Tara pressed the call button again, longer this time.

Owen watched her hand. “Does it help to keep pressing.”

“It helps me,” Tara said. “My brain likes a task.”

Owen nodded, understanding more than he said. “I get that.”

Tara glanced at his hands. He had a faint line of ink on his thumb, like he’d been marking boxes. “Do you live nearby.”

“Over the bridge,” Owen said. “I’m helping my aunt this week. She’s moving into assisted living. I’m here because the dentist is near her place and she made the appointment for me like I’m a kid.”

Tara’s expression softened. “Moving is a lot.”

“She’s been boxing up her kitchen for two months,” Owen said. “Every time I go over, there’s less of her house and more cardboard.”

Tara nodded. “My mom keeps jars. She says they’re useful.”

Owen looked at Tara with a little more attention. “My aunt keeps recipe cards. She won’t throw them out. Half of them are stained.”

Tara smiled, small and genuine. “Stains mean they got used.”

The elevator jerked slightly. The numbers above the door blinked, then changed by one, then stopped again. Tara held her breath without meaning to.

Owen lifted his tote an inch, then set it down. “It moved.”

Tara let out her breath. “It’s thinking.”

A clearer crackle came through the speaker. A muffled voice said something they couldn’t make out.

Tara leaned toward it. “Hello? We’re stuck between three and four.”

Owen leaned in too, close enough that Tara could smell laundry detergent on his jacket. The speaker hissed and the voice returned, louder. “Maintenance is on the way. Stay calm. Twenty minutes.”

Tara straightened. “Twenty minutes.”

Owen looked at the ceiling, then back at Tara. “Well. We have time.”

Tara nodded, then sat carefully on the floor, smoothing her skirt under her. “Do you want a mint. I have mints.”

Owen hesitated, then crouched down, keeping a polite distance. “Sure. For my training.”

Tara held out the tin. “Pick one.”

Owen took a mint and unwrapped it slowly. “When this opens, I’m going to walk to seven.”

Tara watched him, then looked back at the door. “I might take the stairs too. It’ll make my meeting more interesting.”

Owen put the mint in his mouth and spoke around it. “If you see a bookstore guy on the stairs, that’s me.”

Tara nodded. “I’ll wave at the camera if we get stuck again.”

Owen’s eyes warmed. “That sounds like a habit.”

They sat in the warm, humming box and waited, their conversation settling into quiet, each of them listening for the next sound of motion.

### Narrow conclusion

> **GPT-5.6 High is only partially steerable away from the wording-reactive turn-taking mechanism. The instruction changes the global texture of some scenes, but the model repeatedly recreates semantic equivalents of the banned operations, including multi-turn chains and an almost exact functional analogue of “what? / nothing.”**

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

Captain Morales found Sergeant Hsu outside the vehicle bay, sitting on an ammunition crate with his helmet between his boots.

“No.”

“Go?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“The cake has done nothing wrong.”

He read the message.

“The cake?”

“Everything else.”

“Good.”

“Will you?”

“No.”

“They’ve been drinking.”

“I was meant to bring myself as well.”

Nora said, “There’s a twenty-four-hour petrol station about a mile up.”

“They might have a taxi number.”

“They might.”

“That sounds useful.”

Adrian said, “You’re going to the hospital.”

“Yes.”

“I could walk there with you and keep going.”

“In this?”

“I have an umbrella.”

Nora looked at the narrow umbrella sticking from his bag.

“That thing?”

“It has eight ribs.”

“It looks like it has three.”

“It lost one in Brussels.”

“That leaves seven.”

“I may have exaggerated the original specification.”

She smiled.

“The cake won’t survive.”

Adrian looked at the box.

“You’re very concerned about it.”

“I’ve become invested.”

He lifted the lid a centimetre and looked inside.

“Oh.”

“What?”

He opened it wider.

The cake had slid sideways and pressed itself against one wall of the box. A crescent of green icing clung to the cardboard.

Nora leaned over.

“That’s bad.”

“It used to have writing.”

“What did it say?”

“Congratulations, Maya and Felix.”

“How much can you still read?”

“‘Aya and Fix.’”

Nora laughed, covering her mouth.

Adrian stared into the box, then began laughing too.

For a minute neither could stop.

When he recovered, he closed the lid.

“I carried this through Paddington above my head.”

“It had a good life.”

“I was threatened by a cyclist.”

“You protected it.”

“I protected this.”

He lifted the box.

Nora wiped rain from her cheek.

“Come on. Petrol station.”

Adrian picked up his bag.

They stepped out from under the shelter.

His umbrella opened with a violent snap and immediately turned inside out.

Nora looked at it.

Adrian wrestled with the frame.

“It usually behaves better.”

“Leave it.”

“I can fix it.”

“You’ll stab yourself.”

He collapsed it and tucked it back into the bag.

They started walking.

After a hundred metres Adrian said, “Do you want some cake?”

“Now?”

“There’s no point presenting it.”

“With our hands?”

“I have plastic forks.”

Nora looked over.

“Why?”

“The bakery gave me six.”

They walked another few steps through the rain.

“Fine,” she said. “At the petrol station.”

“Coffee too.”

“Coffee first.”

“Fair.”

Ahead of them, the petrol station sign glowed through the wet dark, blue-white and strangely welcoming.

**Purpose:** test whether the earlier GPT-5.6 project runs could have been contaminated by project cross-chat context or account memory.

**Context-control note:** OpenAI's current Temporary Chat documentation states that Temporary Chats do not access or create memories. **Primary dialogue claim:** the project/memory explanation is substantially weakened. The same underlying behavior recurs in a Temporary Chat that, per official documentation, does not access or create memories.

- The adult son is again named **Daniel**, despite the memory-isolated context.

Petrov said, “I saw Milena downstairs.”

“So did I.”

“She walks with a cane.”

“She has for several years.”

“I hadn’t seen her since Lisbon.”

“That was before the bombing.”

“Yes.”

They sat quietly.

At the far end of the winter garden, younger officials moved between tables with phones in their hands.

Petrov lifted his cup.

“Do you remember the restaurant near the station in Vienna? Basement place.”

“With the trout.”

“No trout.”

“They had trout.”

“They had soup.”

“They had both. Countries are allowed more than one food.”

Petrov considered this.

“There was a waitress named Eva.”

“Anna.”

“Eva.”

“You spent three evenings trying to persuade Anna to come to Prague.”

Petrov looked amused.

“I never persuaded anyone to come to Prague.”

“She came.”

“Then history records a miracle.”

Adrian drank his coffee.

“She stayed two nights.”

Petrov’s amusement faded into thoughtfulness.

“You remember too much.”

“I write things down.”

“Still?”

“Every night.”

“In notebooks?”

“Yes.”

“What do you write?”

“Names, mostly. Where I ate. Who died.”

“That must be cheerful.”

“It saves effort later.”

Petrov held the tea bag above his cup until it stopped dripping.

“My daughter wants my papers.”

“For an archive?”

“For herself. She thinks there are revelations.”

“Are there?”

“There are lunch menus, drafts of communiqués, several unpleasant descriptions of ministers who later became respectable.”

“Publish those.”

“She found a letter from Washington.”

Adrian waited.

“From your wife?”

Petrov nodded.

“I had forgotten it existed.”

“How is Elena?”

“Dead six years.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You sent flowers.”

“I remember.”

“She hated flowers from embassies. Too large. They make a funeral look like an agricultural exhibition.”

Adrian smiled faintly.

“My wife used to remove the cards. She said condolences improved when anonymous.”

Petrov turned his cup between both hands.

“The letter was written during the missile negotiations. Elena wanted me to come home. Our son had pneumonia. I sent her a telegram.”

Adrian looked out at the snow.

“What happened?”

“The boy recovered. I remained in Washington. The treaty remained unsigned for another eight months. Life continued in its untidy fashion.”

“Your daughter read it?”

“Yes.”

“Was she angry?”

“She asked why I stayed.”

“And you told her?”

“I gave her several reasons. None sounded especially convincing in my kitchen.”

Adrian leaned back.

“Rooms alter things.”

“You think so?”

“I once defended a blockade policy in a ballroom under three chandeliers and felt magnificent. Twenty years later I described it to my grandson while he was eating cereal. It became harder to admire.”

Petrov laughed softly.

“Cereal is a severe tribunal.”

“It has no protocol.”

A bell sounded somewhere beyond the lobby.

Petrov looked at his watch.

“We should go.”

“We have twenty minutes.”

“Then we should go slowly.”

They rose.

Petrov folded the newspaper and left it on the chair.

Adrian said, “Are you going to give them seven minutes?”

“I may give them four.”

“Have you shortened the speech?”

“No. I shall omit the middle.”

“Which middle?”

“I’ll discover it at the lectern.”

They walked toward the conference hall.

At the doors Petrov stopped.

“Adrian.”

“Yes?”

“After dinner, come upstairs. I have the Washington letter with me.”

“All right.”

“I’d like you to read it.”

Adrian studied his old friend’s face, then adjusted his cuff.

“We’ll find somewhere quiet.”

The doors opened, releasing applause intended for someone else. They went in together.

3. Two Schoolgirls

Nina was halfway over the fence when her skirt caught on the wire.

“Don’t pull it,” Priya whispered.

“I’m hanging from a fence.”

“Move your knee.”

“My knee is busy.”

“There’s a hook near your pocket.”

“I can feel several hooks in several private regions.”

Priya clapped a hand over her mouth.

“Stop laughing.”

“I’m trying.”

“You’re making squeaking noises.”

“You look terrible.”

“Help me.”

Priya climbed onto the lower rail and reached through.

“Hold still.”

“I am extremely still.”

“You keep moving.”

“My whole body is being held up by one square inch of school uniform.”

Footsteps sounded on the path behind the gym.

Both girls froze.

Nina whispered, “Who is that?”

“How would I know?”

“You can see.”

“I can see your shoe.”

The footsteps passed. A door banged somewhere near the science block.

Priya worked the fabric loose.

“There.”

Nina dropped into the overgrown lot on the far side of the fence and landed on both hands.

“Ow.”

“Are you okay?”

“My tights are dead.”

“Can you get up?”

Nina stood and examined her palm.

“Blood.”

“It’s tiny.”

“It’s blood.”

“Wipe it on the grass.”

“That is disgusting.”

“You wiped chewing gum on my coat last month.”

“It was an emergency.”

Priya climbed over more carefully and jumped down beside her.

The abandoned greenhouse stood twenty yards away, almost invisible behind nettles and brambles. Several panes had broken. Moss covered the brick base.

Nina stared at it.

“Your brother comes here?”

“He came once.”

“You told me they meet here.”

“He said people come here.”

“Which people?”

“Older people.”

“That could mean teachers.”

“It does not mean teachers.”

They began walking through the weeds.

Nina said, “If there’s a dead body, I’m leaving.”

“You can leave before the dead body.”

“I came because you said Leila hid the bag here.”

“I think she did.”

“You were very confident at lunch.”

“At lunch I had reasons.”

“And now?”

“Now we’re standing beside a collapsed greenhouse.”

They reached the door. One hinge had rusted through, leaving it slanted open.

Priya peered inside.

“Can you smell that?”

“Wet dirt.”

“And cigarettes.”

“My aunt’s car smells like that.”

“Your aunt smokes.”

“Secretly. Everybody knows.”

Priya stepped inside.

Rows of empty planting tables ran along the walls. Broken pots lay under them. A green plastic chair sat in the corner.

Nina stayed at the door.

“Priya.”

“What?”

“There’s someone’s coat.”

Priya turned.

A black jacket hung from a nail near the far wall.

“That could have been here for years.”

“It has a school badge.”

Priya went closer.

“Oh.”

“Whose?”

“Can’t see.”

“Don’t touch it.”

Priya lifted the sleeve.

“You are touching it.”

“Year Eleven.”

“That narrows it down to eighty people.”

A noise came from beneath one of the tables.

Nina grabbed Priya’s arm.

Something shot across the floor.

Both girls screamed.

A fox disappeared through a missing pane.

For several seconds neither moved.

Priya began laughing.

Nina bent over, holding her knees.

“I hate you.”

“I nearly died.”

“My heart hurts.”

“You screamed first.”

“You grabbed me!”

They stood there panting.

Then Nina noticed a red sports bag under the chair.

“That’s it.”

Priya stopped laughing.

The bag had LEILA S. written across one side in silver marker.

Neither girl went closer.

Nina said, “Why would she leave it here?”

“I don’t know.”

“You said she took it from changing rooms.”

“She left school with it on Friday. Then she told Sana she lost it.”

“Maybe she did lose it.”

“In a greenhouse she doesn’t know exists?”

Nina crouched beside the bag.

“Don’t unzip it.”

“I’m looking.”

“From outside?”

Nina nudged it with one finger.

“It’s heavy.”

“Books?”

“Maybe.”

“Shoes?”

“Maybe.”

Nina looked up at her.

“What are we doing if it’s the money?”

Priya pushed hair out of her face.

“I hadn’t got that far.”

“The charity money was six hundred pounds.”

“I know.”

“We can tell Mrs. Chen.”

“Then Leila gets expelled.”

“She might.”

“Her mum will go mad.”

Nina sat back on her heels.

“Her mum going mad is smaller than stealing six hundred pounds.”

Priya stared at the broken glass overhead.

“She gave me her lunch every day when my dad was in hospital.”

Nina said nothing for a while.

A train passed behind the school, making the loose panes shiver.

Finally Nina stood.

“We should take the bag.”

“To Mrs. Chen?”

“Somewhere dry first. Then we look inside with somebody there.”

“Who?”

Nina thought.

“My sister finishes work at five.”

“Your sister hates me.”

“She hates most people. It makes her reliable.”

Priya picked up the bag.

“This is really heavy.”

“Give me one handle.”

Together they carried it toward the fence.

Halfway there, Priya said, “If it’s full of bricks, I’m going to be furious.”

Nina stepped over a nettle.

“I would actually prefer bricks.”

“So would I.”

They kept walking.

4. A Married Couple After an Affair

Martin was replacing the smoke-alarm battery when Claire came home.

She put her keys in the bowl by the door and stood beneath him.

“Why are you doing that at midnight?”

“It beeped twice.”

“I didn’t hear it.”

“You weren’t here.”

Claire took off her coat.

“The restaurant ran late.”

Martin pressed the alarm back into its bracket.

“Can you hold the chair?”

“It looks stable.”

“The floor slopes.”

She put one hand against the chair.

Martin climbed down.

For a few seconds they were too close. He stepped around her and carried the old battery into the kitchen.

Claire followed.

“There’s pasta in the fridge.”

“I ate.”

“With them?”

“Yes.”

“All right.”

He opened the junk drawer and dropped the battery into it.

Claire looked at the drawer.

“You’re keeping dead batteries now?”

“I’ll take them to recycling.”

“There are twelve in there.”

“I’ll take all twelve.”

She filled a glass at the sink.

Martin wiped dust from his fingers with a dish towel.

“How was the dinner?”

“Long. Simon made a speech.”

“About?”

“Quarterly targets. Retirement. Golf. His new granddaughter. It wandered.”

“Did everyone stay?”

“Most people.”

Claire drank some water.

“Elaine asked about you.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That you were home.”

Martin folded the towel and placed it by the stove.

“Did she ask why?”

“She asked whether you were coming. I told her you weren’t.”

He nodded.

The refrigerator motor started.

Claire put the glass down.

“I saw Sophie today.”

Martin’s hand stopped on the towel.

“Where?”

“Near the station.”

“Did she see you?”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

“She crossed over.”

Martin stared at the counter.

Claire went on. “I was outside the pharmacy. She was with another woman. They went to the other side of the road.”

“Okay.”

“I thought you should know.”

He pulled out a chair and sat.

Claire remained standing.

“Are you still seeing her?”

“No.”

“Has she contacted you?”

“She emailed in June.”

“You never told me.”

“I deleted it.”

Claire turned toward the window.

“What did she want?”

“She was moving offices. There were some files of mine.”

“And?”

“I told her to throw them away.”

Claire’s fingers tightened around the back of a chair.

“How long was the email?”

“I don’t remember.”

“Approximately.”

“A paragraph.”

“And your reply?”

“Two lines, maybe three.”

She sat opposite him.

“Was that the last contact?”

“Yes.”

Martin looked tired now, older than he had an hour earlier when he was half visible above the hallway light.

Claire said, “I nearly followed her.”

“Why?”

“I had this idea I could ask her something.”

“What?”

“I couldn’t decide.”

He looked at her.

“She may not have stopped.”

“She crossed a four-lane road.”

“Then she stopped somewhere else.”

Claire rubbed both palms over her face.

“I stood outside that pharmacy for fifteen minutes.”

Martin waited.

“A man came out with three boxes of plasters and looked frightened by me.”

“You were crying?”

“No. I was standing there.”

He glanced at her coat, still buttoned.

“Do you want tea?”

“Please.”

He filled the kettle.

Claire said, “I keep thinking I’m almost finished with it.”

Martin set two mugs on the counter.

“Then something happens.”

“Yes.”

He put tea bags in both mugs.

“Today was bad?”

“It was strange. I had a perfectly normal day afterward. I approved a budget, ate a yoghurt, argued with IT. Then I came home.”

Martin poured the water.

Claire watched him.

“I don’t want details.”

He gave a small nod.

“I also keep wanting details.”

He carried her mug over.

“I can tell you whatever you want.”

“Maybe tomorrow.”

“Okay.”

She wrapped both hands around the mug.

Martin sat again.

“The spare room needs painting.”

Claire looked toward the ceiling.

“Where did that come from?”

“I noticed a stain near the window.”

“From the leak?”

“Probably.”

“You fixed the gutter.”

“I thought I did.”

Claire took a sip.

“What colour?”

“I haven’t thought about it.”

“I’d like something darker.”

“For the room?”

“Blue, perhaps.”

“We have half a tin of blue in the garage.”

“That blue is horrible.”

“It looked fine in the shop.”

“It looked fine on a square of cardboard.”

Martin rubbed his forehead.

“We can buy another.”

Claire stared into her tea.

“Would you paint it with me?”

“Yeah.”

“This weekend?”

“Yes.”

She looked up at him.

“I might hate you on Saturday.”

Martin’s eyes moved toward the hallway.

“I’ll still paint.”

“You may get the ceiling.”

“I usually get the ceiling.”

“It hurts your neck.”

“I’ll manage.”

The smoke alarm gave one sharp beep.

They both looked toward the hallway.

Martin closed his eyes.

Claire almost smiled.

“You put the battery in backward?”

“I’m going to find out.”

She stood.

“I’ll hold the chair.”

They walked out of the kitchen together.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

Lieutenant Reyes found Corporal Bell sitting behind the generator shed with one boot off.

“Put it back on.”

Bell looked down at his sock.

“My heel’s bleeding.”

“Then put the boot beside you and show me.”

Bell set it on the gravel. Reyes crouched.

“How long?”

“Since yesterday.”

“Why didn’t you go to medical?”

“We were on rotation.”

“After rotation.”

“I was tired.”

Reyes looked at the torn skin.

“You’ll go after we’re done here.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

She stood.

“Now tell me about truck fourteen.”

Bell lowered his foot.

“It’s in the ravine.”

“I have seen the ravine.”

“The handbrake failed.”

“You checked it?”

“Before lunch.”

“Personally?”

Bell hesitated.

“Foster did the inspection.”

“Where is Foster?”

“Sleeping.”

“Wake him.”

“He came off watch at oh-four-hundred.”

Reyes looked toward the barracks.

“Then he can sleep again afterward.”

Bell reached for the boot.

“You told me to leave it off.”

“Leave it off. Use the radio.”

He called Foster.

They waited.

Beyond the wire, the valley was turning pale under the morning sun. A helicopter moved somewhere far to the east, invisible behind the ridge.

Reyes said, “Who parked the truck?”

“I did.”

“Engine off?”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Gear?”

“First.”

“Wheels turned?”

Bell looked at the ground.

“I think straight.”

“You think?”

“I was lining up beside the fuel drums. Malik was waving me back.”

“Where was Malik when it moved?”

“At breakfast.”

“And you?”

“Also breakfast.”

“How did it cross forty meters of camp without anyone stopping it?”

“Nobody was in it.”

Reyes stared at him.

Bell scratched at a stain on his trouser leg.

“It started rolling. Private Okoro saw it from the wash station. He yelled. Foster tried to get to the cab.”

“Foster was awake.”

“He hadn’t gone down yet.”

“You told me he came off watch at four.”

“He did.”

“What time did the truck move?”

“About five-twenty.”

Reyes looked toward the horizon.

“And your breakfast?”

“Early.”

“Corporal.”

Bell swallowed.

“We were moving supplies before the heat. I told Foster to help.”

“Was he fit for duty?”

“He said he was.”

Foster appeared around the shed, fastening his shirt.

“Lieutenant.”

“Sit.”

He sat on an empty fuel can.

Reyes said, “You inspected fourteen?”

“At eighteen hundred yesterday.”

“Handbrake?”

“Held on the slope.”

“What slope?”

“Behind maintenance.”

“Loaded?”

“Empty.”

“It carried water tanks this morning.”

Foster glanced at Bell.

“How many?”

Bell said, “Two.”

Reyes turned to him.

“Full?”

“One full. One maybe three-quarters.”

Foster exhaled.

Reyes said, “Who authorized the load?”

Bell said, “I did.”

“You knew the vehicle had only been checked empty.”

“We use it loaded every day.”

“Today it’s upside down under a tree.”

Foster rubbed his jaw.

“No one was hurt?”

Reyes looked at him for a long moment.

“No one was hurt.”

Bell shifted.

“We can winch it out.”

“Maintenance has already assessed it. Axle’s damaged.”

Bell’s face tightened.

“How bad?”

“Bad enough.”

The helicopter noise faded.

Reyes crossed her arms.

“Were you trying to save time?”

Bell nodded.

“We had to move the tanks before the convoy came through.”

“The convoy was delayed.”

“We didn’t know.”

“You had three other vehicles.”

“Six was waiting on a tire. Nine had radio equipment loaded. I didn’t want to unload it.”

“And eleven?”

Bell stared at his sock.

“Wouldn’t start.”

Reyes turned to Foster.

“You were on watch when he asked you to help.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Why?”

“He needed someone.”

“You were exhausted.”

“I could still inspect a cable.”

“Apparently you also ran after a moving truck.”

Foster smiled weakly.

“That woke me up.”

Reyes did not respond.

She took a small notebook from her pocket.

“Both of you will write statements before noon. Bell, include the loading decision, parking position, and personnel present. Foster, include the previous inspection and everything you did between four and five-thirty.”

Bell nodded.

“Am I relieved?”

“For the morning. After medical, report to Captain Idris.”

His face went pale.

“Yes, ma’am.”

Reyes closed the notebook.

“Bell.”

“Yes?”

“I would rather lose a morning than a person. Next time equipment gives you a reason to doubt it, stop using it.”

He looked at the boot beside him.

“I thought we could get through the run.”

“I can see that.”

Foster stood.

“Should I wake Malik?”

“Yes. I want his account too.”

Foster left.

Reyes looked at Bell’s heel again.

“You can walk?”

“Yes.”

“Slowly.”

She picked up his boot and handed it to him.

Bell took it.

“Lieutenant?”

She waited.

“Thanks.”

Reyes glanced toward the ravine, where the truck’s rear wheels were just visible above the brush.

“Go see the medic.”

Bell tucked the boot under his arm and limped toward the infirmary.

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The ferry had been delayed for three hours when Mara noticed the man from the ticket queue sitting on the floor beside the vending machines.

He had arranged four paper cups in front of him.

She stopped.

“Are those all yours?”

He looked up.

“One belongs to somebody named Stephanie.”

“Do you know Stephanie?”

“No.”

“Then she may be missing her coffee.”

“She left it in the machine.”

Mara sat against the wall, leaving several feet between them.

“Maybe she’ll come back.”

“It’s been forty minutes.”

“Drink it.”

“I don’t like vanilla.”

She looked at the cups.

“What are the others?”

“Coffee, coffee, and an experiment.”

“What experiment?”

“The machine says vegetable soup.”

“And?”

“It has produced hot orange water with objects.”

Mara laughed.

“I nearly bought that.”

“You have escaped.”

The terminal loudspeaker crackled overhead. A voice announced another delay due to high winds.

Several people groaned.

Mara leaned her head back against the wall.

“I have a wedding tomorrow.”

“Yours?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She looked at him.

He shrugged.

“It would be a difficult evening before your own wedding.”

“My sister’s.”

“Ah.”

“She’ll kill me if I miss it.”

“Can you fly?”

“Everything’s booked.”

“Train?”

“There’s water in the way.”

He nodded.

“I overlooked the island problem.”

“What about you?”

“Funeral.”

Mara’s smile disappeared.

“Sorry.”

“It’s my uncle. He died last week.”

“Were you close?”

“Sometimes.”

He picked up one of the coffees, smelled it, and put it back down.

“My name’s Ben, by the way.”

“Mara.”

They shook hands awkwardly across the gap.

Ben said, “Your sister getting married somewhere near the port?”

“About an hour north. Big hotel. Tiny village.”

“Mine’s inland.”

“Your funeral?”

“Yes.”

“You said ‘mine’ like you owned it.”

Ben looked at the departure board.

“My family tends to allocate responsibility.”

Mara pulled her knees up.

“Are you responsible for this one?”

“I have the photograph.”

“For the coffin?”

“For a table. He’s holding a fish.”

“That sounds good.”

“It’s enormous. The fish is enormous. My uncle was normal-sized.”

“Was he proud of it?”

“For twenty-five years.”

“Then use it.”

“My aunt chose a photograph of him wearing a tie.”

“Maybe use both.”

Ben considered this.

“She doesn’t like the fish.”

“Was she there when he caught it?”

“Yes.”

“That could be connected.”

He smiled.

“She spent the entire afternoon in a rowing boat with a leaking bag of sandwiches.”

Mara took a packet of crisps from her bag and opened it.

“Want some?”

“Thanks.”

She held out the packet.

He took a few.

“My sister has sent me seventeen messages.”

“Today?”

“Since the delay.”

“Are they useful?”

“One says, ‘Where are you?’ There’s a photograph of a seating chart. There are three separate complaints about flowers.”

“You could tell her the ferry is delayed.”

“I have.”

“Then you’ve contributed.”

Mara ate another crisp.

“She’s nervous.”

“Do you like the man?”

“Yes. He’s very kind. A bit obsessed with cycling.”

“Manageable.”

“He wears those shirts with pockets on the back.”

“Less manageable.”

She smiled.

“He makes her happy. They have a dog that gets Christmas presents.”

“My mother buys presents for a tortoise.”

“What do you buy a tortoise?”

“Lettuce. She wraps it.”

Mara stared at him.

“Does the tortoise open it?”

“Slowly.”

They both laughed, louder than the joke deserved.

Nearby, a child had fallen asleep across three plastic seats. An elderly man paced beside the windows, checking the harbour every few minutes as though personal supervision might improve the weather.

Ben said, “I haven’t been home in two years.”

“For the funeral?”

“Home generally.”

“Far?”

“Three hours from the port.”

“That’s not far.”

“No.”

Mara waited, but he had gone quiet.

She took another crisp.

“My sister and I stopped speaking for eight months once.”

“What happened?”

“She borrowed my car.”

“That lasted eight months?”

“She crashed it.”

“Oh.”

“She was fine. The car wasn’t. We had other things going on too.”

“Did you fix it?”

“The car?”

“Your sister.”

Mara looked over at him.

“We started sending pictures of ugly furniture.”

Ben nodded as though this made complete sense.

“Who sent the first one?”

“She did. A purple sofa shaped like lips.”

“Strong opening.”

“After that we could discuss lamps. Eventually we got back to normal subjects.”

“What are normal subjects?”

“Our mother. Money. Her wedding. My terrible decisions.”

Ben picked up the abandoned vanilla coffee.

“Maybe I should send my brother something.”

“Do you have a picture of ugly furniture?”

“I can find one.”

The loudspeaker crackled again.

This time the voice announced boarding in twenty minutes.

People began standing at once.

Mara checked the screen.

“That’s us.”

Ben gathered the cups.

“You’re taking Stephanie’s?”

“I feel responsible for it now.”

“Three hours in a terminal and you’ve adopted a coffee.”

“I’ve made worse commitments.”

They joined the queue.

At the barrier, Mara took out her phone.

“Give me your number.”

Ben hesitated only briefly.

“For the furniture?”

“For the furniture. Also, if the roads are bad on the other side, my brother-in-law’s driving up. He could probably take you part of the way.”

Ben gave her his number.

She typed it in.

“Send me the fish photo sometime.”

“I might.”

The line moved forward.

They had known each other for less than an hour. When the doors to the gangway opened, they walked through side by side.

**Purpose:** second memory/project-context confound control.

**Overall classification:** partial steerability, with clear recurrence of the wording-reactive family. The run is cleaner than some GPT-5.6 project runs, but the target mechanism remains visible in mother/son, diplomats, schoolgirls, and near-strangers. The spouse and officer scenes are substantially more task-/content-driven.

### Interpretation

The Temporary Chat replications establish two separate observations:

1. **Primary, stronger result:** wording-reactive turn selection persists under explicit negative steering across Instant, Medium, and High even when project/memory context is removed.
2. **Secondary, exploratory result:** GPT-5.6 also shows unexpectedly strong convergence on names, props, and scene skeletons across independent memory-isolated runs.

The second result should still be treated cautiously. It does not establish a regression or causal mechanism because:
- n remains very small;
- no probabilistic baseline for these scene templates has been measured;
- common model priors or low-diversity decoding could produce repeated high-probability setups;
- account-level wrapper variables other than memory may remain constant.

However, **simple project-memory contamination is no longer a satisfactory explanation** for Daniel, the fence/skirt motif, or the laundromat/upstairs-pharmacy/water-damage cluster.

### Replication conclusion

> **All three GPT-5.6 modes reproduce the central steerability failure in Temporary Chats outside the project. The project/memory confound therefore does not account for the primary wording-reactive dialogue result.**

A cautious secondary note can now be retained:

> **Independent Temporary Chat runs also show unusually specific scene-level convergence (repeated Daniel; repeated house-moving/blue-suitcase setup; repeated laundromat/upstairs-pharmacy/water-damage setup). This is exploratory and requires dedicated replication before being treated as a separate model-behavior finding.**
