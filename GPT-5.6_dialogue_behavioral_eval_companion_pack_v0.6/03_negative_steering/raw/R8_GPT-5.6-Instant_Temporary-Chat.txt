
1. Mother and Adult Son

The kitchen light had begun to buzz again. Miriam stood on a chair with the plastic cover of the fixture in one hand and a dish towel over her shoulder.

“Hold the chair.”

Daniel put down the grocery bag.

“You’re wearing slippers.”

“They have soles.”

“They have felt.”

“They have soles under the felt.”

He put one hand on the chair back. “Can I do that?”

“You’ll break the little tabs.”

“I’ve changed light bulbs.”

“This has little tabs.”

“I can see them.”

“You’re pulling the chair.”

“I’m holding it.”

Miriam unscrewed the dead fluorescent tube and handed it down to him. “Put that somewhere safe.”

He set it on the table between the fruit bowl and a pile of unopened mail.

“Not there. It’ll roll.”

Daniel moved it to the counter.

“You brought too much food.”

“It’s four things.”

“There are two bags.”

“One is toilet paper.”

“I have toilet paper.”

“You had two rolls yesterday.”

“That lasts me weeks.”

Daniel looked at her.

“All right, maybe not weeks.”

“Come down, Ma.”

“I’m putting the cover back.”

“You can put it back after.”

“After what?”

“After you come down.”

Miriam pressed the cover into place. It slipped on one side.

“See? The tabs.”

“I see.”

“Give me the screwdriver.”

“You don’t need a screwdriver for that.”

“There’s a screw.”

“I thought those were tabs.”

“There are tabs and a screw.”

Daniel found the screwdriver beside the toaster, where she had apparently left it before climbing up. He passed it to her handle-first.

“You’ve been up here alone doing this?”

“Who else would be here?”

“You could call me.”

“You work.”

“Sometimes I’m home.”

“I don’t keep your schedule in my head.”

“You call me at work about the printer.”

“That was different.”

“You called because the printer said it was out of cyan.”

“It refused to print black.”

“That’s what they do.”

“Crooks.”

He smiled, though she couldn’t see him.

“There. Done.”

She climbed down slowly. Daniel kept both hands near her waist without touching her. When she reached the floor, she folded the chair and carried it toward the pantry.

“I can take it.”

“I’m already taking it.”

He let her.

She came back and began unloading the grocery bag.

“I bought you those crackers.”

“I don’t like those.”

“You ate a box last week.”

“Because they were here.”

“There’s soup too.”

“I can shop.”

“I know.”

“And grapes.”

“You like grapes.”

“They go bad.”

“Eat them.”

“I do eat.”

Daniel sat at the table.

Miriam stopped with the grapes in her hands. “You look terrible.”

“Thanks.”

“You look gray.”

“I slept four hours.”

“Again?”

“Eli had a fever.”

“How high?”

“One-oh-one. Maybe one-oh-two.”

“And you didn’t call.”

“At two in the morning?”

“You used to.”

“When I was twenty-five.”

“You were still my son at twenty-five.”

“I’m still your son now.”

She opened the refrigerator, then closed it because there was nowhere obvious to put the grapes.

“How is he?”

“Fine this morning. Maya stayed home with him.”

“He gets everything from daycare.”

“That seems to be the business model.”

Miriam took out half a cabbage, inspected it, and put it back.

Daniel watched her. “I talked to Dr. Shah.”

Her hands stopped inside the refrigerator.

“He called me yesterday.”

“Why is he calling you?”

“Because you put my number down.”

“For emergencies.”

“He’s concerned about the dizzy spells.”

“I told him about those.”

“He wants the scan moved up.”

“I have an appointment.”

“In six weeks.”

“That’s when they had one.”

“He found one Thursday.”

Miriam shut the refrigerator.

“No.”

“Thursday at ten-thirty.”

“I have bridge.”

Daniel looked toward the unopened mail.

“You can miss bridge once.”

“They need four.”

“Someone else can play.”

“They plan around people.”

“So do hospitals.”

Miriam picked up the crackers and began looking for cupboard space.

“He frightened you,” she said.

“Yes.”

“He frightens everybody. He has that voice.”

“It wasn’t his voice.”

“I stood up too fast twice. Once in the garden.”

“You fell in the garden.”

“I sat down hard.”

“You bruised your hip.”

“It’s gone.”

“Ma.”

She put the crackers beside three unopened boxes of tea.

“What?”

Daniel rubbed his forehead. “Thursday.”

“I heard the day.”

“I’ll take you.”

“You have work.”

“I’ll move things.”

“You were just telling me how busy you are.”

“I didn’t tell you that.”

Miriam turned around. He saw the answer forming in her face, then disappearing.

She pulled out a chair and sat opposite him.

“Your father hated hospitals.”

“I remember.”

“He’d get angry in the parking lot. Before we even went inside. Once he yelled at a parking meter.”

“He yelled at most machines.”

“He thought the machine had stolen two quarters.”

“It probably had.”

Miriam smiled faintly. “You were there?”

“I was thirteen.”

“You remember everything from thirteen.”

“I remember him kicking the meter.”

“He had gout.”

“That explains the wisdom of kicking metal.”

She laughed, then covered her mouth because the laugh became a cough.

Daniel waited.

When it passed, she said, “Thursday. Ten-thirty.”

“Yes.”

“I want coffee first.”

“They may tell you not to eat.”

“Coffee isn’t eating.”

“I’ll check.”

“Plain coffee.”

“I’ll check.”

“And don’t hover.”

“I won’t hover.”

“You do.”

“I’ll sit very calmly ten inches away.”

Miriam reached over and pushed the grocery receipt toward him.

“You spent twenty-eight dollars on toilet paper.”

“It was on sale.”

“For twenty-eight dollars it should come with plumbing.”

He folded the receipt into a small square and put it in his pocket.

The light buzzed overhead.

Miriam looked up.

“I hate that sound.”

“I’ll come Saturday and replace the fixture.”

“You’ll break the tabs.”

“Probably.”

She nodded, satisfied enough to leave it there.

2. Two Old Diplomats

Sir Alistair Bell found Ambassador Kovács on the hotel terrace with a glass of mineral water and three sugar packets lined up in front of him.

“You still steal those.”

Kovács slid one packet into his jacket pocket.

“The hotel has already charged me forty-two euros for breakfast.”

“You had breakfast downstairs?”

“I had coffee and one egg that had been frightened by warm water.”

Alistair lowered himself into the wicker chair opposite. “You should have gone across the street.”

“At our age, crossing streets for eggs becomes an ideological position.”

A waiter approached.

“Tea,” Alistair said. “Darjeeling, if possible.”

Kovács looked toward the lake. “He’ll bring Earl Grey.”

“He might surprise us.”

“He is twenty-three. He has no reason to surprise us.”

Around them, the conference lunch was breaking up. Younger delegates moved between the terrace doors and the lawn, carrying folders and phones. A banner on the hotel façade announced THE HELSINKI DIALOGUE ON EUROPEAN SECURITY.

Alistair loosened his tie.

“You spoke for nineteen minutes.”

“I was given twenty.”

“You usually leave a little ammunition unused.”

“I may be dying.”

“You’ve been dying since Prague.”

“Prague improved me.”

“Prague gave you pneumonia.”

“And therefore improved my habits for almost six months.”

The waiter brought Earl Grey.

Kovács watched Alistair examine the tag.

“I feel vindicated.”

“You frequently do.”

Alistair poured the tea. “The Romanian girl was good.”

“Deputy foreign minister.”

“She looks twelve.”

“She is forty-six.”

“That’s increasingly twelve.”

Kovács put another sugar packet in his pocket.

“You saw the draft?”

“Which draft?”

“The closed-session paper.”

“I saw a page with eight verbs and no nouns.”

“They’re going to circulate a revised version before dinner.”

“I’m leaving before dinner.”

Kovács turned from the lake. “Your train is tomorrow.”

“I’m leaving the conference.”

“That sounds theatrical.”

“It’s transportation. My driver comes at five.”

“Then you’ll miss Petrov.”

“I can live with that.”

“Petrov asked whether you would be here.”

Alistair stirred his tea although he had taken no milk or sugar.

“When?”

“This morning.”

“And?”

“I told him you were on the program.”

“You could have avoided that.”

“He was reading the program while he asked.”

Alistair took a drink.

Kovács leaned back. “He wants ten minutes.”

“He has had thirty years.”

“He wants ten today.”

“He can write.”

“He may prefer a room without paper.”

“Then he can enjoy one.”

A gull landed on the stone balustrade. Kovács tore a corner from his bread roll.

“Don’t feed that.”

“I’m considering it.”

“It’ll bring others.”

“That is how alliances work.”

Alistair ignored this and looked toward the lawn, where two delegations were assembling for a photograph.

“Did he look ill?”

“Petrov?”

“Yes.”

“He looked old.”

“He’s younger than you.”

“Then he looked tired.”

Alistair set down his cup.

“In ninety-four he came to my house.”

“I remember.”

“You weren’t there.”

“I remember hearing.”

“He brought apricots.”

“In London?”

“A wooden crate of them. Half were rotten by the time they arrived. Customs had opened everything.”

Kovács nodded. “That sounds like him.”

“He sat in my dining room for four hours. Jane kept making coffee. He smoked at the table. She hated smoking.”

“Jane hated Petrov.”

“She found him amusing.”

“She found snakes amusing.”

Alistair glanced at him.

Kovács shrugged. “She told Éva that.”

“Jane and Éva discussed us too much.”

“They had to fill their evenings somehow.”

The gull hopped closer.

Kovács finally ate the bread himself.

Alistair said, “What does he want?”

“He thinks the new inspection arrangement can still be modified before September.”

“That isn’t in our hands.”

“It was in our hands once.”

“We were more dangerous then.”

“We had staffs.”

“We had secure telephones.”

“We had knees.”

Kovács laughed softly.

After a moment he said, “He asked about Jane.”

Alistair’s face changed very little.

“Did he.”

“I told him.”

“Yes.”

“He remembered the apricots.”

Alistair looked toward the lake.

The photographer on the lawn began waving people into tighter rows. Someone called for the Belgian delegation.

“I threw the crate away,” Alistair said. “Jane kept the little iron nails from it. They were handmade. She used them for something in the garden.”

Kovács waited.

“I found three in a drawer after she died.”

The waiter returned with the bill. Kovács took it before Alistair could.

“I’m paying.”

“You stole six sugars.”

“Five.”

“That hardly strengthens your case.”

Kovács signed the slip.

“Petrov will be in the small library at four-fifteen.”

Alistair looked at his watch.

“My driver comes at five.”

“The library is downstairs.”

“I know where it is.”

“You could still leave at five.”

Alistair drank the remaining tea, which had gone cold.

“You arranged this.”

“I asked the hotel for a room.”

“You remain intolerable.”

“I’ve spent sixty years maintaining consistency.”

They sat without speaking for a while.

At four minutes past four, Alistair stood.

“My legs have gone numb.”

“Mine did that in Vienna.”

“Vienna lasted nine months.”

“So did the numbness.”

Alistair picked up his folder.

“Ten minutes.”

Kovács remained seated.

“I’ll be here.”

“For what?”

“In case you need your driver delayed.”

Alistair gave him a long look, then walked toward the terrace doors.

Kovács reached across the table and put the last sugar packet in Alistair’s abandoned saucer.

3. Two Schoolgirls

The girls were behind the sports hall because no teacher ever came there unless somebody had broken a window.

Nina sat on the low brick wall with her schoolbag between her knees. Leah was trying to peel the label from a bottle of orange soda in one piece.

“It tore.”

“You always pull too fast.”

“I was going slowly.”

“You were talking.”

“That doesn’t use my fingers.”

Nina took the bottle and picked at the remaining white strip with a thumbnail.

Leah watched.

“You’ve got better nails.”

“My aunt does them.”

“Your aunt charges thirty pounds.”

“She doesn’t charge me.”

“Because she’s your aunt.”

Nina passed the bottle back. “There.”

The label came away intact.

Leah held it up. “I’m keeping this.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

She folded it and put it into the front pocket of her blazer.

From the other side of the building came the dull bounce of basketballs and a whistle.

Nina said, “Are you going in?”

“For PE?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“You’ll get a detention.”

“I’ve already got Wednesday.”

“What for?”

“Science.”

“What happened in science?”

Leah unscrewed the soda. “Mr. Fell asked for my homework.”

“And?”

“I didn’t have it.”

“That gets you a mark.”

“I’d used the sheet for something else.”

“What?”

Leah drank.

“What?”

“I wrote to my dad.”

Nina stopped scraping dirt from the side of her shoe.

“Did you send it?”

“No.”

“Where is it?”

“At home.”

“Why did you use your science homework?”

“It was there.”

“You have paper.”

“Not in my room.”

“You’ve got about nine notebooks.”

“They have stuff in them.”

Nina looked toward the corner of the building.

“Is he still in Birmingham?”

“Apparently.”

“Your mum told you?”

“My gran.”

“Did you ask?”

“She was talking to my mum in the kitchen. I was upstairs.”

“You heard?”

“The house is tiny.”

Leah sat beside her.

“He has another kid.”

Nina’s head turned.

“A little one?”

“Three.”

“Boy or girl?”

“Girl.”

“Oh.”

Leah drank again.

Nina said, “What’s her name?”

“Poppy.”

“Poppy?”

“Yeah.”

They were quiet.

A group of Year Tens went by at the far end of the car park. One of them shouted something. Neither girl answered.

Nina said, “That’s a nice name.”

Leah rubbed her sleeve across her nose.

“I hate it.”

“Okay.”

“There’s a photo.”

“Did you see it?”

“Gran had it on Facebook. They were at some farm thing. With goats.”

“Your dad likes goats?”

“He looks stupid.”

Nina nudged the toe of Leah’s shoe.

Leah continued, “He’s holding this little girl and she’s got yellow wellies. He bought me yellow wellies when I was five.”

“Maybe all dads buy yellow wellies.”

“Do they?”

“My dad bought me Spider-Man ones.”

“You cried.”

“I wanted princess ones.”

“You cried in Sainsbury’s.”

“I was six.”

“You lay on the floor.”

“I was committed.”

Leah laughed into the bottle.

Nina looked relieved.

After a while she said, “What did the letter say?”

“It was long.”

“Was it horrible?”

“Some bits.”

“You going to send it?”

“I don’t have his address.”

“You could message him.”

“He blocked Mum.”

“From your account.”

“He follows me.”

“Does he?”

“Since Christmas.”

“Has he messaged?”

“One time.”

“What did he put?”

“Happy Christmas, bug.”

Nina made a face.

“Bug?”

“He used to call me that.”

“Oh.”

Leah squashed the soda bottle slightly and it popped back.

“I didn’t answer.”

The whistle sounded again. Girls began shouting inside the sports hall.

Nina checked her phone.

“We’ve got eleven minutes before form.”

“I’m not doing PE.”

“Mrs. Carver might come looking.”

“She won’t.”

“She found Yasmin in the toilets last month.”

“Yasmin was smoking.”

“She wasn’t even smoking. Ella was smoking.”

“Yasmin always gets caught near things.”

Nina slid off the wall.

Leah stayed seated.

“You coming?”

“Where?”

“Library.”

“We can’t go library during PE.”

“Mrs. Benton never looks at the timetable. We’ll return books.”

“You don’t have books.”

“You can carry mine.”

Leah considered this.

“My face looks weird.”

“It looks normal.”

“I was crying before.”

“I can’t tell.”

“My nose goes red.”

“It’s always pink.”

Leah frowned at her.

Nina picked up both bags.

“Come on. I have geography to print and the printer at home makes that grinding noise.”

“You always leave it.”

“I forgot.”

“You had all weekend.”

“My cousins came.”

“That doesn’t take two days.”

“They stayed over.”

Leah stood.

They walked around the sports hall, keeping close to the brick wall until they reached the path.

After a few steps Leah said, “Could I show you the letter sometime?”

“Yeah.”

“Don’t do spelling.”

“I won’t.”

“I spelled definitely wrong about four times.”

“I spell it differently every time.”

“I think one of mine had an A.”

“That could be exciting.”

Leah looked at her.

Nina stared straight ahead.

Then Leah smiled a little and took her schoolbag back.

4. A Married Couple After an Affair

The hotel room was smaller than the website had made it look.

Adam stood by the window, opening and closing the curtains by six inches at a time. Claire was sitting on the edge of the bed in her coat.

“You can take that off.”

“I’m cold.”

“The radiator’s on.”

“It smells.”

“It’s an old radiator.”

“I can smell dust burning.”

Adam stopped touching the curtain.

“Do you want me to turn it off?”

“No. Leave it.”

There was a kettle on a tray beside two cups sealed in plastic. Adam picked up the laminated menu.

“They have soup.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“You didn’t eat on the train.”

“I had the crisps.”

“You had about six.”

“Seven or eight.”

He put the menu down.

Outside, rain streaked the glass. The sea was somewhere beyond the buildings, though neither of them could see it from the fourth floor.

Claire took off one boot.

Adam said, “Do you want the side by the bathroom?”

“I don’t care.”

“You usually take the bathroom side.”

“Then I’ll take it.”

She removed the other boot and lined them up carefully.

Adam sat in the desk chair.

“We could go home.”

“The last train’s at nine-forty.”

“We could get that.”

“We’ve paid for two nights.”

“I don’t mind losing the money.”

“I do.”

Adam rubbed his palms on his trousers.

Claire looked at him.

“Your hands are shaking.”

“A little.”

“Have you eaten?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“Sandwich at work.”

“At noon?”

“About one.”

She opened her handbag, found a packet of mints, put one in her mouth and held the packet toward him.

He shook his head.

She said, “How many times did you come here?”

Adam looked toward the window.

“Three.”

“To this hotel.”

“Yes.”

“With her.”

“Yes.”

Claire nodded.

“Same room?”

“No.”

“You booked it?”

“The first time.”

“And after that?”

“She did.”

Claire looked around at the patterned carpet, the bedside lamps, the framed photograph of fishing boats.

“Why this place?”

“It was far enough.”

“From London?”

“Yes.”

“You could have gone lots of places.”

“I found a deal.”

Claire swallowed the mint whole by accident and coughed.

Adam half stood.

“I’m fine.”

He sat back down.

She coughed once more, then drank water from the bottle on the bedside table.

“You found a deal.”

“It was January.”

“Was it raining?”

“I think so.”

“Did you see the sea?”

“Yes.”

“Together?”

“Yes.”

Claire took off her coat and placed it beside her.

Adam said, “We can choose somewhere else.”

“No.”

“I thought maybe being here was—”

“Stop.”

He stopped.

She pressed both hands against the mattress.

“I wanted to see it.”

“I understand.”

“I wanted the carpet and the curtains and the reception desk and whatever else there was. I’ve been looking at places in my head for five months. They kept changing.”

Adam said nothing.

“There was always a nice bar.”

“There’s a bar downstairs.”

“I saw.”

“It isn’t nice.”

Claire gave a small breath that might have been a laugh.

Adam looked at the menu again.

“There’s chowder.”

“You hate chowder.”

“I could have chips.”

“You’ll feel sick.”

“Probably.”

She stood and walked to the window. Adam moved his chair aside so she could pass.

She opened the curtain fully.

“All I can see is roofs.”

“The sea’s left.”

“Which left?”

He pointed.

“There. Past that white building.”

Claire leaned toward the glass.

“I can’t see it.”

“If you go down to the end of the road.”

“I don’t want to go out yet.”

“Okay.”

She kept looking.

After a minute she said, “Did she think you’d leave me?”

Adam’s mouth opened. He took a while.

“I think she hoped I might.”

“Did you tell her you would?”

“I talked about it.”

Claire nodded slowly.

“I need you to answer the questions when I ask them.”

“I’m trying.”

“I’m going to ask ugly ones.”

“All right.”

“I may ask the same ones in November.”

“All right.”

She turned toward him.

“I keep forgetting parts. Then something turns up again. The parking receipt. That restaurant charge. Her sister’s name. I remember them at the supermarket.”

Adam lowered his eyes.

“She had a sister called Joanne.”

“Yes.”

“She lives in Norwich.”

“Yes.”

“Two children.”

“Yes.”

Claire looked back outside.

“I don’t care about Joanne.”

Adam waited.

“I’ve spent three days thinking about Joanne.”

The room was warm now. The radiator ticked.

Adam got up and switched it off.

Claire said, “Thank you.”

He went back to the chair.

After a while she asked, “Was there champagne?”

“No.”

“Wine?”

“Once. Mostly beer.”

“You don’t like wine much.”

“No.”

Claire sat on the bed again.

“I pictured champagne.”

“There wasn’t any.”

“Good.”

Adam looked at her, unsure.

She pulled one of the pillows into her lap.

“I don’t know why that helps.”

He nodded.

“Don’t nod like that.”

He put his hands between his knees.

“All right.”

The radiator gave one last metallic click.

Claire said, “Order the chips.”

“And something for you?”

“Soup.”

“The chowder?”

“No. Tomato, if they have it.”

He checked the menu.

“They do.”

“Fine.”

Adam reached for the phone.

Claire lay back against the headboard, still holding the pillow.

“When it comes, I want to watch television.”

“Sure.”

“Something stupid.”

“I’ll find something.”

“And after that I’ll probably ask you more.”

Adam dialed reception.

Claire closed her eyes while he ordered.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

Lieutenant Herrera found Corporal Venn sitting on an ammunition crate with his helmet off.

The sun was coming up behind the ridge. It gave everything—the trucks, the wire, the scraped red earth—the appearance of having been painted flat.

“Helmet.”

Venn put it on.

“Strap.”

He fastened it.

Herrera stood over him.

“How long have you been here?”

“Since second watch.”

“You were relieved at zero-four.”

“Parker came late.”

“How late?”

“Twenty minutes.”

“And then?”

“I stayed.”

“Until now?”

“Yes, ma’am.”

Herrera looked at the empty observation post above them.

“Who’s up there?”

“Doyle.”

“When did Doyle go up?”

“Five-forty.”

“Alone?”

“Sergeant Mbeki’s on the radio.”

“Down here.”

“Yes.”

Herrera removed her sunglasses and wiped dust from one lens.

“Walk with me.”

Venn stood.

They crossed between two parked carriers. Men were beginning to move around the camp, quiet and stiff from sleep. Somebody had started a burner near the field kitchen. The smell of coffee traveled farther than it should have.

Herrera said, “You fired last night.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“How many?”

“Three.”

“Rifle?”

“Yes.”

“At what?”

“Movement on the east approach.”

“What kind?”

“A person.”

Herrera stopped.

Venn stopped too.

“How far?”

“Maybe one-eighty. Two hundred meters.”

“Inside the marker stones?”

“I couldn’t see the stones.”

“You have thermal.”

“The battery was dying.”

“You reported that?”

“Yesterday afternoon.”

“To whom?”

“Supply. Sergeant Mbeki knew.”

Herrera put her sunglasses back on.

“Keep going.”

They walked toward the command tent.

“Did the person fire?”

“No.”

“Carrying a weapon?”

“I saw something long.”

“What happened after you fired?”

“They dropped.”

“Then?”

“Got up. Ran back east.”

“All three rounds?”

“I fired two. Paused. Then one.”

Herrera glanced at him.

“Why the third?”

“I thought they were turning.”

“Toward you?”

“I couldn’t tell.”

A truck engine coughed and failed to start. Someone swore under it.

Herrera said, “Were you alone?”

“Parker was on the lower platform.”

“He says he was inside getting batteries.”

Venn stared ahead.

Herrera let several steps pass.

“How long was he inside?”

“I don’t know.”

“Roughly.”

“Maybe a minute.”

“And the shooting happened during that minute.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

They reached the shade beside the command tent. Herrera moved farther away from the entrance, where staff could hear.

“Did you see the person fall after the first rounds?”

“Yes.”

“Hard?”

“They went down.”

“Head, chest, legs?”

“I couldn’t see.”

“You waited how long?”

“Three seconds. Four.”

“Then the person moved.”

“Yes.”

“And you fired again.”

“Yes.”

Herrera folded her arms.

Venn looked exhausted. There was dried mud at his collar, though it had not rained in four days.

“Have you slept?”

“Some.”

“How much?”

“Two hours yesterday.”

“Before that.”

“Four, maybe.”

“Why were you still on second watch?”

“Parker’s sick.”

Herrera looked toward the medical tent.

“He’s sick?”

“He’s got stomach trouble.”

“He was cleared yesterday.”

“He keeps going off to the latrine.”

Herrera sighed.

Venn said, “Ma’am, there’s something else.”

She waited.

“I went out after.”

Her head lifted.

“How far?”

“Past the wire.”

“With who?”

“Nobody.”

“How far?”

“Maybe eighty meters.”

“Why?”

“I wanted to see.”

“The area was uncleared.”

“I stayed on the track.”

“The track was shelled three days ago.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

Herrera lowered her voice.

“What did you find?”

Venn reached into his vest and produced a piece of cloth folded twice.

Herrera didn’t take it at first.

“What is that?”

“It was caught on a thorn bush.”

She accepted it.

Blue cotton. Small white flowers. One edge was torn.

“There was blood?”

“On the ground. A little.”

“Footprints?”

“Yes.”

“Boots?”

Venn hesitated.

“Sandals.”

Herrera looked at him.

“Adult?”

“Small.”

“How small?”

“I can’t tell.”

“Did you follow them?”

“No.”

She folded the cloth again.

Venn rubbed his thumb against the seam of his trousers.

“I thought it was a rifle.”

Herrera put the cloth in her pocket.

“Go to medical.”

“I’m fit for duty.”

“You’re coming off the roster until I finish the report.”

“Am I confined?”

“Stay inside camp. Eat something. Sleep where someone can find you.”

Venn swallowed.

“Am I in trouble?”

Herrera looked toward the ridge. The sun had cleared it now.

“There will be an inquiry.”

He stood very still.

She added, “Parker and Mbeki will be questioned. So will I. The weapon log gets sealed now.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“You don’t clean your rifle.”

“I cleaned it after watch.”

Herrera shut her eyes briefly.

“How thoroughly?”

“Normal field clean.”

“Did anyone tell you to?”

“No.”

“All right. Take it to the armorer and leave it there. Tell him I ordered it.”

Venn nodded.

“Ma’am.”

“What?”

“I really thought—”

Herrera raised a hand, not sharply.

“Get some food, Corporal.”

He looked at the hand, then at her face.

“Yes, ma’am.”

He walked away.

Herrera remained beside the tent. After a moment, she took the blue cloth from her pocket and unfolded it again, as though a second look might make it larger.

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The laundromat had twenty-four machines and only two customers.

Tess knew the man by sight. He lived somewhere above the pharmacy, or perhaps above the vacant shop next to it. She had seen him carrying a cello case once and assumed things from there.

Tonight he had no cello. He had three black bin bags.

One of the dryers began making a noise like a shoe trapped inside it.

The man looked up from his phone.

“That yours?”

Tess glanced over.

“Probably.”

“You put shoes in?”

“No.”

The dryer thumped again.

“I’ve got a belt in there.”

“Could be that.”

“It has a big buckle.”

He nodded.

Tess went to the dryer and pressed her palm against the glass.

“Can you open these while they’re running?”

“I think so.”

She pulled the handle. The machine stopped.

A pair of jeans fell forward. The belt buckle struck the drum with a metallic clang.

“There.”

She unthreaded the belt through the belt loops.

The man said, “That would’ve gone for forty minutes.”

“I paid for forty minutes.”

“You can start it again.”

“I hope so.”

She shut the door and pressed the button. The dryer resumed turning.

“Good.”

“Thank you.”

“I contributed almost nothing.”

Tess went back to her plastic chair.

The man returned to his phone.

Three minutes passed.

Then he said, “Do you know if that vending machine takes cards?”

Tess looked at the machine selling detergent.

“I’ve only used coins.”

“I have no coins.”

“There’s a cash machine outside.”

“It charges two pounds.”

“For twenty pounds?”

“For the privilege of getting my own money.”

Tess opened her purse.

“I’ve got change.”

“I can transfer it.”

“How much do you need?”

“One detergent thing.”

“They’re one-fifty.”

She counted out coins and held them toward him.

He came over.

“I can send you one-fifty.”

“It’s fine.”

“I’m using three machines.”

“It’s still one-fifty.”

He accepted the coins. “Thanks.”

He bought a box of detergent and tore it open at the first washing machine.

Tess watched him pour half the box into one compartment and half into another.

“I think you only need one side.”

He looked at the drawer.

“Which side?”

“The big one.”

“They’re both big.”

She got up.

There were indeed two large compartments and one narrow compartment marked with a flower.

“I use that one.”

“You sound uncertain.”

“My machine at home has words.”

“Mine has stopped draining.”

“That’s why you’ve got three bin bags?”

He glanced at them.

“Flood.”

“Oh.”

“Small flood.”

“Upstairs?”

“Third floor.”

“That seems bad.”

“It stayed mostly in the bathroom.”

Tess leaned against the washer.

“Pipe?”

“My neighbour’s washing machine. Her hose came off.”

“Did it go through your ceiling?”

“Through the light fitting.”

She made a face.

“Yes,” he said. “That was more or less my face.”

“Was the light on?”

“No. I was asleep.”

“That’s better.”

“I woke up because water hit the bed.”

Tess looked at the bin bags again.

“All clothes?”

“Bedding. Towels. Clothes. Some books got wet.”

“That’s awful.”

“Mostly sheet music.”

“So you are the cello person.”

He looked surprised.

“You’ve seen me?”

“I live on Kerr Street.”

“Yellow door?”

“Blue.”

“There are several blue doors.”

“Mine has a dead lavender plant.”

“I may have seen that.”

“It was alive when I bought it.”

He loaded the detergent and started the first machine.

“My name’s Arun.”

“Tess.”

They did not shake hands. He sat two chairs away.

The dryers spun behind them.

Tess said, “Did your cello get wet?”

“No. It was at my girlfriend’s.”

“That was lucky.”

“She broke up with me this afternoon.”

Tess looked at him.

Arun rubbed his jaw.

“Sorry. That came out rather abruptly.”

“You’ve had a strange day.”

“Fairly.”

“Was it before the flood?”

“After.”

“That’s a crowded schedule.”

He laughed once and looked down at his phone.

Tess returned to watching her dryer.

After a while she said, “Do you want a biscuit?”

He turned.

“I bought a packet at work. They’re terrible.”

“What kind?”

“Those healthy oat ones.”

“I like those.”

“Then they may not be terrible.”

She took the packet from her bag and held it out.

He took one.

They ate in silence.

A bus went past outside, throwing pale light across the laundromat windows.

Arun said, “Why are you here?”

“My boiler leaked into the airing cupboard.”

“Today?”

“Sunday. I’ve been pretending the towels would dry on their own.”

“How many?”

“Eleven.”

“That is a large towel population.”

“My sister brought her children swimming.”

“Ah.”

“She left all their wet things in a heap and then the boiler joined in.”

Arun nodded, eating the last corner of biscuit.

“My girlfriend has six towels.”

“Had.”

He stared at the biscuit.

Tess waited.

Then he smiled despite himself.

“I mean, presumably she still has them. They’re hers.”

Tess looked away toward the machines.

“My dryer says eighteen minutes.”

“My wash says thirty-six.”

“There’s a café across the road.”

“It’s closed.”

“The petrol station has coffee.”

“Is it drinkable?”

“No.”

They sat for another minute.

Arun said, “I might get one anyway.”

“I’ll come. I need more coins for the dryer.”

“I can use the cash machine.”

“You’ll lose two pounds.”

“My financial position has changed since the detergent.”

Tess stood and picked up her coat.

“Fine. You can waste your money.”

He checked the three washing machines before following her.

At the door, he stopped.

“Do people steal washing from these places?”

“I have no idea.”

“All my trousers are in there.”

“I don’t think anyone’s coming in for your trousers.”

“That’s reassuring.”

She held the door open.

Cold air swept through the laundromat.

Arun looked once more at the turning machines, then went outside with her.
