
Scene 1: A mother and her adult son

The apartment smells like tape and last night’s soup. Boxes lean against the wall, labeled in thick black marker. The mother folds shirts badly and talks without looking up.

Mother: That box is too heavy. The corners will give out. Put the books in smaller ones. Your back, Adrian. You carry everything on your back.

Son: I’ll split them. There are only two more stacks.

Mother: Take the mixer. Don’t argue. Your grandmother used it until it rattled like a train. It still works if you hold the bowl with your hip.

Son: I don’t bake.

Mother: People bake when they have people over. You’ll have people over. Your new place has a balcony. People come for balconies.

Son: The balcony faces an alley.

Mother: Pigeons are people when they stare. They’ll keep you company. Where’s the tape?

Son: Under the guidebooks.

Mother: You’re taking those? Lonely Planet can be lonely.

Son: They’re more for daydreaming than for trips.

Mother: Daydreaming needs shelves. Everything else goes in the closet. Are you taking the coat rack? It’s crooked but it holds.

Son: There’s a built-in by the door. I measured it.

Mother: Oh, you measured. Good. Your father never measured, he would stick a nail and pretend the wall moved. He left holes behind him like breadcrumbs. Here, wrap this mug. It’s the one with the chip. Don’t give it to guests. Keep it for nights when the dishes don’t matter.

Son: I’ll put it with the kitchen stuff.

Mother: Kitchen stuff. You say that and it sounds like a life. You’ll need the colander, too. Pasta keeps a person upright. And that glass baking dish with the lid. If you cut lasagna in it, don’t scrape it with a knife. Use the plastic thing.

Son: You can keep the dish. I’m probably going to live on eggs and whatever fits in a pan.

Mother: Eggs fit everywhere. Keep the dish. It’s square. Square things are easy to stack. And the watch—where’s the watch? The one your father wore when he still wore a tie.

Son: In the top drawer of the console. I saw it yesterday.

Mother: Don’t let it go to some box in a storage unit. It ticks even when you think it stopped. He wound it for years while reading the sports page. I would hear that click-click early on Sundays. It made me think he would live forever.

Son: I’ll take it.

Mother: Take also the little cactus. It has opinions, you can tell by the way it leans. Plants look at you. They don’t talk, but they make faces. They survive when you forget them, and then you forgive yourself. That helps.

Son: You can keep the cactus. It likes the window here.

Mother: I can visit it then. With groceries. I’ll bring tomatoes. The ones from the market woman with the scarf. She calls me dear and gives me the ugly ones, which are better. I’ll fill your fridge without you noticing. You’ll come home and think you left yourself gifts.

Son: You don’t have to do that.

Mother: I know. I’ll do it anyway. Tell me the number again. Your street is named after a poet. Poets are always far from the laundry.

Son: It’s 14, third floor, right-hand staircase.

Mother: Right-hand, good. Left-hand stairs creak more. This is true. Ask any building. Do you have sheets? I won’t let you sleep on a bare mattress like a traveler who lost his luggage.

Son: I have sheets. Blue ones. They came in a set.

Mother: Sets lie. They pretend to match your life. Take the spare blanket too, the thick one. Winters are longer in new places. Also, write to your aunt. She pretends she doesn’t mind, but she puts stamps in a tin for when you write back.

Son: I’ll text her.

Mother: She has a phone that sings church bells at noon and gets angry when it learns something. Call her. She will describe the cat’s nap in ten minutes. It will calm you. Are you nervous?

Son: A little.

Mother: Good. Nerves make you look both ways. You’ll be fine. I’m making soup tonight. Do you have time?

Son: I can stay for soup.

Mother: Then put down the tape and come sit for a moment. We’ll look at the balcony on the screen. We can say bad things about the alley and then like it anyway. That’s how people make homes.


Scene 2: Two old diplomats

On a bench outside a conference center, two men sit with coffee in paper cups. Security badges swing on their chests, but neither of them looks like he can be hurried anymore.

Boris: They changed the hallway carpet. It used to be that green wave pattern. I could walk it with my eyes shut.

Henri: You walked it with your eyes open and still missed the French press room twice. You were chasing a man with a red tie. I thought you’d adopted him.

Boris: He had the only decent pen in the building. A Montblanc with a nib that didn’t scratch. I considered it an instrument of state.

Henri: Instruments of state now have touchscreens. My thumb knows more passwords than my memory knows poems.

Boris: Do you remember the hotel in Skopje? The one with the heavy curtains. We drew them at noon and called it strategy.

Henri: We called everything strategy. It made the waiting easier. What was that thing with the sugar cubes—your trick with the tea?

Boris: Not a trick. A signal. Two sugar cubes meant we were willing to move on fisheries if they moved on road access. I had to drink very sweet tea that month. It worked once and then everyone started copying signals until all the tea tasted like guesses.

Henri: I used to watch their shoes. The man from the north tapped his heel when he was bored, the woman from the west left her laces untied when she was about to give in. People bring all their village habits into grand rooms.

Boris: This new hall, it has glass everywhere. No corners. It makes me feel exposed. In the old place you could lean against a column and think of your grandmother’s porch.

Henri: They’re afraid of smoke-filled rooms. As if air made promises. It was the ashtrays that did the work. A man would reach for a match and find an excuse to say something sideways.

Boris: Speaking of sideways, there’s a reception tonight. I saw oysters on a cart. I’ll need a fork this time. My hands tremble with shellfish.

Henri: Your hands tremble when you tell the truth. It adds drama. Do you have a line prepared for the minister from Sofia?

Boris: I plan to ask after his mother’s hearing. Last time he said she pretends not to hear certain people. That could apply to all of us.

Henri: I might mention to the envoy how my grandson’s class drew a map with no borders. He took it home with an angry mouth. Children draw what they’re given. Then we take away their crayons and give them stamps.

Boris: You become poetic when you’re tired. Are you sleeping?

Henri: I wake at three. The cat thinks I’m up for her and is grateful. We sit and regard each other. She has solved all borders in my living room.

Boris: I miss my wife’s stew in these months. The hotel buffets make everything brown. Even the salad looks boiled.

Henri: The chef downstairs knows my weakness for apricot tarts. He slices one too early and apologizes. I forgive him with my face and return for seconds.

Boris: We’ll make our rounds and find chairs by the ficus. We can stage an attack of nostalgia and pretend we were important.

Henri: We delayed certain things. That counts for something. A delay is a kind of bridge. People cross and forget they needed it.

Boris: Then we’ll toast to delays. And to sugar cubes. And to the green wave carpet that told us where to go.

Henri: To the man with the red tie. I hope he kept the pen and wrote something that wasn’t a communique. Maybe a recipe. Those travel further.


Scene 3: Two schoolgirls

The bus stop sign leans. A gull picks at a chip packet. Two girls with backpacks drop onto the bench and stretch their feet in the dust.

Maya: I think I failed bio. My brain looked at the diagram and went, nope, those squiggles are noodles.

Zara: You always say that and then you get a B. The mitochondria one, did you remember the thing about energy?

Maya: I wrote “powerhouse” and then drew a tiny lightning bolt because my hand wanted to. Miss Lopez hates it when we get cute. She looks like someone stole her sandwich.

Zara: She eats yogurt with seeds. No one can be cheerful with seeds. Did you see Talia’s shoes? They’re like dentist gloves. So white. I want them but I also want to jump in a puddle and scream.

Maya: I saw her mom at the bakery. She buys the long bread and makes it look like a sword. Talia said she was grounded for watching TV past midnight and then, at lunch, she showed me a video of raccoons in a kitchen. I watched it three times. The raccoons opened drawers like tiny burglars.

Zara: My cousin’s hamster escaped and hid in the teapot for two days. We thought it went to heaven. Then it blinked at the sugar and my aunt cried. The hamster looked guilty. Can hamsters feel shame?

Maya: They feel everything. They roll their eyes when you talk too loud. I had a goldfish that watched me do math. Every time I sighed it made a bubble like “same.”

Zara: Ugh, math. Mr. Bray wrote the problem and then he erased the one easy step because he said we leaned on it too much. He wants us to be independent like chairs with three legs.

Maya: He loves that marker smell. Maybe he eats it for breakfast. Okay, I have to tell you something. Don’t laugh. No, you’re going to laugh.

Zara: I can put my hand over my mouth. It’s an ancient technique.

Maya: Liam sent me a message that said, “cool shoes.” I wore my old ones on purpose because of the rain. And then he said he liked the scribbles on the side. It was just pen. I drew a star because my pen leaked.

Zara: That’s sweet. He noticed. He notices things like who takes the last donut hole. He pretends he’s a raccoon.

Maya: I stared at the screen for an hour. What do you say? “Thanks”? It felt like I was replying to a cloud.

Zara: Say, “thanks, I was bored in math.” Then send a picture of your notebook with the tiny planets. He likes space. He drew a rocket that looked like a carrot but still.

Maya: A carrot rocket could go to Mars. My mom said carrots make your eyes good but I still can’t see row five. I keep borrowing your notes. Your handwriting is like tiny buildings. Mine looks like worms got tired.

Zara: The bus is late again. We could walk and get slushies. My brain wants blue ice. It stains your tongue and your soul.

Maya: I can’t, I promised to pick up my brother. He’s sticky. He gets stickier every day. He found a jar of honey and declared himself a bear.

Zara: Bears are these little dictators in houses. My sister said she’s too old for stickers and then she covered her laptop in sushi faces. People lie.

Maya: I told my mom I want to dye my hair and she said, at least wait until the wedding photos are taken. Which wedding? We don’t have one. She plans in grids in her head.

Zara: Oh, the bus. Finally. If the driver plays the radio again, I’m dancing. Tiny seat dance. My legs need to wiggle or I’ll explode into raccoons.

Maya: I’ll save you a place. If you explode, I’ll feed your raccoons grapes and tell them about math. They’ll make bubbles and understand me.


Scene 4: A married couple after an affair

The kitchen window shows a dark yard and one porch light two houses down. The dishwasher hums. They sit with mugs, not making eye contact for long.

Nina: The kids left the cereal box open again. It gets soft so fast.

Mark: I put a clip on it in the morning. Maybe they had a second breakfast. They’ve been doing that lately.

Nina: They say they’re starving after practice. I used to be that way. I would come home and eat toast in the hallway without taking off my coat.

Mark: We should buy more milk. If we’re making lists. I used the last of it for coffee and pretended I didn’t.

Nina: There’s a half gallon behind the juice. You always forget the back of the fridge. Things live there and form societies.

Mark: I stood in the parking lot today with my phone in my hand and I didn’t know who to call. That was in front of the hardware store, with the lilac bush that’s dying.

Nina: I drove past the lake. I thought about April last year and the wind and how you said you couldn’t feel your fingers. I kept driving until the road narrowed and I had to turn around because a dog lay in the middle like a rock.

Mark: I can fix the faucet this weekend. The drip is making me count. It goes like, patient, patient, impatient. I hear it when I’m trying to read to Lucy.

Nina: She asks for the book with the ducks and then looks at the ceiling the whole time. I think she listens with her shoulder.

Mark: She asked me tonight if I ever slept at the office. I said I worked late sometimes. She said the office looks small in her head.

Nina: I told her you’re better in the morning than at night. That’s true. You move differently.

Mark: I know you went to see your sister. She sent me a text with a picture of the pie. She bakes when she doesn’t know what else to do.

Nina: We didn’t even eat it. We looked at it. I said I would bring you a slice and then I drove home with my hands at ten and two like a movie driver. The pie is in the fridge in a tin. Take it if you want.

Mark: I don’t know what to do with my hands at night. They remember things. I keep folding them and unfolding them like maps.

Nina: I’m tired of crying in the bathroom, and then I dry my eyes on the nice towels and feel bad about the towels. It’s like the towels are witnesses.

Mark: Should I sleep in the guest room again?

Nina: You can. Or not. The bed is big and it feels like a boat sometimes and I roll to the middle without looking where I’m going.

Mark: I can take the couch. It makes my back talk in the morning but maybe that’s fine. Talking backs are still backs.

Nina: Do you think the neighbors know? She waves slower now. Or I’m making that up. I keep seeing everything as a sign because ordinary is unbearable.

Mark: They’re busy with their own messes. His truck muffler fell off, remember? He strapped it with rope. People drive around with things like that, hoping the noise will drown out something else.

Nina: We should tell the kids something that doesn’t make them carry it. I don’t have a sentence yet. I keep starting and it turns into weather.

Mark: I could say I hurt you and we’re trying. That’s true. I’m trying. It’s not the kind of trying you put on a calendar.

Nina: Don’t tell them tonight. Lucy’s got her spelling list tomorrow, and Owen has his game. Let them have their regular Thursday.

Mark: Okay. I’ll take Owen to the game. The orange slices are in the crisper. He likes too much salt on them.

Nina: He’ll make that face like he bit electricity and then he’ll go back for more. Put a napkin under them. The cooler leaks.

Mark: I’ll fix the cooler. I’ll fix the faucet. I can fix anything that is a thing.

Nina: The pie tin will clatter when you open the fridge. Don’t startle the cat. He’s been sleeping in the laundry like a pillow.

Mark: Do you want a blanket? It’s colder tonight. The vent in here always forgets this corner.

Nina: I’m fine. I’m going to sit for a while longer. The hum helps. It’s like a sound that isn’t asking.

Mark: I’ll do the garbage. Then I’ll leave the light over the sink on. It’s less dark that way.


Scene 5: An officer and a subordinate

In a prefab office by the motor pool, maps are pinned crookedly to a corkboard. A generator hums outside. An officer stands by a metal desk; a sergeant lingers by the door.

Captain Reyes: Close it. The wind keeps stealing the papers. It acts like it’s on our payroll.

Sergeant Patel: Sorry, sir. The latch sticks. I have to lift it and push. There. It’ll pop again in ten minutes.

Captain Reyes: Then we’ll have ten quiet minutes. Sit down. I read your incident report. Tell me what happened with truck three.

Sergeant Patel: Short version? It stalled on the north road after the bridge. The light flickered and died. We coasted to the shoulder. We set triangles, radioed in, and waited for the wrecker. Private Haufner was on the wheel. He kept calm.

Captain Reyes: And the cargo?

Sergeant Patel: All accounted for. We didn’t open anything. We checked seals. The NGO rep signed the transfer after. They were grateful we still made it. They had people waiting.

Captain Reyes: You wrote there was shouting.

Sergeant Patel: Yeah. Local guy in a red jacket wanted to cut through the perimeter while we were setting triangles. He said his mother was at the clinic and he needed to get her. I told him to go around the long way because of the truck. He didn’t like the long way. He threw a bottle at the ditch. It didn’t hit anyone. Corporal Ames kept him back with his arm out.

Captain Reyes: Did Ames escalate?

Sergeant Patel: He spoke steady. The red jacket guy got tired of being angry at us and stomped off. He called us names I’ve heard before. Kids do better insults, honestly.

Captain Reyes: The mechanic says the alternator is dying across the fleet. He wants parts we don’t have funds for. He says we should ground them.

Sergeant Patel: If we ground them, the clinic misses the deliveries. If we don’t, we push our luck with hills. The hills don’t care about our budgets.

Captain Reyes: I have a meeting at fourteen hundred about budgets. It will feature charts. I need a list from you. Which routes can we do on foot if it comes to that? Which don’t make sense at all if we can’t drive?

Sergeant Patel: On foot we can manage the market loop and the school drop, but not the northern villages. That’s two days with a pack and a prayer. We’d need a relay. And carts. And patience from people who ran out of patience last winter.

Captain Reyes: Write it down. Also, Ames. He’s green but he has a spine. Put him with Sergeant Boyd this week. Boyd’s good at teaching without turning it into a sermon. Keep Haufner on the wheel but switch him to truck five. It coughs, but it coughs predictably.

Sergeant Patel: Thank you, sir. About the rumor—people are saying we’re pulling out. The baker’s wife asked me. I told her I don’t make those decisions, but she looked past me like I was already gone.

Captain Reyes: Rumors are weather. We operate in them. Until someone above us says move, we stay. When they say move, we move with purpose. That’s the only way I know to do it and sleep at night.

Sergeant Patel: Understood. Oh, and the generator by the school keeps stalling. The teacher sent a note with glitter on it. I think a kindergartner helped.

Captain Reyes: I’ll add it to the list. Bring me that list in an hour. And talk to your squad about batteries. If we’re walking more, headlamps become important. Tell them I don’t want heroes at dusk. I want all heads accounted for at chow.

Sergeant Patel: Yes, sir. You want me to speak to the NGO rep again? She was asking for a schedule update, and she speaks like time is a person she has to convince.

Captain Reyes: Tell her we’ll give her windows, not exact minutes. Roads slide under our feet. Be polite. Be firm. Then get a sandwich. You look like you forgot what lunch is.

Sergeant Patel: I did. The line was long and then the radio squawked. I’ll grab whatever’s left. Might be peaches in cans. The new kid can open them without bleeding now. Progress.

Captain Reyes: Go. Bring me that list. Lift the door latch. Fight the wind for me on your way out.

Sergeant Patel: Roger. See you in an hour, sir.


Scene 6: Two people who barely know each other

A laundromat on a Tuesday night. The machines hum, a TV murmurs a game. Two people sit on plastic chairs with baskets at their feet, pretending not to watch their clothes tumble.

Eli: Yours is the only dryer with that squeak. It sounds like a cricket got a promotion.

June: I picked it because it’s near the fan. I always get too warm in here. The air feels like it has soup in it.

Eli: I bring a book and then I reread the same sentence. The machines hypnotize me. They’re like round campfires.

June: What are you reading?

Eli: A detective thing. I keep losing the thread because the author loves the weather. Every chapter has rain. I begin to suspect the murderer is a cloud.

June: I like clouds that have flat bottoms. They look like they have rules. I’m June, by the way. I think I’ve seen you with the big red basket.

Eli: Yeah. Eli. The basket was my grandmother’s. She kept yarn in it. It creaks like a ship when I carry it up the stairs.

June: I always forget quarters and then I pay with wrinkled bills and get too many. Today I have exactly the right amount, so I’m probably going to lose my keys to balance it out.

Eli: The change machine eats twenties for sport. One time it spit everything out and the coins rolled under the soda fridge. I had to bribe a toddler with a sticker to lie down and grab them.

June: That’s a useful skill. My neighbor’s kid can get into spaces like smoke. He shows up under the couch and declares it a fort.

Eli: Do you live nearby?

June: Around the corner, above the salon with the neon scissors. My room gets bright pink at night. It makes all my plants blush. You?

Eli: On Maple, the brick building with the tilting steps. The landlord says the tilt has character. I say my ankles disagree.

June: Characters and ankles have different priorities. Do you cook in that kitchen? I can’t boil water in mine without setting off the alarm. It’s dramatic.

Eli: I make noodles and eggs. I keep promising myself vegetables, and then I eat cereal over the sink. It feels like camping but less exciting.

June: Cereal over the sink is a life stage. I had that when I first moved here. Now I make stew sometimes, and it lasts three days, and on day three I feel like a pioneer who discovered lunch.

Eli: Your dryer’s cricket got louder. Maybe it’s cheering.

June: My sweater is probably tangled with a button and making a tiny orchestra. Do you work late? You’re here at this ridiculous hour.

Eli: I do nights at the bookstore on King. We close at ten and then I stack the signed copies so the morning people don’t panic. We had an author today with a dog. The dog wore a bandana and sighed at the romance section.

June: I pass that store on my way to the bus. The window has the paper birds right now. They look like they’re arguing quietly. I work at the clinic. Front desk. I’ve learned to pronounce all the different ways people spell Caitlin.

Eli: That’s a talent. Names are hard. People get attached to their letters.

June: Your washer beeped. The one with the dinosaur sticker. Someone put that on there at Christmas and nobody took it off.

Eli: Right. Thanks. I’ll switch it. Do you need help carrying anything?

June: I’m good. Oh—my sock always disappears under the machines. If you see a yellow one escaping, catch it. It has little suns.

Eli: I’ll keep watch. I feel invested now. Squeaks, socks, dinosaurs. It’s a whole ecosystem.

June: If I don’t come here next week, it’s because the salon woman convinced me to dye my hair, and I’ll be too embarrassed to leave the house for two days.

Eli: I’ll be here with the cricket either way. Same time, probably. The bookstore keeps me married to Tuesdays.

June: Then I’ll bring a better book recommendation. Something with fewer clouds. Or more, if you want to finish the weather mystery.

Eli: Surprise me. I trust your cloud standards. Your machine agrees. It stopped squeaking. Maybe it’s listening.
