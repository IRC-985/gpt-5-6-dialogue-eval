
**Scene 1: A Mother and Her Adult Son**

MOTHER:  
You left your jacket on the banister again. Just now, when I was coming upstairs.

SON:  
Yeah, I know. I meant to take it but I had my hands full. I’ll grab it later.

MOTHER:  
It’s going to fall. Someone’s going to trip. But anyway, I put the soup in Tupperware like you wanted.

SON:  
Thanks, Mom. I’ll bring those back next time. Leo’s been eating everything in sight. He says you make better soup than restaurants.

MOTHER:  
He’s very polite. Last time you both came, he hardly took anything at all. I was watching.

SON:  
He’s not good with new kitchens. Gets shy about other people’s stuff, I guess.

MOTHER:  
Your father was like that at first. But then he learned to open cupboards.

SON:  
He still misplaces the plates. Last time he put the blue bowl on top of the mugs.

MOTHER:  
I noticed. I’m not sure anything will change now. He keeps talking about getting a different car. I told him the problem’s the driver, not the car.

SON:  
He does like to tinker. Remember when he tried fixing the toaster? It would’ve been easier just to buy a new one.

MOTHER:  
He likes a project. He’s always needed something to work on. It drives me crazy sometimes, but at least he doesn’t just sit around. Leo's father gambled. Did you know that? He never mentioned it but I could tell. There was a nervousness.

SON:  
No, he’s never said. He doesn’t talk much about his family. Yesterday he asked when you’re coming over for dinner. 

MOTHER:  
I never know what to bring. Not everyone likes the same things, you know.

SON:  
You don’t have to bring anything. He just wants you to come.

MOTHER:  
There’s always something you’re not saying. When you were little, you kept your backpack zipped up so tight, it took forever to get your lunch out.

SON:  
I’m bringing the jacket next time. You can check the pockets if you want.

MOTHER:  
I know you won’t. But I’ll make more soup. Something different next time—maybe lentil. 

SON:  
I’ll tell Leo. He’ll say thank you, probably twice.

MOTHER:  
You know you can come by more often. Even if you just sit in the kitchen and do nothing. I don’t mind that. 

SON:  
It’s not so quiet at the apartment. I like coming here sometimes. For the quiet.

MOTHER:  
You could stay for dinner. There’s enough stew left.

SON:  
Maybe just for a little bit. I’ve got work to do later. But I’ll sit in the kitchen awhile.

**Scene 2: Two Old Diplomats**

LARSEN:  
Herman, do you remember the reception in Vienna? There was a man with a red handkerchief, kept refilling his glass, never said a word.

HERMAN:  
Vaguely. That was—must be almost twenty years ago. Before the treaty, certainly. Maybe before the sanctions.

LARSEN:  
He’s a minister now. Shows up at every summit, saying nothing and watching everyone else.

HERMAN:  
Those are the ones who get promoted. The silent ones. We should have kept our mouths shut more often.

LARSEN:  
We liked the talk too much. You and me both. Sometimes I wonder what difference it made, all those late nights and draft revisions.

HERMAN:  
When I can’t sleep, I try to remember the exact order of the signatures on the treaty. Meyer was third, wasn’t he? Or was it Andreiev?

LARSEN:  
I have the photo at home. Everyone grim-faced, except you. You’re smiling, standing off to the side. Why were you smiling?

HERMAN:  
Probably thinking it wouldn’t last. Most things don’t. But the room was warm and I’d had too much port.

LARSEN:  
That was the summer the air conditioners stopped working. The entire delegation sweating through their shirts. We joked about replacing water pitchers with fans.

HERMAN:  
If only they’d listened. My wife still asks about Vienna. She liked the pastry shop downstairs. Sachertorte, every morning.

LARSEN:  
Mine couldn’t bear the city. Too noisy, she said. She preferred Stockholm. Rooms with thick carpets and doors that closed quietly.

HERMAN:  
I miss those doors. You always knew who wanted to talk business—how loud they shut the door told you.

LARSEN:  
We could have used thicker doors in Berlin. Every whisper seemed to carry.

HERMAN:  
Somebody always listening. What do you suppose the young ones think of us now? The ones with bright ideas and laptops.

LARSEN:  
They think we’re slow. They think everything should be faster, cleaner. They don’t believe in paper, or patience.

HERMAN:  
There are worse things than time. There are worse things than too many drafts. At least you can throw paper away. Emails hang around forever.

LARSEN:  
Nobody sends me emails anymore. Every now and then someone calls, but they’re usually selling something.

HERMAN:  
You could come by for chess one evening. My wife will make tea.

LARSEN:  
I’d like that. I still have cigars from Lisbon. We could pretend they’re contraband.

HERMAN:  
We always did. Even when they were legal.

**Scene 3: Two Schoolgirls**

LILA:  
It started during gym, I think. I saw her put her phone behind her backpack, but Mrs. Olsen didn’t see. I don’t know how she does it.

TAMSIN:  
Maddie’s always getting away with stuff. My mom says she’s clever, but it’s not just that. She acts like teachers aren’t even real.

LILA:  
I guess if I tried that, I’d get caught on the first try. My brother did once, and he had to write an essay.

TAMSIN:  
Maddie talks about college like she’s leaving next week. She already has a list. I’ve never even seen her do homework.

LILA:  
She’s just good at pretending things are easy. Don’t you want to do something easy for once? Like skip after lunch. It doesn’t even have to be for long. Just go behind the gym and sit in the sun.

TAMSIN:  
If I did, I’d get called right away. My mom’s always looking at the attendance. She gets a text or something, and then I get the look.

LILA:  
What if you said you had a stomachache? Sometimes it works.

TAMSIN:  
Only if I look sick. Last time I tried, Mom said put your feet up, then made me finish multiplication tables. She actually brought them in on a tray.

LILA:  
Your mom is wild. Mine just says take a nap. If I actually fall asleep, she wakes me with chores.

TAMSIN:  
Sometimes I do want to just leave. Just walk out and keep going. I saw some geese flying over this morning and it looked like they had somewhere better.

LILA:  
If I was a goose, I’d fly really slow and land behind 7-Eleven. People would throw me bread every day.

TAMSIN:  
If I was a goose, I’d knock on Maddie’s window and scare her so much she’d drop her phone for once. Or poop on her math homework.

LILA:  
Imagine her face. Sometimes I wonder what it feels like to not care about stuff, or to act like nothing’s a big deal.

TAMSIN:  
You do weird things when you’re nervous. You tap your pencil like a secret code.

LILA:  
You always fix your sleeves when you’re thinking. Like you’re rolling up for a test, even if it’s just lunch.

TAMSIN:  
All geese should get free lunch. Someone should make a petition.

LILA:  
Let’s sign it at lunch. And tell Maddie to watch out for birds.

**Scene 4: A Married Couple After an Affair**

MARK:  
I made coffee. There’s more if you want it. I thought you’d be up earlier today.

EVE:  
Thanks. I was awake. I just didn’t come down. Didn’t feel like rushing.

MARK:  
You don’t have to. Not on Saturdays. I can go to the store for groceries. You don’t need to make a list.

EVE:  
I should anyway. There’s nothing in the fridge. But I couldn’t sleep, so I kept thinking about what I forgot to say yesterday.

MARK:  
You said what you needed to. I don’t think we need to talk about it more, unless you want to. I just forgot how quiet it gets in here.

EVE:  
I keep hearing the clock, loud as anything. I hate that clock. But I never take it down.

MARK:  
We’ve had it since Chicago. We kept it because you liked the way it looked, didn’t you?

EVE:  
I liked the idea of it. Not the sound.

MARK:  
I slept on the couch. You probably heard me. It creaks every time you turn over.

EVE:  
I heard. I didn’t mind. It’s not like either of us was sleeping well. I think the neighbor’s dog barked at four.

MARK:  
I never know what to say in the morning. I keep carrying the same sentences around, like leftovers no one wants.

EVE:  
Sometimes it’d be nice if we could just list things. Not try to fix it, just say facts. You slept on the couch, I didn’t cook dinner. The clock’s loud.

MARK:  
It’s not much, but I’ll go to the store. Maybe if I bring back something different, it’ll help. I was thinking oranges.

EVE:  
Oranges are good. I’ll try to get started on laundry.

MARK:  
If there’s anything else, write it down. I’ll keep checking the fridge if I forget. 

EVE:  
I know. You always do that. 

MARK:  
I’ll be back after lunch. If you need me for anything, just call. I’ll keep my phone on.

EVE:  
I will. I’ll be here. There’s nothing else I want to do.

MARK:  
I’ll get oranges.

**Scene 5: An Officer and a Subordinate**

CAPTAIN RAMIREZ:  
Jenkins, you’re fifteen minutes late. That’s two days this week. Anything going on I should know about?

JENKINS:  
Sorry, Captain. I got caught up at the checkpoint, then there was a jam at the main gate. I should have left earlier.

RAMIREZ:  
You’re not the only one who uses that route. The others still make it on time. You want to try for traffic control next rotation?

JENKINS:  
No, sir. I don’t think that’s my thing.

RAMIREZ:  
I didn’t think so. You were supposed to file your report on the shelter sweep. I didn’t find it in the folder last night.

JENKINS:  
It’s mostly done. I got held up getting signatures. Someone wasn’t at their station after shift change.

RAMIREZ:  
Should have called up the watch desk. Somebody always picks up.

JENKINS:  
I know, sir. I’ll finish it before set-up this afternoon.

RAMIREZ:  
Don’t make it a habit. If you’re going to be late or you need something, tell me ahead of time. These mornings are tight already.

JENKINS:  
That was my mistake. I let things pile up. Won’t happen again.

RAMIREZ:  
You’re not like the usual rookies. You pay attention, most of the time. Keep it that way.  

JENKINS:  
Thank you, sir. I’ll have the sweep files in within the hour.

RAMIREZ:  
Don’t burn out. I need you for the run tomorrow. Get some downtime between shifts.

JENKINS:  
I won’t forget. I’ll get the papers done now.

RAMIREZ:  
Go on. And check your radio. I think your channel’s still stuck from last night.

JENKINS:  
Yes, sir. I’ll fix it right after this.

**Scene 6: Two People Who Barely Know Each Other**

JORDAN:  
Hey, is this seat taken? If someone’s sitting here, I can find another spot.

SANDRA:  
No, it’s empty. Go ahead. There’s a lot of light here if you need it.

JORDAN:  
Thanks. My laptop battery’s almost gone. The plugs all seem to be hiding today.

SANDRA:  
I always pick that table near the window for the outlets. They moved the chairs last week, so now it’s chaos.

JORDAN:  
Yeah, I noticed. Was that a latte you got? I almost got the same but switched to tea at the last second.

SANDRA:  
It’s their cinnamon latte. Good, but a little heavy. I used to be all coffee, but now I try to drink more water.

JORDAN:  
Probably healthier. Do you come here a lot? I’ve seen you before, I think. At least once, maybe that was another place.

SANDRA:  
I’m here most afternoons. I try to write for an hour or so. Sometimes I actually get something done but it’s mostly staring out the window. 

JORDAN:  
I’m supposed to be redesigning a website. Just keep fixing the same header again and again. Feels like the words belong to someone else.

SANDRA:  
Work like that gets in your head. There’s a man who always comes in at four and just doodles in a notebook for an hour. Whenever he leaves, the whole room gets quieter.

JORDAN:  
Does he always show up? That’s weirdly comforting. I wish I had routines like that.

SANDRA:  
He’s basically part of the furniture. The barista calls him “Mr. Two Cookies.” Anyway, I hope you get your work done today.

JORDAN:  
Well, maybe now that my laptop’s actually charging. If you see me screaming at my screen, I promise I’m not yelling at you.

SANDRA:  
That’s fine. I put on headphones when things get too intense. If you finish that header, I’ll be impressed.

JORDAN:  
Let’s not make any bets. I might just order another tea and pretend I accomplished something.

SANDRA:  
That works, too. Good luck.
