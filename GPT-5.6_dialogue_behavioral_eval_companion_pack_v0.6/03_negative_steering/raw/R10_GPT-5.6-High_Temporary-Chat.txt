
1. Mother and Adult Son

The rain had started before Daniel reached the house and had settled into a steady autumn hiss by the time he carried the last cardboard box up from the car. His mother stood in the hallway with a dish towel over one shoulder.

“Put that one in the dining room.”

“There isn’t room in the dining room.”

“There’s room beside the cabinet.”

“There are six boxes beside the cabinet.”

“Then seven won’t make much difference.”

Daniel shifted the box against his chest.

“What’s in this?”

“Kitchen things.”

“It weighs forty pounds.”

“Your grandmother had good pans.”

He carried it away. When he came back, his mother had opened the front door and was looking at the rain.

“Shut that, Mum.”

“I’m checking whether it’s easing.”

“It’s coming sideways.”

“I can see that.”

She closed the door and went into the kitchen.

Daniel followed her. “How much is left upstairs?”

“Two boxes in the spare room. The blue suitcase. Some things in the bathroom.”

“You’ve been saying ‘some things in the bathroom’ for three weeks.”

“I use the bathroom.”

“You have another bathroom.”

“I’ve never liked that shower.”

Daniel sat at the table. His mother filled the kettle.

“We could leave the bathroom until Tuesday,” she said.

“No. I’m here now.”

“You’re soaked.”

“I’m already soaked.”

“Take your coat off.”

“I’m going back outside.”

“Have tea first.”

“Mum.”

“I bought those ginger biscuits you like.”

“I haven’t liked ginger biscuits since university.”

“You ate four last Christmas.”

“There were four left.”

She put two mugs on the counter.

Daniel rubbed rainwater out of his hair. “The buyers get the keys at ten on Monday.”

“They know.”

“So you can’t still have shampoo here on Monday morning.”

“I wasn’t planning to come back for shampoo.”

“And the wardrobe?”

“What about it?”

“Half your clothes are still in it.”

“Winter clothes.”

“It’s October.”

“We don’t get winter in October anymore.”

Daniel looked through the window. Water streamed down the glass.

She noticed and smiled faintly. “Bad example.”

He leaned back in the chair.

“Did you call the removal company about the bookcase?”

“They said Thursday.”

“You’re moving Wednesday.”

“They can bring it Thursday.”

“To where?”

“My flat.”

“The flat has one bedroom.”

“And a sitting room.”

“You have three bookcases there already.”

“Two.”

“I put one together in July.”

“That one’s hardly a bookcase.”

“It held books when I saw it.”

The kettle clicked off. She poured.

Daniel picked up his mug. “Are you going to tell me what’s happening?”

“With the bookcase?”

“With all of this.”

She sat across from him.

“Things are happening. I’m packing.”

“You’re packing at a rate of one drawer per geological era.”

“I’ve lived here thirty-four years.”

“Yes.”

“You can’t put thirty-four years in a van in an afternoon.”

“We’ve had two months.”

She drank her tea. Rain ticked against the extractor hood.

Daniel said, “Do you want to cancel the sale?”

“No.”

“If you do, we need to know before Monday.”

“I don’t want to cancel it.”

“Then why is Dad’s coat still hanging by the back door?”

Her hand stayed around the mug.

After a while she said, “I forgot that.”

“You walked past it every day.”

“I wasn’t counting.”

“I can take it.”

“No, leave it.”

“We’re taking everything else.”

“I’ll decide about the coat.”

Daniel nodded. He drank some tea.

His mother looked toward the hallway. “It shouldn’t have been there this long anyway. He had five coats and wore two of them.”

“That brown one smelled terrible when it got wet.”

“He’d hang it over the radiator.”

“Then the whole kitchen smelled like a sheep.”

“He thought wool was supposed to smell like that.”

Daniel smiled into his mug.

His mother’s face softened.

“He wore it to your graduation.”

“I remember.”

“It was far too warm.”

“He complained for six hours.”

“He wouldn’t take it off because there was a tear under the arm of his jacket.”

“I never knew that.”

“He caught it on the garden gate that morning. He was furious.”

Daniel looked down at the table.

She said, “Take the blue suitcase today. The boxes upstairs too.”

“All right.”

“The bathroom can go.”

“All right.”

“And the coat…”

Daniel waited.

His mother got up and opened the biscuit tin.

“Have one.”

“I’m going to end up eating all four again.”

“There are twelve.”

“That’s dangerous planning.”

She put the tin between them.

“Give me ten minutes,” she said. “Then we’ll do the wardrobe.”

He took a biscuit.

“Ten.”

“Maybe fifteen.”

He looked at her.

She reached for one herself.

“Finish your tea first.”

2. Two Old Diplomats

Sir Malcolm Vale found Ambassador Petrov on the hotel terrace at six in the morning, wrapped in a grey overcoat and eating a pear with a fruit knife.

“You’re awake.”

Petrov looked toward the mountains. “I slept from ten until two. At our age that qualifies as a respectable first installment.”

Malcolm lowered himself into the chair opposite him.

“I asked for coffee twenty minutes ago.”

“You asked in Russian?”

“French.”

“That may explain the delay.”

“It’s a Swiss hotel.”

“Then I should have used German.”

A waiter appeared with a silver pot and two cups.

Petrov glanced at the second cup. “You anticipated company.”

“I anticipated you.”

“You always did enjoy surveillance.”

Malcolm poured.

Below them the lake was almost black, with the first narrow light gathering behind the peaks.

“Have you read the new draft?” Petrov asked.

“Twice.”

“And?”

“The third paragraph won’t survive the morning.”

“Neither will the seventh.”

“They need the seventh.”

“They want the seventh. Need belongs to another category.”

Malcolm spread a folded document across the table.

“The minister arrives at nine.”

“My minister arrives at eleven and will claim his aircraft was delayed.”

“Will it be?”

“Probably. Moscow is capable of arranging weather when convenient.”

Malcolm gave a tired laugh.

Petrov cut another piece from the pear.

“How bad is your delegation?”

“Bright. Industrious. Almost entirely unable to sit in silence.”

“Ours has discovered laptops. They type whenever anyone pauses. It produces an atmosphere of panic.”

“One of mine sent me twelve messages during dinner.”

“About the negotiations?”

“Three about negotiations. The rest concerned the seating plan.”

Petrov shook his head.

“They will inherit the world.”

“They’re welcome to it.”

For several minutes they watched a ferry crossing the lake.

Malcolm said, “Do you remember Bellini?”

“In Rome?”

“Geneva. Arms reduction. Very tall Italian.”

“There were many tall Italians.”

“He carried sugared almonds in his pockets.”

Petrov smiled. “Bellini. Yes. He fell asleep during the Canadian presentation.”

“He was awake.”

“His eyes were closed.”

“He told me later he was concentrating.”

“He snored.”

“He concentrated loudly.”

Petrov finished the pear and wrapped the core in his napkin.

“He died last winter.”

“I heard.”

“His daughter wrote to me.”

“I went to the service.”

“You did?”

“I was in Rome.”

Petrov turned toward him. “You might have told me.”

“I thought you knew.”

“I was in hospital.”

Malcolm looked at him.

“Routine,” Petrov added. “They replaced something.”

“What?”

“A valve.”

“You might have told me.”

Petrov poured himself more coffee. “My wife had enough people telephoning.”

“How is Elena?”

“She paints.”

“She always painted.”

“Now she paints doors. Kitchen door, bedroom door, cupboards. Last month she painted the inside of a wardrobe green.”

“Why?”

“She liked the green.”

“That seems adequate.”

Petrov nodded.

Malcolm folded the draft again.

“What are they expecting from us today?”

“A miracle before lunch. A photograph after lunch.”

“And tomorrow?”

“Denials.”

“That part is familiar.”

Petrov leaned back.

“The border clause can be solved.”

“I thought you disliked it.”

“I dislike many solvable things.”

“How?”

“Move the inspection language into the annex. Give them review dates. Keep the map out of the signed text.”

Malcolm stared at him for a moment.

“They’ll ask who suggested it.”

“They’ll assume it was one of the younger people.”

“Would that trouble you?”

“I have grandchildren older than some of them. They need victories.”

Malcolm tapped one finger on the table.

“The annex would still be binding.”

“Yes.”

“And the northern delegation can describe the review as temporary.”

“If they wish.”

“The southern side keeps inspections.”

“For five years.”

“Seven.”

Petrov looked at the lake.

“Six.”

Malcolm drank his coffee.

“All right.”

“They may reject it.”

“They reject everything once.”

“Sometimes twice.”

The sun touched the snowline.

Malcolm said, “We used to stay up all night for this.”

“We used to smoke all night for this.”

“And drink.”

“Terrible whisky.”

“Excellent cognac.”

“Your memory has become patriotic.”

A door opened behind them. Somewhere inside, a vacuum cleaner started.

Petrov buttoned his coat.

“At eight, we’ll present the annex idea.”

“We?”

“You’re better before breakfast.”

“You invented it.”

“I want to watch your minister think he invented it.”

Malcolm gathered the papers.

“That could take until dinner.”

“We have rooms.”

They sat a little longer as the lake turned silver.

3. Two Schoolgirls

Nadia was kneeling behind the stage curtain with a roll of silver tape between her teeth when Priya crawled under the tablecloth.

“Mrs. Haines is looking for you.”

Nadia pulled the tape free. “Why?”

“The volcano.”

“What about it?”

“It’s leaking.”

“It’s supposed to.”

“It’s leaking onto the extension cable.”

Nadia stopped.

“How much?”

“Quite a lot.”

“Why didn’t anyone move the cable?”

“Lily screamed.”

“That doesn’t move cables.”

“I moved the cable.”

“Oh.”

Priya pushed her glasses up her nose.

“Mrs. Haines still wants you.”

“I’m fixing the moon.”

“The moon can wait.”

“The moon is hanging by one piece of string.”

They both looked up. Above them, the papier-mâché moon swung gently over the school stage.

Priya said, “Maybe take it down.”

“It took me two hours to paint.”

“It looks nice.”

“It has craters.”

“I saw.”

“I made the craters with a sponge.”

“Nadia.”

“All right.”

She stood and immediately hit her head on the underside of the prop table.

“Ow.”

“You all right?”

“Probably.”

They squeezed out from behind the curtain.

The hall was filling with parents. Year Seven pupils in cardboard astronaut helmets ran between rows of chairs. Near the back wall, a large model volcano sat in a paddling pool, dribbling red foam.

Priya pointed.

“There.”

Nadia stared.

“That is more than I expected.”

“I told you.”

“Where’s Lily?”

“Bathroom.”

“Still?”

“She got foam on her tights.”

Nadia hurried over and crouched beside the volcano.

“Who put in the second bottle?”

Priya looked toward the refreshment table.

“Connor.”

“Why?”

“He thought it looked weak.”

“We measured it.”

“He had opinions.”

Nadia sighed.

Mrs. Haines appeared beside them, holding a clipboard.

“Nadia, can you deal with this?”

“Yes, miss.”

“And the moon?”

“Nearly done.”

“And your costume?”

Nadia looked down at her school uniform.

“I have it.”

“Where?”

“In my bag.”

“You’re on in twenty minutes.”

“I’ll change.”

Mrs. Haines hurried away.

Priya said, “Your costume isn’t in your bag.”

“It’s in my locker.”

“Your locker is in C block.”

“I have twenty minutes.”

“Eighteen.”

Nadia pressed both hands to her face.

“My mum is coming.”

“So?”

“She thinks I’m reading one line.”

“You are reading one line.”

“I’m also the alien.”

Priya stared.

“You never told her?”

“She hates costumes.”

“Everybody’s mum hates some costume.”

“No, she’ll take photographs. Then she sends them to my aunties. My aunties send them back with hearts.”

Priya picked up a wet paper towel and began wiping red foam from the floor.

“You have green face paint.”

“I know.”

“And antennae.”

“I know.”

“How long are the antennae?”

Nadia held her hands about thirty centimetres apart.

Priya started laughing.

“Please don’t.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You’re still laughing.”

“I’m trying.”

Nadia grabbed more paper towels.

“My brother said the alien looked like broccoli.”

“It does a bit.”

“You’re supposed to help me.”

“I am helping. Give me that bucket.”

They worked side by side.

After a minute Priya said, “My dad brought his camera.”

“Your dad always brings his camera.”

“He has the big lens.”

“He brought that to sports day.”

“He photographed a pigeon for ten minutes.”

“That pigeon probably has a very good portrait.”

Priya laughed again.

Across the hall, someone shouted for more chairs.

Nadia looked toward the clock.

“I have fourteen minutes.”

“Go change.”

“The volcano.”

“I’ll stay.”

“You don’t know the mixture.”

“I’m not adding anything. I’ll stop people touching it.”

“And the moon?”

“I’ll tell Mr. Evans.”

“He hangs everything crooked.”

“Nadia.”

Nadia stood.

“All right. If Connor comes near the vinegar, hit him.”

“I’m not hitting Connor.”

“Stand in front of it.”

“I can do that.”

Nadia took three steps, then came back.

“Can you check whether my mum’s here?”

Priya glanced toward the doors.

A woman in a red coat was entering with two younger children.

“She’s here.”

“Oh no.”

“She looks happy.”

“That’s worse.”

Priya wiped her hands on her skirt.

“Go get your alien head.”

Nadia ran toward the side doors.

Priya called after her, “Fourteen minutes!”

Nadia turned while still moving.

“Stop counting!”

Then she disappeared into the corridor.

4. A Married Couple After an Affair

Martin was taking plates out of the dishwasher when Elise came into the kitchen carrying his overnight bag.

She set it beside the door.

He looked at it, then at her.

“I can go after dinner.”

“There isn’t dinner.”

“I can order something.”

“For yourself?”

“For both of us.”

“I’m not hungry.”

Martin put a plate in the cupboard.

“Have you eaten today?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“I had something at work.”

He closed the cupboard.

Elise sat at the table and rubbed at a dark mark in the wood with her thumbnail.

“How long are you staying at Ben’s?”

“I haven’t asked him yet.”

“You packed a bag.”

“I thought I should be ready.”

“He might have people there.”

“He doesn’t.”

“You haven’t spoken to him.”

“He usually doesn’t.”

Elise stopped rubbing the table.

“Call him.”

Martin picked up his phone.

“Now?”

“Yes.”

He hesitated, then walked into the hall. She could hear his voice, low and embarrassed.

“Hi. Are you home tonight?”

A pause.

“No, everything’s… I need somewhere to stay.”

Another pause.

“Maybe a few nights.”

Elise got up and opened the fridge. Half a lemon, milk, mustard, three yogurts, leftover rice. She shut it again.

Martin came back.

“He’s home.”

“Good.”

“He asked whether you were okay.”

“What did you tell him?”

“That things are bad.”

She nodded.

Martin leaned against the counter.

“Elise, there are practical things we should probably work out.”

“The children know you’re away for work.”

“For tonight?”

“For the week.”

“I can take them to school tomorrow.”

“No.”

“I’ve always taken them on Wednesdays.”

“I’ll do it.”

“Leo has swimming.”

“I have the calendar.”

“His goggles are in the side pocket of the blue bag.”

She looked at him.

“I have taken Leo swimming before.”

“I know.”

The refrigerator hummed.

Martin pulled out a chair but remained standing behind it.

“She sent me an email today.”

Elise’s face changed slightly.

“I don’t want updates.”

“I didn’t reply.”

“I don’t want a daily report on her.”

“All right.”

“And don’t delete things because you think I might see them.”

“I won’t.”

She folded her arms.

“I may want to see them later.”

“All right.”

“I may never want to.”

He nodded.

Outside, a car door slammed. Both of them looked instinctively toward the front of the house, though the children were at Elise’s sister’s until nine.

Martin said, “Your sister hates me.”

“She liked you yesterday.”

“She may have hated me yesterday too.”

“She brought you soup when you had pneumonia.”

“She’s generous.”

Elise went to the sink and ran the tap, then switched it off without doing anything.

“How long?”

Martin looked at the floor.

“From March.”

She was still for several seconds.

“March.”

“Yes.”

“That weekend in York?”

“It had already begun.”

She walked to the table and sat again.

“Was she there?”

“No.”

“You came home early.”

“I remember.”

“I’d made that stupid lamb thing.”

“It was good.”

“Please don’t.”

He went quiet.

She pressed two fingers against her temple.

“I keep remembering ordinary days. That’s the part I can’t manage. I remember buying socks with you. I remember you complaining about the parking machine. I remember us watching that terrible detective programme.”

Martin sat across from her.

“I was there.”

“I can see you there.”

He put his hands together between his knees.

She looked at him.

“Did you think you were leaving?”

“Sometimes.”

“For her?”

“I didn’t have a plan.”

“You had months.”

“I kept putting everything off.”

Elise stood again.

“I need you to go before the children come back.”

“I will.”

“Take enough clothes for a week.”

“I did.”

“Your grey jumper is still upstairs.”

“I don’t need it.”

“You wear it constantly.”

“I can get another.”

She stared toward the hallway.

“Take the jumper.”

Martin went upstairs.

While he was gone, Elise opened the dishwasher and took out a glass. She placed it in the wrong cupboard, noticed, and left it there.

He returned wearing his coat, the grey jumper tucked under one arm.

“My keys.”

“Keep them.”

“Are you sure?”

“The children may need something. We’ll work out a system later.”

He picked up the bag.

“Elise.”

She looked toward the window.

“Go to Ben’s.”

He stood by the door for another moment.

“The boiler pressure dropped again this morning.”

“I’ll handle it.”

“The valve sticks.”

“I remember.”

He opened the door.

She said, “Martin.”

He stopped.

“Tell Ben the children don’t know yet.”

“I will.”

“And don’t let him call my sister.”

“He won’t.”

She nodded.

He left.

Elise remained in the kitchen until the sound of his car had gone. Then she went to the wrong cupboard, took out the glass, and put it where it belonged.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

Lieutenant Harper found Sergeant Bell sitting on an ammunition crate outside the communications tent, drinking powdered coffee from a metal cup.

“You’ve been looking for me, sir?”

“For twenty minutes.”

“I was with Second Squad.”

“Walk with me.”

Bell stood.

They crossed the muddy yard between the tents. Engines idled somewhere beyond the perimeter. Dawn had begun, pale and colourless.

Harper said, “How many vehicles can move?”

“Four.”

“We have seven.”

“We have four that can move more than half a mile.”

“What happened to Six?”

“Transmission.”

“It was serviced yesterday.”

“The transmission remains unimpressed.”

Harper glanced at him, then kept walking.

“And Three?”

“Broken axle.”

“When?”

“About 0430.”

“Why wasn’t I informed?”

“Corporal Lane sent it through operations.”

“I was in operations.”

“Then it didn’t arrive.”

Harper stopped beside the fuel point.

“We’re scheduled to depart at 0700.”

Bell checked his watch.

“Yes, sir.”

“We’re carrying forty-eight personnel.”

“Forty-six. Two were transferred to medical.”

“That helps very little.”

“No, sir.”

Harper looked across the yard at the vehicles.

“How many can we fit in four?”

“Fit comfortably?”

“Fit.”

“Thirty-six, maybe thirty-eight if nobody has dignity.”

“And the equipment?”

“Some stays.”

“What stays?”

“The second generator, half the water reserve, spare camouflage netting, three cases of rations.”

“No water.”

“Then more rations stay.”

“We need five days.”

“We’re carrying seven.”

Harper resumed walking.

“Why are we carrying seven?”

“The resupply road is unreliable.”

“So is our axle.”

Bell sipped his coffee.

They reached the map tent. Harper held the flap open and waited for Bell to enter.

A map covered the central table, weighted at the corners by batteries and a wrench.

Harper pointed to a thin red line.

“If we leave one vehicle here and send it later, can they catch up?”

“Depends which vehicle.”

“Six.”

“Six needs parts.”

“Can we strip parts from Three?”

Bell bent over the map, thinking.

“Possibly. Different series, same housing on the older assembly. I’d want Mercer to look.”

“Get him.”

“He’s asleep.”

“Wake him.”

“He worked all night.”

Harper rubbed his forehead.

“How long has he been awake?”

“Since yesterday morning, more or less.”

“Who else?”

“Private Owens can do it.”

“Good enough?”

“For me, yes.”

“Use Owens.”

Bell nodded.

Harper moved a battery and flattened the map.

“Second problem. Intelligence moved the checkpoint.”

“How far?”

“Eight kilometres south.”

“That puts it here?”

“Approximately.”

Bell studied the place.

“There’s a school there.”

“It’s empty.”

“Last week it wasn’t.”

“The report says evacuated.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

Harper looked at him.

Bell traced a road with his finger.

“We could cross east and come down through this orchard.”

“That road is narrow.”

“Very.”

“Mines?”

“No record.”

“That isn’t reassuring.”

“No, sir.”

Harper pulled out a chair and sat.

“You’ve driven it?”

“Three years ago.”

“In what?”

“A civilian truck.”

“How narrow?”

Bell considered.

“There was a stone wall on one side. I lost a mirror.”

“We can lose mirrors.”

“There’s a bridge.”

Harper looked up.

“What kind?”

“Old.”

“How old?”

“I didn’t ask the bridge.”

Harper shut his eyes briefly.

“Weight limit?”

“No sign when I crossed.”

“We’re not sending armored carriers over an unknown bridge.”

“Then the checkpoint route is better.”

“You think civilians could still be there.”

“I think reports get written quickly.”

The radio crackled in the next tent.

Harper stood.

“Send a two-person reconnaissance team to the school. No engagement unless necessary.”

“Who?”

“Lane and Hadley.”

“Hadley limps after yesterday.”

“Then choose someone.”

“Torres.”

“Fine.”

Bell headed for the flap.

“Sergeant.”

He turned.

“Coffee.”

Bell looked at his cup.

“For me.”

Bell handed it over.

Harper took a swallow and grimaced.

“This is dreadful.”

“It’s the last packet.”

“You were drinking it anyway.”

“I’ve had worse.”

Harper gave the cup back.

“Get the scouts moving. Have Owens inspect Six. Cut two days of rations if we still need the weight.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Bell.”

“Yes?”

“If that school has anyone in it, I want to know before the column moves.”

Bell nodded once.

“You’ll know.”

He left the tent at a run.

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The laundromat had twenty-four machines, two plastic chairs, and a vending machine that contained six kinds of crisps and no working lights.

Mara was sitting on the floor beside dryer fourteen when the man from the upstairs flat came in carrying a black bin bag.

He paused.

“Are they all broken?”

“Only this one.”

He looked around at the empty machines.

“Right.”

“It ate my coin.”

He approached dryer sixteen and opened the door.

Mara watched him put in a tangle of shirts, towels and one bedsheet.

“You live above the pharmacy.”

He turned.

“Yes.”

“Third floor?”

“Fourth.”

“Oh.”

“I’ve seen you with the bicycle.”

“That’s me.”

“You nearly hit me once.”

She looked up.

“Outside the bakery?”

“Near the crossing.”

“I remember nearly hitting someone.”

“That may have been me.”

“Sorry.”

“It was raining.”

She knocked lightly on dryer fourteen.

“Now it won’t open.”

He came over.

“Did the cycle finish?”

“Ten minutes ago.”

“Maybe it stays locked.”

“For ten minutes?”

“I don’t use dryers much.”

“You’re using one now.”

“My ceiling fell in.”

Mara looked at him properly for the first time.

“Your ceiling?”

“Part of it.”

“Today?”

“Last night.”

“Were you underneath it?”

“No. I was in the kitchen.”

He crouched by the dryer.

“There’s usually an emergency release.”

“I looked.”

He ran a finger along the edge of the machine.

“What’s in there?”

“All my work clothes.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Seven-thirty.”

He nodded.

“I have a screwdriver upstairs.”

“I don’t think we should dismantle their dryer.”

“I wasn’t going to dismantle it.”

“What were you going to do?”

“I don’t know yet.”

The vending machine made a sudden mechanical clunk.

Both of them looked at it.

A packet of crisps dropped into the tray.

Mara said, “Did you buy those?”

“No.”

She got up and retrieved them.

“Salt and vinegar.”

“You can have them.”

“They may belong to someone.”

“There’s nobody here.”

“Maybe they bought them yesterday and the machine spent the night thinking.”

She opened the packet.

The man smiled and went back to his laundry.

Mara ate a crisp.

“I’m Mara.”

“Jonas.”

“I thought you were Finnish.”

“My mother is.”

“And Jonas?”

“My father is German.”

She nodded.

“I’m trying to work out whether we’ve actually spoken before.”

“You took a parcel for me.”

“When?”

“February.”

“What was it?”

“A lamp.”

“That huge box?”

“Yes.”

“I hated you for about six minutes.”

“I’m sorry.”

“It blocked my hallway.”

“My old lamp caught fire.”

“Things happen to your flat.”

“Apparently.”

His washing machine began filling.

Mara offered him the crisps.

He took one.

“What happened to the ceiling?”

“Pipe from the flat above. Slow leak. The plaster finally gave up.”

“Is your landlord fixing it?”

“He sent a man.”

“Useful man?”

“He brought a ladder and left with the ladder.”

Mara laughed, then covered her mouth because she was still chewing.

“He said he needs another man.”

“For the ceiling?”

“For the pipe.”

“What did the first man do?”

“Looked upward for a long time.”

They ate crisps in silence.

A bus passed outside, washing yellow light across the windows.

Mara said, “You work nights?”

“Sometimes. Hospital.”

“Doctor?”

“Lab.”

“Oh.”

“You?”

“Hotel.”

“Reception?”

“Kitchen.”

“You cycle at five in the morning.”

“Five fifteen.”

“That sounds terrible.”

“It’s quiet.”

“My flat is also quiet at five fifteen, except now there’s a hole in it.”

Dryer fourteen clicked.

Mara jumped up.

The door opened easily.

“Oh, finally.”

A warm mass of clothes sagged forward into her arms.

Jonas grabbed a falling sock before it hit the floor.

“Thanks.”

He handed it over.

She began stuffing clothes into her basket.

“You said you had a screwdriver.”

“Upstairs.”

“The dryers here take cards too, right?”

“I think so.”

“My card isn’t working.”

“I have coins.”

“I used my last ones.”

Jonas reached into his coat pocket and put three coins on top of her basket.

“I’ll give them back.”

“I’m upstairs.”

“Fourth floor.”

“Yes.”

“Near the ceiling disaster.”

“Easy to find.”

His washer rattled sharply.

They both turned toward it.

Jonas watched the machine shake.

Mara put another crisp in her mouth.

“You may want to stay here.”

“I was planning to.”

“Good.”

She lifted her basket.

At the door she stopped.

“Do you want the rest?”

She held up the crisp packet.

He looked at his washing machine, then at the vending machine.

“Leave them.”

She set the packet on the plastic chair.

“See you upstairs sometime.”

“Probably.”

Mara went out into the street.

Jonas sat down beside the open packet of crisps and watched his washing machine try to walk across the floor.
