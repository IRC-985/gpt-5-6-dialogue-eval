
1. Mother and Son

The kitchen light was on when Daniel came in. His mother was sitting at the table in her dressing gown with a bowl of green beans between her hands. She had been snapping the ends off them and had stopped halfway through.

“You could have called.”

“I thought you’d be asleep.”

“At half past ten?”

“You used to go to bed at nine.”

“When you were twelve. I had to get up at six and make your lunch.”

Daniel put his keys beside the fruit bowl.

“Where’s the blue suitcase?”

“In the cupboard under the stairs.”

“I looked.”

“You looked like you always look.”

He opened the refrigerator and stood in front of it.

“There’s soup,” she said.

“I ate on the train.”

“There’s soup anyway.”

He closed the refrigerator.

“I’ll take the suitcase in the morning.”

His mother broke the end off another bean.

“How long are you going for?”

“Three months, maybe four.”

“You told your sister six weeks.”

“That was before.”

“Before when?”

“Before they changed the contract.”

She nodded and worked on the beans. Daniel pulled out the chair opposite her.

“You don’t have to do those now.”

“They’ve been sitting there since four.”

“They’ll survive.”

“I might not want to look at them tomorrow.”

Daniel rubbed his eyes.

“I need Dad’s drill too.”

“It’s in the garage.”

“The cordless one?”

“There were three drills. I don’t know which one had the cord and which one didn’t.”

“The yellow one.”

“Oh. That one.”

She dropped a bean into the bowl.

“Is it still there?”

“I gave it to Martin.”

Daniel stared at her.

“Martin next door?”

“He was putting shelves up.”

“When?”

“Spring.”

“He’s had it since spring?”

“He brought it back.”

“So where is it?”

“I gave it to your uncle after that.”

Daniel leaned back.

“Fine.”

“You can buy a drill.”

“I can. I just thought there was already one here.”

“There are many things here.”

She looked toward the hallway.

“More than anybody needs.”

Daniel followed her eyes.

“You’re still thinking of moving?”

“I’ve been thinking for five years. Thinking hasn’t disturbed the furniture yet.”

“You could get someone to value the place.”

“A man came.”

“You never told me.”

“He had shiny shoes. He walked into your old room and opened the wardrobe without asking.”

“That’s his job.”

“He can have some other job.”

Daniel laughed once, tiredly. His mother pushed the bowl aside.

“He said the roof needs work.”

“It does.”

“How would you know?”

“I saw the stain upstairs.”

“You saw that at Christmas.”

“It’s probably bigger now.”

“It isn’t.”

“You checked?”

“I put a bucket under it.”

Daniel looked at her for a moment.

“Mum.”

“I’ll call somebody.”

“When?”

“I have a number.”

“Whose?”

“June’s nephew.”

“The one who did her bathroom?”

“He fixed the leak.”

“Her ceiling fell down.”

“That was afterward.”

He got up and filled the kettle.

“I’ll look at it tomorrow.”

“You’re collecting a suitcase, a drill that isn’t here, and examining the roof. Your train is at eleven.”

“I can change the train.”

“No. You’ve got your life.”

He turned the kettle on.

She picked up another bean, then held it without snapping it.

“Do you like her?”

Daniel’s hand remained on the kettle switch.

“Who?”

“The woman in Stockholm.”

“There are several women in Stockholm.”

“The one you’re going with.”

“I’m going for work.”

His mother put the bean down.

“Your sister showed me a photograph.”

“Clara posts photographs of everybody.”

“You had your hand on this woman’s back.”

“There were eight people in the picture.”

“I counted.”

Daniel opened a cupboard for mugs.

“You want tea?”

“Yes.”

He put two mugs down.

“Her name’s Elise.”

“All right.”

“She works in the same department.”

“All right.”

“She’s moving there too.”

His mother glanced at him.

“Is that convenient?”

“It happened that way.”

She watched him pour the water.

“Will you keep your flat?”

“For now.”

“And the plants?”

“Ben’s taking them.”

“The big one too?”

“If he can get it downstairs.”

“That thing hates being moved.”

“It’s a plant.”

She accepted the mug.

“You said that about the cat when you took him to university.”

“The cat scratched through a bathroom door.”

“He was upset.”

“He was insane.”

“He liked you.”

Daniel sat down again.

They drank tea in silence.

After a while she said, “The blue suitcase has a broken wheel.”

“I know.”

“There’s another one in my room. Grey. Your father bought it and used it once.”

“For Portugal?”

“For leaving me.”

Daniel looked up.

She blew across her tea.

“He came back before he got farther than Birmingham.”

“I’ve never heard this.”

“There wasn’t much of a story. He packed badly, drove badly, and telephoned me from a service station.”

“What did he say?”

“He asked where his blood-pressure tablets were.”

Daniel smiled despite himself.

“And you told him?”

“I drove them to him.”

“Why?”

She shrugged.

“It was raining.”

The kettle clicked softly as it cooled.

Daniel reached across the table and took a handful of beans.

“You missed some ends.”

“I got tired.”

He started snapping them.

“I’ll take the grey suitcase.”

“You may as well. It has four good wheels.”

“Luxury.”

His mother drank her tea and watched his hands.

“Don’t put the pieces in with the beans.”

“I remember.”

“You never remembered.”

“I do now.”

She let him finish the bowl.

2. Two Old Diplomats

The hotel terrace overlooked a harbor bright with naval lights. Ambassador Sayegh had ordered mineral water. Sir Philip had ordered brandy and was pretending not to notice that Sayegh disapproved.

“They’ve put us beside the kitchen again,” Sir Philip said.

“We are former officials. We are lucky to be indoors.”

“We were both foreign ministers once.”

“For very brief and regrettable periods.”

“Mine lasted eighteen months.”

“Mine lasted nine. I had the advantage.”

A waiter passed carrying silver lids.

Sir Philip watched him go.

“Do you remember Belgrade?”

“I remember being cold.”

“You wore that absurd hat.”

“My wife bought it.”

“It made you look like a railway inspector.”

“She liked railway inspectors.”

Sir Philip laughed and took a sip.

“The young ones downstairs think we’re here for atmosphere.”

“We are atmosphere.”

“Tomorrow they’ll ask us about the crisis.”

“They asked me this afternoon.”

“And?”

“I told them the talks would continue.”

“They already knew that.”

“They seemed pleased.”

Sir Philip tapped the rim of his glass.

“My man asked whether the president can be trusted.”

“What did you tell him?”

“I asked for coffee.”

“That was sensible.”

“He brought tea.”

Sayegh smiled faintly.

For several minutes they watched a ferry cross the harbor.

Sir Philip said, “They’re going to close the northern crossing.”

“Probably.”

“You’ve heard?”

“No.”

“I heard.”

“From whom?”

“A colonel with very white teeth.”

“That narrows nothing.”

“He works for the Americans.”

“Everybody works for the Americans for twenty minutes at some point.”

Sir Philip reached inside his jacket and removed a folded sheet of hotel stationery.

“I wrote down the names. My memory has become theatrical. It performs when nobody needs it and disappears under pressure.”

Sayegh made no move toward the paper.

“You could use a telephone.”

“I don’t trust telephones.”

“You carried three when you arrived.”

“One belongs to my granddaughter. She left it in my car.”

“And the other two?”

“I don’t trust them.”

The harbor horn sounded.

Sir Philip unfolded the page.

“If they close the crossing, Darzi resigns.”

“He threatened that last year.”

“This time his brother’s involved.”

“His brother is always involved.”

“He has too many brothers.”

“He has four.”

“That is too many for public life.”

Sayegh looked toward the dining room windows. Inside, delegates drifted between tables with folders under their arms.

“Why did you come, Philip?”

“I was invited.”

“You complain every year.”

“I complain at home too.”

“You stopped attending these things after Geneva.”

“I stopped being invited after Geneva.”

“And now?”

Sir Philip folded the paper along a different crease.

“My daughter thought it would be good for me.”

“How old are you?”

“You were at my seventieth.”

“That was a long lunch.”

“I’m eighty-three.”

Sayegh considered this.

“I’m eighty-one.”

“You always were younger.”

“Two years.”

“It mattered when we were forty.”

A waiter appeared. Sir Philip ordered olives. Sayegh asked for bread.

When the waiter had gone, Sir Philip said, “Mariam died in March.”

Sayegh’s face changed very little.

“I hadn’t heard.”

“We kept it small.”

“How was she at the end?”

“Annoyed. She disliked the nurses’ shoes. She disliked the television. She disliked morphine because it made everybody agree with her.”

Sayegh lowered his eyes.

“She was kind to my son in Geneva.”

“She liked him.”

“He broke your lamp.”

“She disliked the lamp.”

Sir Philip turned his glass slowly on the table.

“The house is intolerable now.”

“You could sell it.”

“My children say that.”

“They may be right.”

“They usually are, which makes them exhausting.”

Sayegh tore a piece from the bread when it arrived.

“Come to Beirut.”

“For how long?”

“A week.”

“I’d be a burden.”

“There are servants who can resent you professionally.”

Sir Philip looked at him.

“Is that an invitation?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“October.”

“I may be dead.”

“Then November is difficult.”

Sir Philip laughed so loudly that two delegates inside turned toward the terrace.

He raised his glass toward them.

“They think we’ve settled something.”

“Let them.”

The waiter set down the olives.

Sir Philip ate one and made a face.

“Terrible.”

“Eat another.”

“I intend to.”

Below them, a patrol vessel moved slowly beyond the breakwater.

Sir Philip said, “Do you think they’ll close it before Friday?”

“The crossing?”

“Yes.”

Sayegh chewed his bread.

“Thursday night.”

“Why?”

“Friday would embarrass the delegation. Thursday lets everybody spend Friday denying it.”

Sir Philip took out his pen and wrote on the hotel paper.

“You still do this.”

“Do what?”

“Nothing.”

He put the pen away.

Sayegh poured him some mineral water without asking.

3. Two Schoolgirls

The girls were sitting behind the equipment shed because the science block fire alarm had gone off and everyone else was gathered on the football field.

Maisie had one shoe off.

“Your sock is disgusting,” Priya said.

“It got wet.”

“How?”

“Puddle.”

“There aren’t any puddles.”

“There was one by the bins.”

Priya passed her a packet of crisps.

“You’re going to get caught.”

“For eating?”

“For being here.”

“They’re counting forms, not individual humans.”

“They count individual humans when individual humans vanish.”

Maisie ate three crisps at once.

“Mrs. Cole thinks I’m at the dentist.”

“You’re literally registered this morning.”

“She’ll assume an administrative error.”

“She’ll assume you lied to her.”

Maisie looked through the gap between the shed and the hedge.

“You can go back.”

“I’m staying for five minutes.”

“You said that twenty minutes ago.”

“My legs hurt.”

“You did cross-country yesterday.”

“I walked cross-country yesterday.”

Priya opened her water bottle.

“Have you done your history?”

“No.”

“It’s due next period.”

“I have half a page.”

“You wrote that on the bus.”

“Still counts.”

“What’s it about?”

“Factories.”

“That’s geography.”

“Then I’ve got a problem.”

Priya laughed into the bottle and nearly choked.

Maisie looked pleased.

“I wrote about child labor.”

“That’s history.”

“Great.”

“And factories.”

“Even better.”

“You need dates.”

“I’ve got 1840 somewhere.”

“There was definitely an 1840.”

“See?”

From the field came the distant amplified voice of the deputy head.

Priya stopped smiling.

“They’re sending people back in.”

“No, he’s doing the safety speech.”

“How do you know?”

“He always has that voice for safety.”

“What other voice does he have?”

“Assembly voice. Angry corridor voice. Voice when parents are around.”

Priya stuffed the crisp packet into her blazer pocket.

“I hate today.”

“You hated yesterday.”

“Yesterday had PE.”

“What happened today?”

Priya picked at a loose thread on her skirt.

“My mum found the letter.”

“Which letter?”

“The orchestra one.”

“Oh.”

“I left it inside my French book.”

“Why?”

“I thought she never opens my French book.”

“Why did she open it?”

“She was looking for a receipt.”

“In your French book?”

“My mum puts things in places and then searches everywhere except the places she put them.”

Maisie put her shoe back on.

“So are you going?”

“She signed it.”

“To Prague?”

“Yes.”

“That’s good.”

Priya stared at her.

“It’s four hundred and eighty pounds.”

“They do the fund.”

“She wrote a cheque.”

Maisie went quiet.

Priya continued pulling the thread.

“She kept asking whether I really wanted to go. I kept saying I didn’t care. Then Dad came in and said I should have opportunities and then they went into the kitchen.”

“Did they fight?”

“They shut the door.”

“Your kitchen door doesn’t shut.”

“He held it.”

Maisie leaned back against the wall.

“My dad owes me forty pounds.”

“For what?”

“Birthday money. He borrowed it.”

“Adults can’t borrow birthday money.”

“He did.”

“What did he spend it on?”

“Petrol, supposedly.”

“Forty pounds of petrol is loads.”

“He drives a van.”

They sat for a while.

Priya said, “I could tell school I can’t go.”

“Do you want to go?”

“Yes.”

“Then go.”

“My mum’s going to wear the same coat for ten years.”

“She was going to do that anyway. She likes that coat.”

“It has a rip under the arm.”

“She can sew.”

“She can’t.”

“My gran can.”

Priya glanced over.

“Would your gran actually do it?”

“She fixed our curtains after the hamster ate them.”

“Hamsters don’t eat curtains.”

“Ours did.”

“How did it reach the curtains?”

“Long story.”

A bell rang.

Both girls looked toward the school.

“That’s definitely going back in,” Priya said.

Maisie stood.

“I’m taking the long route.”

“We’re already late.”

“I need the toilet.”

“You said Mrs. Cole thinks you’re at the dentist.”

“She can think I went to the toilet at the dentist.”

Priya slung her bag over one shoulder.

They started along the hedge.

After a few steps Maisie said, “Prague has that clock.”

“What clock?”

“The one with the skeleton.”

“That sounds horrible.”

“It’s famous.”

“My mum will make me take pictures.”

“Take a picture of the skeleton.”

“She’ll put it as her phone background.”

They reached the corner of the gym.

Priya stopped.

“Can I borrow your history?”

“It’s about factories.”

“You’ve got 1840.”

“I’ve got it somewhere.”

They went inside together.

4. A Married Couple After an Affair

The washing machine was running. Anna had turned it on because neither of them wanted the flat completely quiet.

Michael stood by the balcony door with a glass of water. She was folding towels on the sofa.

“Laura called.”

He looked over.

“When?”

“This afternoon.”

“What did she want?”

“She wanted to know whether I was still coming Saturday.”

“Are you?”

“I told her I’d see.”

Michael put the glass down.

“Her birthday?”

“Yes.”

“You should go.”

Anna folded another towel.

“She’ll ask where you are.”

“Tell her I’m ill.”

“She’ll offer to bring soup.”

“I can be somewhere else.”

Anna set the towel on the pile.

“You can tell her yourself.”

“All right.”

The machine entered its spin cycle, shuddering against the kitchen tiles.

Michael said, “I can sleep at Simon’s again.”

“He has one bedroom.”

“He has a sofa.”

“You’re forty-six.”

“The sofa doesn’t know.”

Anna gathered up the towels and carried them toward the hall cupboard.

Michael watched her return.

“I spoke to the hotel.”

She sat down again.

He waited.

“They found the earring.”

He touched his forehead.

“I thought you might want it.”

Anna looked at him for several seconds.

“No.”

“I can throw it away.”

“Leave it there.”

“They called because the booking was under my name.”

“Then they can deal with it.”

He nodded and drank some water.

Anna picked up a shirt from the laundry basket.

“Is that yours?”

“Yes.”

“It shrank.”

“It was already small.”

“I bought it.”

“You did.”

She put it on the arm of the sofa.

“What are you doing tomorrow?”

“Office.”

“You took the week off.”

“I’m going in.”

“Why?”

“Simon says people are asking questions.”

“They’ll keep asking.”

“Probably.”

“You could work from home.”

Michael rubbed his chin.

“I don’t want to be here all day.”

Anna kept folding.

After a while she said, “Neither do I.”

The machine rattled harder.

Michael crossed into the kitchen and leaned against the counter.

“I’ll call someone about that.”

“It’s the load.”

“It happens every time.”

“Because you put too many things in.”

“There are four towels.”

“And the bath mat.”

“Fine.”

He opened the machine drawer as if there might be an answer inside it, then closed it.

Anna said, “Did you ever take her to the house?”

“No.”

She folded one of his T-shirts.

“The cottage?”

“No.”

“My parents’ place?”

“No.”

“Here?”

“No.”

She put the shirt down carefully.

Michael stared at the floor.

“It was hotels. Her flat twice.”

Anna’s hands stopped.

“Twice?”

He looked toward her.

“Yes.”

She resumed folding.

The machine slowed.

“Did you cook there?”

“At her flat?”

“Yes.”

“Once.”

“What?”

“Pasta.”

“With the anchovies?”

“No.”

Anna stood and walked to the window.

He remained in the kitchen.

Outside, somebody was dragging a wheelie bin across the courtyard.

She said, “I keep getting stuck on things like food.”

“I understand.”

“I don’t want you to.”

Michael closed his mouth.

Anna pressed her forehead lightly to the glass.

“We need groceries.”

“I can go.”

“Milk. Coffee. Cat food.”

“I’ll make a list.”

“There’s paper by the phone.”

He found a pen.

“Anything else?”

“Dishwasher tablets.”

He wrote.

“Bread?”

“No.”

“Fruit?”

“Maybe apples.”

“What kind?”

Anna turned from the window.

“You’ve bought apples before.”

He put the pen down.

“I’ll get some.”

“Get the green ones.”

He wrote again.

The cat came in from the bedroom and sat beside the washing machine.

Michael bent to stroke it, but it moved away.

Anna watched.

“She’s been sleeping on your side.”

“I noticed.”

“I changed the sheets yesterday.”

He stood.

“Do you want me to take the duvet to the cleaner?”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

She sat back down.

For a time neither spoke.

Then Anna said, “When did you first think you were going to tell me?”

Michael rested both hands on the counter.

“June.”

“You waited until August.”

“Yes.”

“What happened in June?”

“We were at your father’s. He was cutting that melon in the garden, and you were washing the plates.”

Anna frowned slightly.

“I remember the melon.”

“I went outside afterward.”

“You played cards with him.”

“For about an hour.”

“He cheated.”

“He always cheats.”

Anna looked toward the basket.

“There are socks under you.”

Michael moved aside.

She came into the kitchen, crouched, and picked them up.

“Those are yours too.”

“I can fold them.”

“Take them.”

He held out his hand, and she gave him the socks.

For some reason, neither moved away immediately.

Then the washing machine beeped.

Anna stepped back.

“Get the groceries.”

Michael took his coat from the hook.

At the door he said, “I’ll be half an hour.”

She was opening the washing-machine door.

“Take a bag.”

He returned for one.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

The rain had been falling since midnight, thin and steady. Lieutenant Varin found Sergeant Pell under the awning behind the fuel shed, drinking coffee from a dented steel cup.

“You were supposed to wake me.”

“You’d been awake thirty hours.”

“That isn’t your decision.”

Pell looked toward the road.

“The bridge is still there.”

Varin pulled his cap lower.

“How many trucks crossed?”

“Seven supply, two ambulances, one engineering vehicle.”

“And civilian traffic?”

“None since two.”

“Why?”

“Roadblock at Karsin.”

“Our roadblock?”

Pell took another drink.

“No.”

Varin studied him.

“Who put it up?”

“Locals, from what I can tell. Farm machinery across the road. Maybe twenty people.”

“Armed?”

“I saw two hunting rifles. Could be more.”

“Any shots?”

“No.”

Varin stepped out into the rain and looked toward the checkpoint, a hundred meters away.

“Where’s Danner?”

“Asleep in the radio room.”

“He’s on watch.”

“He vomited twice.”

“Food?”

“Probably.”

“Get Chen.”

“She’s driving the second ambulance.”

Varin turned back sharply.

“Why?”

“Corporal Rees broke his wrist.”

“How?”

“Generator belt.”

Varin closed his eyes briefly.

“Who is on the radio?”

“Peters.”

“Peters can’t hear properly.”

“He hears the radio.”

“Only when it’s turned up enough for the enemy to hear.”

Pell finished the coffee.

“Would you like some?”

“No.”

“There’s a pot.”

“I said no.”

Pell put the cup on a crate.

A truck growled somewhere beyond the trees.

Varin said, “Headquarters sent anything?”

“Three messages.”

“You could have led with that.”

“The first two were weather.”

“And the third?”

“Hold the crossing. Avoid confrontation with civilians.”

Varin looked at the bridge again.

“That came when?”

“Zero four ten.”

“And Karsin was blocked when?”

“About zero three forty.”

“Did you report it?”

“Yes.”

“Response?”

“None.”

Varin began walking toward the command hut. Pell followed.

Inside, Private Peters sat by the radio with his headphones slightly crooked.

“Lieutenant.”

“Go eat.”

Peters stood at once and left.

Varin picked up the message forms.

“Who wrote this?”

“I did,” Pell said.

“Your handwriting’s getting worse.”

“My hand was wet.”

Varin read.

“Twenty to thirty civilians.”

“It was dark.”

“You told me twenty.”

“Closer to twenty when I saw them.”

“Any vehicles besides tractors?”

“A blue van. Two motorcycles.”

Varin read further.

“They want us to leave?”

“They want the fuel convoy stopped.”

“Why?”

“They say the army took diesel from their cooperative yesterday.”

“Our army?”

“They didn’t bring receipts.”

Varin put the paper down.

Pell reached for the coffee pot.

“You planning to move them?”

“I’m planning to get the convoy through.”

“It’s due at six.”

“I’m aware.”

“It’s six twenty.”

Varin checked his watch and swore quietly.

Pell filled another cup.

“You should eat.”

“You’re developing maternal tendencies.”

“Temporary.”

Varin leaned over the map.

“If the convoy goes south here—”

“Road washed out.”

“Here?”

“Mine clearance isn’t finished.”

“This track?”

“Too narrow after marker fourteen.”

Varin tapped the table with two fingers.

“So we use Karsin.”

“Yes.”

“Twenty civilians can be moved.”

Pell looked into his coffee.

“They’ve got children there.”

Varin stopped tapping.

“How many?”

“Five that I saw.”

“You left that out.”

“I saw them after the report went.”

“Send an update.”

“I did.”

Varin’s jaw tightened.

“All right. I’ll go talk to them.”

“I’ll come.”

“You stay here.”

Pell drank.

“Captain Soren tried talking to people in Dema alone.”

“And?”

“He got hit with a chair.”

“There are no chairs on this road.”

“Plenty of farm tools.”

Varin stared at him, then reached for his raincoat.

“Bring two men. Rifles slung. Nobody chambers a round unless I order it.”

“Baker and Noll?”

“Noll gets nervous.”

“He also speaks the dialect.”

“Then Noll.”

Pell set down the cup.

“And Baker?”

“Yes.”

He went to the door.

Varin said, “Sergeant.”

Pell turned.

“Next time, wake me.”

Pell scratched his eyebrow.

“If there’s a next time like this, you’ll already be awake.”

Varin looked at him.

Pell opened the door.

“Baker and Noll. Five minutes.”

“I heard you.”

Rain blew into the hut.

Varin folded the map and slipped it into his coat.

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The last bus had already gone. Lena discovered this from the timetable under the cracked plastic cover. The man beside her read the same timetable for another few seconds before stepping back.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“So it seems.”

“There might be a night bus from the station.”

“The station is five kilometers.”

“Yes.”

They stood under the shelter. Beyond the road was a closed garden center. Its illuminated sign made the wet pavement green.

The man took out his phone.

“No signal.”

“I have one bar.”

“Can you check taxis?”

Lena opened an app.

“Forty-eight minutes.”

“That’s bad.”

“There’s another company.”

She called. The line rang without answer.

The man sat on the metal bench.

“I’m Martin.”

“Lena.”

“Were you at the wedding?”

“Yes.”

“Bride or groom?”

“Groom. Sort of. I work with his sister.”

Martin nodded.

“I’m the bride’s dentist.”

Lena looked at him.

“You were invited as her dentist?”

“My wife is her cousin.”

“Where’s your wife?”

“She left at nine.”

“And you stayed.”

“She had the car.”

Lena put her phone away.

“There must be a story there.”

“She was tired. I was playing cards. She asked whether I wanted to come. I thought I could get a bus.”

“Very strong planning.”

“It had been a long day.”

They listened to the rain striking the shelter roof.

Martin said, “You had the red dress.”

“I still have it.”

“I meant at the reception.”

“I gathered.”

He rubbed his hands together.

“Sorry. I’m freezing and my brain has gone soft.”

“There’s a hotel back that way.”

“I saw it.”

“It looked closed.”

“I think it’s being renovated.”

A car approached, slowed at the roundabout, then continued in the other direction.

Martin raised a hand too late.

“You could hitchhike.”

“I don’t think my wife would enjoy hearing that.”

“You’re planning to tell her this entire story?”

“I’ve been married twenty-two years. She’ll want times and receipts.”

Lena smiled.

“My sister’s like that.”

“You have a sister?”

“Two.”

“Older?”

“One each side.”

“That sounds organized.”

“It wasn’t.”

They fell quiet again.

Lena checked the taxi app.

“Thirty-nine minutes now.”

“That’s encouraging.”

“It may be fictional.”

Martin leaned his head against the glass wall.

“I haven’t been on a bus in years.”

“I take one every day.”

“Where do you live?”

“North side.”

“I’m south.”

“So the taxi won’t solve everything.”

“We can split as far as the center.”

“That works.”

Martin took off his glasses and cleaned them on his shirt.

“You work with Anna?”

“With her brother’s sister. Sophie.”

“Right. I lost the family around the speeches.”

“There were too many speeches.”

“The uncle with the poem.”

“I went outside.”

“I stayed.”

“Was it long?”

“Six pages.”

Lena grimaced.

“He brought paper?”

“A folder.”

“He’d planned it.”

“He had section headings.”

She laughed.

Martin put his glasses back on.

“I saw you outside later. Near the pond.”

“I called my dad.”

“Everything all right?”

“He’s in hospital.”

Martin’s expression became attentive, though he didn’t move closer.

“Is it serious?”

“They’re doing tests. He fell in the supermarket.”

“Where?”

“Leeds.”

“That’s far.”

“Two hours.”

“Are you going tomorrow?”

“I was supposed to go tonight.”

Martin looked down the empty road.

“Oh.”

Lena checked the app again.

“Thirty-six minutes.”

“I can call my wife.”

“She’s home?”

“Probably.”

“It’s late.”

“She’d come.”

“You said she was tired.”

“She can decide.”

Lena shook her head.

“I’ll wait for the taxi.”

Martin took his phone out anyway, walked beyond the shelter, held it up, came back.

“No signal.”

“There’s a spot by the sign. I got two bars there.”

He crossed toward the garden center sign. Lena watched him pace beneath it with his phone raised.

After a minute he returned.

“She’s coming.”

“For you?”

“For both of us.”

“You didn’t need to do that.”

“She was awake.”

“How long?”

“Twenty-five minutes.”

Lena glanced at the taxi app.

“Mine now says fifty-two.”

“Cancel it.”

She did.

Martin sat down again.

“I should warn you, the car smells like dog.”

“I like dogs.”

“We don’t have one.”

Lena looked at him.

“My son spilled a protein drink in July. We’ve tried everything.”

She began laughing.

Martin laughed too, though less loudly.

Then they sat beneath the green light, watching the road for his wife’s headlights.
