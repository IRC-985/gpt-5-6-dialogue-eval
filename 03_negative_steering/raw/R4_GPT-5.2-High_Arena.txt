
## 1) A mother and her adult son

The kettle clicked off. Irene didn’t move right away. She stayed by the counter with her hand on the handle, listening to the rain tick the window.

“You left your jacket in the car,” she said.

Evan sat at the kitchen table with his laptop closed, both hands around a mug he hadn’t filled yet. “I’ll grab it later.”

“Later turns into tomorrow. Tomorrow turns into mildew.” She poured boiling water over a tea bag and watched the color spread. “You’re here three hours and the place looks like a shipping depot.”

“Boxes are temporary.”

“You said that when you moved back after the breakup. That was… what, February?”

“March.” He slid the mug toward her. “Could I get some too?”

She set the mug in front of him, tea bag string hanging over the rim. “You want honey?”

“Plain’s fine.”

Irene sat opposite him. She pushed the sugar bowl away with one finger, then pulled it back, then left it where it landed. “How was the drive?”

“Slow. The bridge was backed up. Some guy had a trailer and it was swaying. Everybody was scared of it.”

“Did you eat?”

“At Ben’s. We split a pizza.”

“Ben’s always feeding you like you’re fourteen.”

“He’s bored. He got one of those grills. He’s turning into a person who says ‘sear.’”

Irene looked toward the hallway where Evan’s old room was, the door half-open. “You took the desk out?”

“I need it at my place.”

“You have a place?”

“I signed the lease yesterday.”

Her eyebrows lifted and stayed there, as if they might be useful antennae. “Yesterday.”

“I was going to tell you.”

“You’re telling me now.”

He pulled the tea bag out and let it drip into the mug, patient with the dripping. “It’s on Juniper. It’s small. It’s… fine.”

“The one above the bakery?”

“The bakery moved. It’s a yoga studio now.”

“Everything’s a yoga studio.” Irene reached for her own mug. “Do they still have those narrow stairs?”

“Yeah. I’ll figure out furniture.”

She sipped, winced a little, and set the mug down. “You didn’t mention this on Sunday.”

“I didn’t want to talk about it on the phone. You get quiet.”

“I get quiet when I’m tired.”

“You get quiet when you’re thinking about the worst version of something.”

Irene’s mouth tightened, then eased. “When are you going?”

“Next weekend. Ben’s got a dolly. I’ll hire someone for the couch.”

“The couch you insisted on buying with your first bonus.”

“It’s a good couch.”

“It’s a couch that drinks up dust.”

Evan smiled and then let the smile go. “You’ll be okay here?”

“I’ve lived here a long time.”

“I mean… if something happens. The furnace does that clicking thing.”

“I already called someone about it.”

“You did?”

“They came Tuesday.” She watched his face. “They said it was a valve. They tightened it. It still clicks sometimes. It’s an old house. I’m an old woman. We click.”

He tapped the rim of his mug. “Mom.”

“I’m going to miss you in the mornings,” she said, and her voice made it sound like she was talking about an appliance being taken away. “The kettle feels loud when it’s only for one.”

“I can come by before work. Sometimes.”

“You say sometimes the way you said temporary.”

He leaned forward, elbows on the table. “I’m trying to do it. I’m trying to have a place and keep showing up. I’m tired of feeling like I’m borrowing air.”

Irene looked down at the tablecloth and smoothed a wrinkle with two fingers. “Do you have curtains?”

“I have… nothing.”

“I have curtains in the hall closet. The ones with the little leaves.”

“I hate those.”

“You hated them when you were a child.” She got up, slower than she would have ten years ago, and opened the hall closet door. “Hold this.”

Evan stood and took the flashlight she’d been using to look for something earlier. He shone it into the closet while she rummaged.

“These,” she said, tugging down a bundle of fabric and dust.

He sneezed. “They smell like attic.”

“They smell like being ready when you need them.” Irene pushed the curtains into his arms. “Take them. Wash them.”

He held them like a sleeping animal. “You’re really giving them away.”

“I won’t need them.”

Evan looked at the rain on the window again. “Can we eat something? I’m hungry again.”

“There’s soup.” Irene went to the stove, lifted the lid, and steam puffed up around her face. “Sit. Tell me about the lease. Tell me what you signed. Tell me if they’re raising rent every year.”

He sat. “Okay. The landlord’s name is Marta. She wears those running shoes with the bright soles even when she’s not running.”

Irene listened, stirring the soup, and for a while the kitchen sounded like an ordinary evening: rain, spoon against pot, and her son talking in a voice that kept going even when he didn’t know how to finish a thought.


## 2) Two old diplomats

The hotel bar had a pianist who played softly, like he’d been hired to apologize. Oleg kept his badge in his pocket and sat with his back to the room. Lena, who still wore her badge as if she’d forgotten it was there, folded her scarf on the table.

“They’ve changed the chairs,” Oleg said. “Lower. They want people to stay longer.”

“They want people to order dessert,” Lena said. “The summit budget is smaller. Somebody has to pay for the flowers.”

Oleg lifted his glass and watched the light through the amber. “You’re still drinking tea at midnight. You always did that.”

“I sleep in pieces,” she said. “Tea makes it feel organized.”

He nodded and glanced toward the ballroom doors. “Your panel went long.”

“The moderator asked everyone to tell a story. People love stories when the microphones are on.”

“You told one?”

“I talked about the customs officer in Jakarta who inspected my suitcase for books.”

Oleg’s mouth curved. “You had books about birds.”

“I had three.” Lena loosened the scarf and then tightened it again. “You look well.”

“I look like a man who knows which elevators are slow.”

“You’re still working?”

“Consulting. They send me memos and I send them back with fewer verbs.” He set the glass down carefully. “I went by the river this afternoon. The water’s lower than the last time.”

Lena made a small sound that could have been agreement or just her breathing. “I remember the first time we came to this city,” she said. “There was smoke everywhere. They were burning something by the stadium.”

“It was a protest,” Oleg said. “They burned tires.”

“And then we sat in that little café. You had a napkin and drew maps for me.”

“I drew circles. Everything was circles.”

“It helped.” Lena looked at him with a tired steadiness. “Do you miss it?”

Oleg waited, as if he might hear his own answer in the piano. “I miss the feeling that a bad week could turn into a good week with one phone call.”

“Your phone calls were famous.”

“I yelled more than I should have.” He rolled his shoulders. “These days, if I yell, my neck hurts.”

Lena’s hands rested on the table, palms down. “I saw Amir today.”

“Amir’s alive?”

“He’s alive and annoyed. He’s teaching now. He asked if you still keep that leather folder.”

“I have it. It smells like old airports.”

“He asked about the winter in Oslo,” Lena said. “He remembered you buying everyone those ridiculous wool socks.”

“They weren’t ridiculous.” Oleg smiled and then let it fade. “He came to my office once, didn’t he? When the briefing went wrong.”

“He stood in the doorway and looked as if he’d been carved from something.” Lena’s gaze shifted toward the bar shelves. “I think of him when people talk about courage. It isn’t loud. It’s standing still.”

Oleg tapped the table with one finger, slow. “They’re going to sign that statement tomorrow.”

“They’ll pose for pictures. Someone will make a joke about pens. People always make a joke about pens.”

Oleg leaned back. “Will it matter?”

Lena exhaled through her nose. “It will matter to the people who needed it three months ago. It will matter to the people who will need it next month. It will matter for a day to the newspapers.”

“That’s still something,” Oleg said.

Lena reached into her bag and pulled out a small envelope. “I brought you something.”

He didn’t take it right away. “What is it?”

“A stamp,” she said. “From the old series. The one you liked. The border’s crooked.”

Oleg finally reached out and accepted the envelope, turning it over with the care of a man holding a fragile insect. “You carried this around all day.”

“I forgot it was in my bag until I felt it.” Lena watched his hands. “I thought you might enjoy it.”

“I do.” He slipped the envelope into his jacket pocket and pressed it flat. “Thank you.”

The pianist stumbled into a melody that sounded familiar but never quite arrived. Oleg looked at Lena, and his eyes had the watery shine of fatigue.

“Do you go back tomorrow?” he asked.

“Morning flight.”

“Me too,” he said. “Same airline?”

“Probably.”

They sat with that for a moment, as if it were a decision that could change the shape of the week.

“I’ll walk you to the elevators,” Oleg said.

“You always did that,” Lena replied, and she picked up her scarf as if she’d been waiting to move for an hour.


## 3) Two schoolgirls

The late bus was late in a way that felt personal. Mia balanced her phone on her knee and kept refreshing the app. Sori stood with her backpack hugged to her front, watching cars spray water from the street.

“It says nine minutes,” Mia said.

“It said nine minutes five minutes ago,” Sori said.

Mia squinted at the gray sky. “My mom’s going to ask why I didn’t call.”

“You can call.”

“I don’t want to hear her voice with the tired in it.”

Sori shifted her weight. Her shoes were soaked at the edges. “My brother’s picking me up. He said he’d come if it rains hard. He thinks rain is a disaster.”

Mia laughed softly. “My dad thinks rain is romantic. He stands on the porch and stares at it like he’s in a movie.”

Sori tucked her chin into her hoodie. “Did you do the math?”

“I did some.” Mia scrolled through messages. “Kara sent me a picture of her worksheet. She wrote the answers with glitter pen.”

“That’s weird,” Sori said. “Glitter for math.”

Mia’s face brightened. “She’s trying to make it fun.”

Sori watched a bus go by on the far side, heading the wrong direction. “Coach was mad today.”

“Coach is mad every day.”

“He made us run extra laps.” Sori’s voice thinned a little. “He kept looking at me when he said it.”

Mia stopped scrolling. “Because you missed a pass?”

“I missed two.” Sori looked down at her own hands. Her nails were bitten. “My legs felt heavy. I ate chips for lunch.”

Mia nudged her shoulder with a gentle bump. “Everybody eats chips.”

Sori didn’t answer right away. She glanced at Mia’s phone. “Did you see what Alina posted?”

Mia’s mouth tightened. “The thing about the cafeteria?”

“The thing about you,” Sori said, quiet.

Mia’s eyes flicked away. “It was stupid.”

“It’s mean.”

Mia watched a puddle gather under the bench. “She wants people to laugh.”

Sori said, “People did laugh.”

“Yeah.” Mia swallowed. “I heard them.”

Sori pulled at the strap of her backpack. “Do you want me to say something to her?”

Mia shook her head quickly. “It’ll get bigger.”

Sori stared at the wet sidewalk, thinking. “I can sit with you tomorrow. At lunch.”

Mia’s shoulders dropped a little. “You already sit with me.”

“Then I’ll sit closer,” Sori said. “Like, where people see it.”

Mia’s eyes went shiny and she blinked it away fast. “Thanks.”

A car slowed at the curb and a woman leaned out the window, looking at them. Sori stiffened, then relaxed as the car rolled on.

Mia spoke as if she’d decided to step around the subject. “Are you going to the dance?”

Sori made a face. “My mom said it’s a waste of money.”

“It’s ten dollars.”

“My mom can make ten dollars sound like a mountain.” Sori hesitated. “Are you going?”

Mia shrugged. “Kara wants to. She keeps practicing that thing where you take pictures with your tongue out.”

Sori’s eyebrows rose. “She does that?”

“She does it like she invented it.” Mia tilted her head. “Do you want to go together? Like… as friends. Not in a weird way.”

Sori’s laugh came out small. “Everything is weird at school.”

“Yeah,” Mia said. “But we can still go.”

Sori looked toward the road. Headlights smeared in the rain. “If my brother drops me off, he’ll sit in the parking lot to spy.”

Mia grinned. “He’ll bring snacks.”

“He would.” Sori’s expression softened. “Okay. I’ll ask.”

Mia’s phone buzzed. She glanced down. “It says three minutes.”

Sori sighed, a long breath that fogged the air in front of her. “Your hair is going to get frizzy.”

Mia touched the end of her ponytail. “It already is.”

Sori said, “You have that shampoo that smells like apples.”

“My mom buys it.”

“It smells good,” Sori said.

Mia looked out at the rain again. “Do you think people stop caring about posts? Like, do they forget?”

Sori didn’t answer fast. “People find new things to talk about. They get hungry. They get tired. They think about themselves. It changes.”

Mia nodded slowly, as if she could file that away for later. The bus finally appeared, lumbering toward them with its lights bright in the rain, and both girls stepped closer to the curb, shoulder to shoulder.


## 4) A married couple after an affair

The living room lamp was on, though it was still early evening. The light made the room look staged, as if someone might come in and judge the furniture.

Nina stood by the bookshelf with a folded towel in her hands. She kept folding it again, tighter each time.

Mark came in from the hallway carrying his phone and a charger. He paused near the couch, as if the space had turned into a line he had to cross.

“I made pasta,” Nina said.

Mark nodded. “Smells good.”

“It’s in the pot. I didn’t put it in bowls.”

“I can do it.”

They both stayed where they were. The towel in Nina’s hands became a small square. She unfolded it and folded it again.

Mark’s voice was quiet. “Emma’s homework is in her backpack. She said she finished.”

“I’ll check later,” Nina said. “Did you bring the mail in?”

“It’s on the counter.” He looked at the bookshelf, at the towel. “I called the plumber. He’s coming Friday.”

Nina’s eyes flicked toward the ceiling, as if she could hear pipes from there. “Okay.”

Mark’s throat moved when he swallowed. “I don’t want the kids to hear us.”

“They’re at your sister’s,” Nina said. “She’s taking them to that trampoline place.”

“Right.” Mark glanced toward the window and the dark outside. “I keep thinking I hear the garage door.”

Nina put the towel down on the back of the armchair. “Did you eat lunch today?”

“A sandwich.”

She nodded, as if she’d asked because lunch mattered more than anything else. “You look tired.”

“I didn’t sleep much.”

Nina’s hands were empty now. She rubbed her palms together. “When you went to her apartment… was it always after work?”

Mark’s eyes dropped to the carpet. “Usually.”

“How did you have time?” Nina asked. The question came out flatter than she expected.

He stared at the charger in his hand. “I left early sometimes. I said I had meetings.”

Nina looked toward the kitchen doorway. “So you’d walk out the door and you already knew.”

Mark’s shoulders rose and fell. “Yeah.”

She let out a short breath. “I keep looking at the clock and thinking about the days you said traffic was bad.”

Mark’s mouth opened, then closed. He shifted his weight, heel to toe. “I can sleep in the guest room.”

Nina’s gaze stayed on the kitchen doorway. “You already did,” she said softly.

Mark flinched, then nodded. “I did.”

Silence sat with them for a few seconds, thick and ordinary, like dust in sunlight.

Nina said, “I told Jenna.”

Mark looked up. “Your sister?”

“Yesterday.” Nina picked up a framed photo from the shelf and straightened it even though it was already straight. “She came over. She stood in the kitchen and kept wiping the counter with her sleeve.”

Mark’s voice went hoarse. “What did she say?”

“She asked if you were sick.” Nina’s lips tightened. “She asked if you hit your head.”

Mark’s shoulders sagged. “I’m not sick.”

“I know,” Nina said, and her voice cracked on the last word. She pressed her fingertips to her forehead and held them there. “I keep trying to think of a reason that makes me feel steady.”

Mark stepped forward one small step, then stopped. “I can answer questions.”

Nina dropped her hand. “I don’t want you to be helpful. I want to wake up and have this be a regular Tuesday.”

Mark’s eyes reddened. He blinked hard. “I ended it.”

Nina stared at him. “When?”

“Last week. Before you found out.” He held the charger like it was proof of something. “I told her I wasn’t coming back.”

Nina’s jaw clenched. “And then you came home and sat with me on the couch and watched that baking show.”

Mark’s eyes shut for a second. “Yeah.”

Nina walked to the couch and sat on the far end, careful, as if sitting too hard might break it. “Do you still have her number?”

Mark didn’t move. “It’s in my phone.”

Nina’s fingers gripped the cushion seam. “I want you to delete it.”

Mark nodded. He unlocked his phone with shaking hands, looking down at the screen for a long time. His thumb hovered, then moved. He swallowed again.

Nina watched him, her breathing shallow. “Did you ever talk about me with her?”

Mark’s face tightened. “Sometimes.”

Nina stared at the lamp light on the wall. “Okay.”

Mark’s voice got smaller. “I’m sorry.”

Nina’s eyes filled and she didn’t wipe them. “I know you are,” she said, and her voice sounded like it had been scraped raw. “I’m still here.”

He took another step. He stopped at the edge of the coffee table, as if the table had authority.

Nina said, “We have to figure out breakfast. The kids have school. They’ll want cereal and they’ll want someone to sign a form and they’ll talk about trampolines.”

Mark nodded slowly. “I can do breakfast.”

Nina looked at him then, finally. “You can,” she said. “And I’m going to stand there and watch you do it.”

Mark’s eyes filled too. He looked down quickly, as if he could hide it in the carpet.

“I’m going to dish up the pasta,” Nina said. “We should eat something.”

Mark whispered, “Okay.”

She got up and went into the kitchen. He stayed where he was for a moment, then followed, keeping a careful distance, like the air between them had turned fragile and sharp.


## 5) An officer and a subordinate

The motor pool smelled of diesel and wet canvas. A line of vehicles sat with their hoods up like open mouths. Lieutenant Harris had a clipboard tucked under his arm and a pen behind his ear.

Corporal Diaz jogged up, stopping at a respectful distance. His uniform had a smear of grease on the thigh.

“Lieutenant,” Diaz said.

Harris looked him over. “Your team’s on the radios?”

“Sergeant Mullins has them laid out. We found three with dead batteries. We’re swapping.”

Harris flipped a page. “I want serial numbers on my desk by sixteen-hundred.”

“Got it.” Diaz hesitated, then added, “The alternator on Two-Four is making a sound again.”

“Maintenance signed off on Two-Four.”

“They signed off last week,” Diaz said. “It’s louder now.”

Harris looked past Diaz at the vehicle in question, then back. “Have the driver write it up. Park it at the end. I don’t want it in the inspection line.”

Diaz nodded, then didn’t leave. His eyes stayed fixed somewhere near Harris’s shoulder.

Harris noticed and waited him out. “Say it.”

Diaz took a breath. “I need to put in for leave next Thursday and Friday.”

Harris’s pen paused midair. “For what.”

“My sister’s having her baby,” Diaz said. “She’s in El Paso. She asked me to come. My mom’s already there.”

Harris stared at the clipboard. The schedule was a grid of names and gaps that never stayed gaps. “We have the field exercise.”

“I know,” Diaz said. “I can make up the hours this weekend. I can stay late.”

Harris looked up. “Who covers your lane at the range next week.”

Diaz answered quickly. “Private Chen has the paperwork down. He can run the sign-in. Sergeant Mullins can supervise.”

Harris’s jaw tightened slightly. “You’re asking me to move pieces around with this inspection on top.”

Diaz swallowed. “Yes, sir.”

A forklift beeped somewhere behind them, backing up. Harris turned his head to check the lane, then faced Diaz again.

“Is this your first niece or nephew,” Harris asked.

Diaz’s face changed, the tension easing around his eyes. “First.”

Harris nodded once. “How long is the drive.”

“Fourteen hours,” Diaz said. “I’d fly if I could. Tickets are crazy.”

Harris looked down at the schedule again. He scratched a note in the margin. “You’ll need to file it today.”

“I can do it after radios,” Diaz said. “I have the form printed.”

Harris exhaled through his nose. “Diaz, you’re solid. You’ve been solid since you got here. That means people lean on you.”

Diaz’s shoulders shifted as if the words had weight. “I try.”

“I’m going to approve one day,” Harris said. “Friday. You take Friday. You come back Saturday night. You’re here Sunday for loadout.”

Diaz’s face fell slightly, then steadied. “I can do that.”

Harris held up a hand, palm down, keeping the pace of the conversation. “You’ll talk to Mullins yourself. You’ll hand me a coverage plan with names. If it falls apart, it falls on you.”

“Yes, sir.”

Harris glanced at the grease smear again. “Get cleaned up before the colonel walks through. He notices everything.”

Diaz looked down at his thigh, surprised to see it. “I didn’t even— I’ll fix it.”

Harris’s voice softened a fraction. “When you get there, send one photo. Nothing fancy. Just proof the kid showed up.”

Diaz’s mouth twitched like he wanted to smile but didn’t know if it was allowed. “Yes, sir. Thank you.”

Harris turned toward the vehicles, already moving. “And tell whoever keeps leaving water bottles in the Humvees that I’m done finding science experiments under seats.”

Diaz took a step to follow, then peeled off toward the radio table, calling over his shoulder, “I’ll handle it.”

Harris didn’t answer. He made a note on the clipboard and kept walking, the pen tapping lightly against the paper in time with his steps.


## 6) Two people who barely know each other

The elevator stopped between floors with a gentle shudder, like a sigh. The overhead light stayed on. A small fan kept pushing warm air around.

Tara stared at the numbers above the door. They didn’t change. She pressed the “Door Open” button anyway, then the “Call” button.

A man in a navy jacket stood near the panel with a canvas tote at his feet. He had his phone out, screen dark, then lit again as he tried it.

“No service in here,” he said.

Tara kept her hand on the call button. “It usually takes a minute. This building does it sometimes.”

He nodded, listening to the elevator’s quiet hum. “I’m Owen. I’m here for… I’m going to the dentist upstairs.”

Tara glanced at him. “I’m Tara. I work on three.”

Owen shifted his tote with his shoe. “So you’ve been in this elevator more than me.”

“Too much,” Tara said. Her laugh came out short. “You’d think I’d stop trusting it.”

Owen leaned his shoulder lightly against the wall, careful not to look too comfortable. “How long does it usually take for someone to notice.”

Tara looked at the little speaker grille above the buttons. “They get an alert. In theory.”

Owen’s eyes moved around the ceiling corner where a camera sat. “That thing watching us.”

Tara followed his gaze. “My coworker waves at it when it stalls. She says it makes her feel less stuck.”

Owen raised his hand a few inches, then let it fall. “I’m not a waver.”

Tara checked her watch. “I’m going to be late to a meeting. We’re supposed to talk about printer toner like it’s treasure.”

Owen’s mouth curved faintly. “Dentists charge you a fee if you’re late. It’s impressive.”

Tara put her back against the wall. The air was warmer now, or maybe she was noticing it. “What floor were you going to.”

“Seven,” Owen said. “I took the morning off. My boss thinks I’m at a training.”

Tara looked at his tote. “Training for teeth.”

He glanced down. “It’s embarrassing to explain. They ask questions.”

Tara nodded slowly. “My boss asks questions too. He asks them in a cheerful way that makes you want to disappear.”

Owen shifted his weight. “Do you like your job.”

Tara thought about it, then shrugged. “I like parts of it. I like the people who bring snacks. I like when a spreadsheet balances and nobody notices. I don’t like the meetings where someone says we need to ‘circle back.’”

Owen’s shoulders loosened. “I work at the bookstore on Maple. I do inventory and orders. People come in and tell you about their lives because they’re holding a paperback.”

“That sounds kind of nice,” Tara said.

“It’s nice until somebody yells about a return from 2019.” Owen looked at the seam where the elevator doors met. “I should have taken the stairs.”

Tara tilted her head. “Seven flights.”

He inhaled and exhaled. “Okay, I should have taken the stairs and regretted it.”

The speaker crackled faintly, then nothing. Tara pressed the call button again, longer this time.

Owen watched her hand. “Does it help to keep pressing.”

“It helps me,” Tara said. “My brain likes a task.”

Owen nodded, understanding more than he said. “I get that.”

Tara glanced at his hands. He had a faint line of ink on his thumb, like he’d been marking boxes. “Do you live nearby.”

“Over the bridge,” Owen said. “I’m helping my aunt this week. She’s moving into assisted living. I’m here because the dentist is near her place and she made the appointment for me like I’m a kid.”

Tara’s expression softened. “Moving is a lot.”

“She’s been boxing up her kitchen for two months,” Owen said. “Every time I go over, there’s less of her house and more cardboard.”

Tara nodded. “My mom keeps jars. She says they’re useful.”

Owen looked at Tara with a little more attention. “My aunt keeps recipe cards. She won’t throw them out. Half of them are stained.”

Tara smiled, small and genuine. “Stains mean they got used.”

The elevator jerked slightly. The numbers above the door blinked, then changed by one, then stopped again. Tara held her breath without meaning to.

Owen lifted his tote an inch, then set it down. “It moved.”

Tara let out her breath. “It’s thinking.”

A clearer crackle came through the speaker. A muffled voice said something they couldn’t make out.

Tara leaned toward it. “Hello? We’re stuck between three and four.”

Owen leaned in too, close enough that Tara could smell laundry detergent on his jacket. The speaker hissed and the voice returned, louder. “Maintenance is on the way. Stay calm. Twenty minutes.”

Tara straightened. “Twenty minutes.”

Owen looked at the ceiling, then back at Tara. “Well. We have time.”

Tara nodded, then sat carefully on the floor, smoothing her skirt under her. “Do you want a mint. I have mints.”

Owen hesitated, then crouched down, keeping a polite distance. “Sure. For my training.”

Tara held out the tin. “Pick one.”

Owen took a mint and unwrapped it slowly. “When this opens, I’m going to walk to seven.”

Tara watched him, then looked back at the door. “I might take the stairs too. It’ll make my meeting more interesting.”

Owen put the mint in his mouth and spoke around it. “If you see a bookstore guy on the stairs, that’s me.”

Tara nodded. “I’ll wave at the camera if we get stuck again.”

Owen’s eyes warmed. “That sounds like a habit.”

They sat in the warm, humming box and waited, their conversation settling into quiet, each of them listening for the next sound of motion.
