
1. Mother and Adult Son

The refrigerator had been moved away from the wall and stood in the middle of the kitchen with its cord trailing behind it.

Daniel stopped in the doorway.

“Why is the refrigerator there?”

His mother was sitting at the table with a screwdriver, three batteries, an unopened packet of biscuits, and the instruction booklet for a smoke detector.

“I dropped an earring.”

“Behind the refrigerator?”

“It went under something.”

“You moved this by yourself?”

“I got it started with the broom handle.”

Daniel put down the pharmacy bag.

“Where’s the earring?”

“Somewhere underneath. I lost interest.”

He looked at her.

“You could have called me.”

“You were working.”

“I work most days.”

“Yes. So there would have been a long wait.”

He went behind the refrigerator and unplugged it.

“Don’t scratch the floor.”

“I’m trying not to.”

“The boards are soft there.”

“I remember.”

“They’ve always been soft. Your father wanted tile.”

Daniel leaned his shoulder against the appliance and pushed. It moved several inches.

“Can you see anything?”

His mother bent, although from her chair she could see nothing.

“There’s probably fifty years of dirt.”

“About three months.”

“That’s optimistic.”

He crouched and reached underneath.

“A bottle cap. A pencil. Something I’m not touching.”

“Use the kitchen tongs.”

“I’m not putting kitchen tongs under there.”

“They go in the dishwasher.”

He retrieved the pencil.

“You still use these?”

“That one’s been missing since Christmas.”

“Did you report it?”

“I kept hoping the police would take an interest.”

Daniel sat back on his heels.

“Blue stone?”

“Green.”

“You said blue on the phone.”

“I didn’t phone you about the earring.”

“No, last month. You showed me the pair.”

“That pair was blue.”

“So this is another pair.”

“I own several earrings, Daniel.”

“Apparently one less now.”

She opened the biscuits and pushed them toward him.

“Eat something.”

“I had lunch.”

“At what time?”

“Two.”

“It’s nearly six.”

“I’m fine.”

“You look pale.”

“I’m kneeling behind a refrigerator.”

“That has nothing to do with your face.”

He reached farther under the appliance.

“Here.”

He held up a small gold hoop with a green glass bead.

His mother squinted at it.

“That isn’t mine.”

Daniel stared at the earring in his hand.

“You live alone.”

“I have lived alone for eleven years. The kitchen existed before that.”

“Whose is it?”

“How would I know?”

“You moved the refrigerator for an earring and there’s a completely different earring underneath it.”

“Yes.”

“Where did you lose yours?”

She glanced toward the sink.

“Possibly in the sitting room.”

He stood slowly.

“Mum.”

“I thought it might have rolled.”

“From the sitting room into the kitchen?”

“It was a busy morning.”

Daniel plugged the refrigerator back in and began pushing it toward the wall.

“You wanted me to come over.”

“I had a prescription for you to collect.”

“You could have told me you wanted company.”

“I wanted my prescription.”

“It’s on the counter.”

She looked toward the pharmacy bag.

“Good.”

He pushed the refrigerator the last inch.

“There. Done.”

“Thank you.”

Daniel sat opposite her.

She poured him tea from a pot he had not noticed.

“You made tea before I got here.”

“I generally make tea in the afternoon.”

“Two cups.”

“One cup was for rinsing the pot.”

He took the cup nearest him.

“That’s a terrible lie.”

She busied herself with the smoke detector.

“This thing keeps chirping.”

“I changed that battery last week.”

“It chirped yesterday.”

“Where?”

“Hall.”

“That one’s in the bedroom.”

“There are too many of them.”

“There are three.”

“In a small house, three is a population.”

He picked up the instruction booklet.

“Which one is chirping?”

“I don’t know now.”

“We can wait.”

“For a noise?”

“For the chirp.”

She ate half a biscuit.

Daniel watched her.

After a while she said, “You could stay for supper.”

“What are you making?”

“There’s soup.”

“What kind?”

“Orange.”

“That narrows it down.”

“Carrot, probably.”

He smiled despite himself.

“I’ll stay.”

“Good. You can put the refrigerator back properly after.”

“It is back properly.”

She looked past him toward the wall.

“There’s a gap.”

“There’s supposed to be a gap.”

“Oh.”

They sat in silence.

From somewhere upstairs came a sharp electronic chirp.

His mother raised one finger.

“There.”

Daniel looked at the ceiling.

“That’s definitely the bedroom.”

“I told you the hall.”

He stood.

“Stay here.”

“I’m not planning an expedition.”

He took the batteries and headed for the stairs.

Behind him she called, “And see if there’s a green earring by the bed.”

Daniel stopped.

“The one you actually lost?”

“It may be there.”

He looked back.

She was pouring herself more tea.

“Soup at seven,” she said.

He went upstairs.

2. Two Old Diplomats

The rain had emptied the hotel terrace. Only Ambassador Varga and Sir Malcolm Reed remained beneath the canvas awning, each with a blanket across his knees.

The waiter set down two coffees.

Varga watched him retreat.

“He thinks we are going to die here.”

“He’s hoping we leave before dinner service.”

“At our age the distinction becomes administrative.”

Malcolm stirred his coffee.

“Your train is tomorrow?”

“Eleven forty.”

“Mine is nine.”

“You always liked to leave early.”

“I like breakfast on trains.”

“You like bad coffee in paper cups.”

“It gives the journey a purpose.”

Varga took a folded newspaper from his coat pocket.

“Have you read this?”

“I have spent forty years reading newspapers I didn’t want to read. Retirement should offer some protection.”

“They’ve appointed Markovic.”

Malcolm’s spoon stopped.

“To Vienna?”

“Yes.”

“Ah.”

“You remember him.”

“Small man. Large watch.”

“He was thirty when we knew him.”

“He was already wearing the watch.”

Varga folded the paper again.

“They asked me for a comment.”

“And?”

“I said I remembered his father.”

“That will please him.”

“It was true.”

Malcolm drank his coffee.

“His father once kept me at a border crossing for six hours.”

“For the radio equipment?”

“For having a second suitcase.”

“You had radio equipment.”

“In the first suitcase.”

Varga laughed, then coughed into his handkerchief for several seconds.

Malcolm looked toward the rain.

“You should go inside.”

“It’s warmer here than inside. They refrigerate the lobby.”

“You’ve become thin.”

“My tailor has become generous.”

“Are you still seeing that doctor?”

“Every Thursday. He has three photographs of sailing boats and no boat.”

“Perhaps he dislikes sailing.”

“Then the photographs are disturbing.”

A gust of rain tapped against the awning.

Malcolm pulled his blanket higher.

“Markovic in Vienna. I suppose they want somebody patient.”

“They want somebody who won’t embarrass the ministry.”

“That reduces the field.”

Varga looked at him.

“You never liked him.”

“I barely knew him.”

“You refused to take him onto the delegation in ’98.”

“He had a habit of bringing his wife into meetings.”

“She was an interpreter.”

“She was a sculptor.”

“She spoke excellent French.”

“She spoke excellent French while making sculptures.”

Varga smiled.

“She was very beautiful.”

“Yes.”

“You see? You remember.”

“I remember everybody who caused scheduling problems.”

They drank in silence for a while.

In the square beyond the hotel, water ran silver along the tram tracks.

Varga said, “Do you ever think we should have signed in May?”

Malcolm put down his cup.

“Sometimes.”

“I think about it more now.”

“We had instructions.”

“We had room.”

“Some.”

“Enough.”

Malcolm rubbed his thumb along the cup handle.

“The other side wanted the port.”

“They wanted language about the port.”

“They would have used it.”

“They used the port anyway.”

“Yes.”

A trolley bell sounded somewhere in the rain.

Varga said, “Nineteen people were killed in June.”

“I remember.”

“I attended one funeral. The boy’s mother kept thanking me for coming.”

Malcolm’s face remained still.

“I went to three.”

“You never told me.”

“There wasn’t much to report.”

“Why three?”

“I was nearby.”

Varga nodded and folded the newspaper smaller.

“I used to think memory would simplify things.”

“Mine loses names and keeps entire seating plans.”

“I can still see that room.”

“So can I.”

“The curtains were yellow.”

“Brown.”

“Yellow.”

“They may have been yellow by daylight. We were always there after midnight.”

Varga considered this.

“Do you remember the sandwiches?”

“Cheese.”

“Ham.”

“There were cheese ones.”

“For the vegetarians.”

“There were no vegetarians.”

“Then perhaps they were for you.”

Malcolm smiled faintly.

“My wife says I’ve begun telling stories about negotiations to the grandchildren.”

“Do they listen?”

“They ask whether there were spies.”

“What do you tell them?”

“That most spies are disappointing.”

“That’s irresponsible. They’ll grow up without glamour.”

“I’ve tried giving them stamps.”

Varga looked pleased.

“Stamps are good.”

“They threw them out.”

“Barbarians.”

The rain strengthened. Neither man moved.

After a minute Malcolm said, “If we had signed in May, something else might have happened in June.”

“Yes.”

“We would still be sitting here inventing another regret.”

“Probably.”

Malcolm reached for the pot and filled Varga’s cup.

“Are you going to send Markovic a note?”

“I thought I might.”

“Send one.”

“Warm?”

“Civil.”

“I can manage civil.”

“You made a career of it.”

Varga slipped the newspaper back into his coat.

“Come on my train tomorrow.”

“Why?”

“Breakfast.”

“You said the coffee was bad.”

“It is. We can complain about it for three hours.”

Malcolm looked out at the rain.

“Eleven forty?”

“Platform six, according to the board.”

“All right.”

“Good.”

Neither mentioned the earlier train again.

3. Two Schoolgirls

Mina had one shoe off and was balancing on the cold pipe behind the gym.

“Hold the fence.”

“I am holding it.”

“You’re touching it with two fingers.”

Leah wrapped both hands around the wire mesh.

“There. If it falls over now, I’m going with it.”

“It won’t fall.”

“You said that about the shelf in art.”

“The shelf was already loose.”

Mina pushed herself higher.

Her skirt caught on a twist of wire.

“Wait.”

“I’m waiting.”

“No, my skirt.”

Leah looked up.

“Oh.”

“Can you unhook it?”

“You’re too high.”

“Stand on the brick.”

“That brick has moss.”

“Use the dry side.”

Leah stepped onto it, reached up, and began picking at the fabric.

“You’ve ripped it.”

“My mum’s going to kill me.”

“It’s tiny.”

“She notices tiny things. She found a pen mark inside my pocket.”

“Why was she looking inside your pocket?”

“She washes clothes like she’s investigating a murder.”

The skirt came free.

Mina climbed another foot.

“Can you see it?”

“See what?”

“The ball.”

“You’re the one looking.”

“Oh. Right.”

Leah squinted through the fence.

“It might be near the bins.”

“That’s a plastic bag.”

“Maybe farther left.”

“There’s the science block.”

“I know where the science block is.”

A whistle sounded faintly from the playing field.

Leah glanced around the corner.

“Miss Hargreaves is still with Year Nine.”

“Good.”

“This is taking ages.”

“You kicked it.”

“I passed it to you.”

“It went over my head.”

“Your head is small.”

Mina put a foot on the top horizontal rail.

The fence gave a metallic groan.

“Mina.”

“It’s fine.”

“If you fall, I’m going home.”

“You can’t go home. We have geography.”

“If you break your spine, geography is probably cancelled.”

Mina swung one leg over.

“Don’t look.”

“I’m literally keeping watch.”

“My tights.”

“I can see your shorts.”

“Still.”

She disappeared down the other side with a thump.

Leah pressed her face to the mesh.

“You alive?”

“Yes.”

“Did you land on the nettles?”

“No.”

A pause.

“Actually yes.”

Leah began laughing.

“Stop.”

“I’m trying.”

“They’re all over my ankle.”

“Rub a dock leaf on it.”

“Which one’s dock?”

“The big one.”

“They’re all big.”

“I saw my grandma do it.”

“Your grandma also drinks hot water.”

“That’s for her stomach.”

Mina emerged from behind the bins holding a red football.

“Got it.”

“Good. Come back.”

She looked at the fence.

“I don’t think I can.”

“You just climbed it.”

“This side has no pipe.”

Leah stared.

“There’s a gate.”

“It’s locked.”

“Check.”

Mina walked out of sight.

A moment later Leah heard a squeal of hinges.

“It’s open.”

“You’re joking.”

Mina appeared through the gate, football tucked under her arm.

Neither girl spoke for three seconds.

Then Leah sat down on the brick.

“We’re idiots.”

Mina came over and sat beside her.

“My ankle hurts.”

“Let me see.”

“It’s going red.”

Leah bent over it.

“There are little bumps.”

“Am I going to get a rash?”

“You have a rash.”

“Like a bad one.”

“It looks like nettles.”

Mina tugged her sock up.

“Don’t tell anyone.”

“Fine.”

“And the skirt.”

“I wasn’t planning to announce your skirt.”

They sat behind the gym with the ball between them.

From the field came another whistle.

Leah said, “Are you still going to Sophie’s on Saturday?”

“I don’t know.”

“You told her yes.”

“My cousin’s coming.”

“The cousin from Birmingham?”

“Coventry.”

“The one with the eyebrow thing?”

“She had it pierced. Her school made her take it out.”

“Does it close?”

“I think so.”

Leah picked dried mud off her trainer.

“Sophie invited Amira.”

“I thought they hated each other.”

“They were together at lunch.”

Mina looked toward the corner of the building.

“Maybe they’re fine now.”

“Maybe.”

“I don’t really want to go.”

“You were excited yesterday.”

“I was excited about the pizza.”

“We could eat pizza somewhere else.”

“My mum won’t let me go into town at night.”

“My dad might.”

“Your dad lets you do anything.”

“He asks where I’m going.”

“After you leave.”

Leah smiled.

“We could say we’re studying.”

“That never works at my house.”

“It works at mine because nobody believes I study anyway.”

Mina rolled the ball under her palm.

“We should go back.”

“In a minute.”

“Miss Hargreaves will notice.”

“She has thirty-one people.”

“She knows everybody’s shoes.”

Mina looked down at her bare foot.

“Oh.”

“Where’s your shoe?”

They both looked at the pipe behind the gym.

The shoe was still wedged there.

Leah closed her eyes.

“We have seven minutes.”

Mina stood.

“Hold the fence.”

Leah grabbed it with both hands.

4. A Married Couple After an Affair

Peter was cutting mushrooms when Anna came into the kitchen.

She put her keys in the bowl by the door.

“The traffic was awful.”

“There’s wine open.”

“I saw the bottle.”

He continued slicing.

She took off her coat and hung it on the back of a chair.

“What are you making?”

“Pasta.”

“With those?”

“Yes.”

“I bought chicken.”

“It smelled strange.”

“I bought it yesterday.”

“You can check it if you want. It’s in the bin.”

Anna opened the cupboard for a glass.

“I’m not checking chicken in the bin.”

Peter nodded.

The knife moved steadily against the board.

She poured wine.

“Did Emma call?”

“About five.”

“What did she want?”

“Money.”

“How much?”

“Eighty.”

“For what?”

“Shoes for the winter formal.”

“She has shoes.”

“I gave her forty.”

Anna leaned against the counter.

“Why forty?”

“It seemed like a number I could survive.”

“She’ll ask me for the rest.”

“Probably.”

“You could have told her to wear the black ones.”

“I don’t remember the black ones.”

“She wore them to your mother’s birthday.”

“There were a lot of shoes there.”

Anna drank.

“Peter.”

He put the mushrooms in the pan.

“Yes.”

“Claire emailed me.”

His hand stopped briefly over the stove.

“When?”

“This afternoon.”

“All right.”

“She wants the camera back.”

He lowered the heat.

“I thought I returned it.”

“She says the charger is here too.”

“I can look.”

“She asked me.”

Peter turned toward her.

“I can put them in a bag.”

Anna stared into her glass.

“I told her she could come Friday.”

He looked at the clock on the oven.

“What time?”

“Six.”

“I’ll be here.”

“She knows.”

“All right.”

The mushrooms hissed in the pan.

Anna went to the table and sat.

“Emma’s coming home Sunday?”

“Saturday afternoon. I can pick her up.”

“She told me Sunday.”

“She may have changed it.”

“She changes everything three times.”

“She’s nineteen.”

“She’s been doing it since she was six.”

Peter added garlic.

Anna watched him.

“Do you want Claire to come when you’re here?”

“I don’t know.”

“I could leave the things downstairs.”

“I can give them to her.”

“She may prefer downstairs.”

“Then downstairs.”

Anna stood and opened the refrigerator.

“There’s no milk.”

“I used the last of it.”

“I wanted coffee.”

“There’s oat milk.”

“I hate oat milk.”

“I can go out after dinner.”

“No. I’ll get it tomorrow.”

He dropped pasta into boiling water.

She closed the refrigerator.

“Did she tell you she was emailing me?”

“No.”

“When did you last speak to her?”

“June.”

Anna’s face tightened slightly.

“That was after.”

“Yes.”

“What was that conversation about?”

“Her camera. And a book.”

“You still have the book?”

“I think it’s in the office.”

Anna went quiet.

Peter stirred the pasta.

After a while she said, “I keep finding objects.”

“I’ll collect anything that belongs to her.”

“There’s a blue scarf in the hall cupboard.”

“That’s Emma’s.”

“It isn’t.”

Peter considered.

“I’ll put it with the camera.”

Anna sat again.

“I hate that sentence.”

He turned off the burner.

“I’ll put everything on the table tonight.”

She rubbed her forehead.

“Fine.”

He drained the pasta.

The kitchen filled with steam.

“Do you want parmesan?”

“Yes.”

He grated cheese over both bowls.

Anna watched him carry them to the table.

They sat.

She took two bites.

“This is good.”

Peter ate without looking up.

“I forgot salt.”

“It’s fine.”

After another minute Anna said, “At work today, Martin had a stroke.”

Peter stopped eating.

“Martin from accounts?”

“Yes.”

“The tall one?”

“He collapsed beside the photocopier.”

“Is he alive?”

“Yes. They took him to St. George’s.”

“How old is he?”

“Fifty-eight.”

Peter sat back.

“My age next year.”

“I thought of that.”

“Was he conscious?”

“By the time they took him out.”

Anna pushed a mushroom around her plate.

“His wife got there before the ambulance left. Somebody must have called her right away.”

Peter looked at her.

She kept her eyes on the bowl.

“He looked annoyed,” she said. “That’s what I remember. He was on the floor and his face wasn’t working properly, and he looked furious because everyone was standing around him.”

Peter reached for the wine bottle.

“Do you want more?”

“A little.”

He poured.

Anna said, “I don’t know why I’m telling you this.”

Peter set the bottle down.

“Martin’s been in your office for twenty years.”

“Twenty-three.”

They ate.

Outside, a car door slammed in the street.

Anna said, “Friday, I might stay.”

“For Claire?”

“Yes.”

Peter’s fork paused.

“All right.”

“I want to be there.”

He nodded.

She looked at him for a long moment, then reached for the parmesan.

“There’s almost none left.”

“I’ll add it to the milk.”

“And coffee.”

“We have coffee.”

“We have decaf.”

Peter got up, found a pen, and wrote on the shopping pad.

Milk. Parmesan. Coffee.

Anna watched him write.

“Emma will want cereal.”

He added cereal.

Neither of them mentioned Friday again during dinner.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

Lieutenant Sayeed found Corporal Mercer sitting behind the communications truck with his helmet between his boots.

“You’re supposed to be at the eastern post.”

Mercer looked up.

“Sergeant Vale sent Rowe.”

“Why?”

“He said I was shaking.”

Sayeed crouched.

Mercer’s hands were clasped so tightly his knuckles had gone white.

“How long?”

“Maybe ten minutes.”

“Before Vale saw?”

“I don’t know, sir.”

“Can you feel your fingers?”

“Yes.”

“Any dizziness?”

“A bit.”

“Chest?”

“Fine.”

Sayeed sat on an ammunition crate.

“Drink.”

Mercer lifted his canteen.

“I already did.”

“Again.”

Mercer took a mouthful.

The generator beside the truck coughed and settled back into its steady rattle.

Sayeed said, “Tell me where your rifle is.”

“With Vale.”

“Good.”

“Sir, I can go back.”

“Rowe can hold the post for fifteen minutes.”

“I don’t need fifteen.”

Sayeed looked toward the ridge.

“Did something happen?”

“No.”

“Did you sleep?”

“Some.”

“How much?”

“Two hours, maybe.”

“Last night?”

“Yes.”

“And before that?”

“Four.”

Sayeed rubbed dust from his sleeve.

“You’ve had coffee?”

“Three.”

“Those sachets?”

“Yes.”

“They taste like roof tar.”

Mercer gave a brief laugh, then pressed his palms onto his thighs.

“I thought somebody was moving by the wall.”

“Was somebody moving?”

“Plastic sheet. Wind caught it.”

“You fired?”

“No, sir.”

“Good.”

“I nearly did.”

Sayeed watched him.

Mercer said, “My finger was there.”

“On the trigger?”

“Yes.”

“Anybody near the sheet?”

“Jenkins was twenty metres left.”

“Did he notice?”

“I don’t think so.”

A helicopter passed far to the south, too distant to see, its pulse fading in and out beneath the wind.

Sayeed said, “You tell Vale?”

“No.”

“You’re going to.”

Mercer stared at the ground.

“He’ll take me off the line.”

“For tonight.”

“I don’t want that.”

“You can want something else tomorrow.”

Mercer picked up a pebble and rolled it between his fingers.

“My brother told everybody I’d come back with stories.”

“You can tell him about the coffee.”

“He was in two years.”

“Here?”

“North sector.”

“When?”

“Four years ago.”

Sayeed nodded.

“He acts like none of this bothered him.”

“People are selective when they get home.”

Mercer threw the pebble.

It hit the truck tyre.

“Sir, can I ask you something?”

“Yes.”

“You ever get this?”

Sayeed looked toward the communications mast.

“Once in training I spent half an hour convinced I’d forgotten how to read a map.”

Mercer waited.

“That sounds different.”

“It was a bad half hour.”

“Were you shaking?”

“I threw up on a major’s boots.”

Mercer looked at him for the first time.

“Really?”

“He was wearing polished boots in mud. I improved them.”

Mercer rubbed both hands over his face.

Sayeed continued, “Another time I stayed awake nearly three days and heard a telephone ringing. There was no telephone.”

“Did you tell anyone?”

“Eventually.”

“What happened?”

“They made me sleep.”

Mercer took another drink.

“I can’t sleep here.”

“Most people can when they’re tired enough.”

“I close my eyes and I hear the fence.”

“The loose panel?”

“Yes. It bangs.”

“We can fix that.”

“I asked Jenkins. He said the bracket’s bent.”

“Then we replace the bracket.”

Mercer gave him a tired look.

“You make it sound easy.”

“It requires a wrench.”

For a while they listened to the generator.

Then Sayeed stood.

“Come with me.”

“Where?”

“Vale.”

Mercer did not move.

Sayeed waited.

“Sir.”

“Yes?”

“Can you tell him?”

“I’ll be standing there.”

Mercer picked up his helmet.

“That’s not really what I meant.”

Sayeed adjusted the strap on his own vest.

“I’ll begin.”

Mercer rose slowly.

His hands were steadier now.

They walked around the truck.

Halfway to the command tent Mercer said, “Sir, about Jenkins.”

“What about him?”

“He was nearer than I told you.”

Sayeed stopped.

“How near?”

“Maybe eight metres.”

Sayeed looked at him for several seconds.

“Any other people?”

“No.”

“Anyone behind the sheet?”

“No.”

“You’re certain?”

“Yes.”

Sayeed resumed walking.

“Tell Vale the whole sequence.”

Mercer followed.

“I will.”

“And after that, food.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“Eat anyway.”

“What is it?”

“Something with beans.”

“It’s always something with beans.”

“That part of military life has remained stable for centuries.”

Mercer managed another small laugh.

They reached the tent.

Sayeed lifted the flap.

“Sergeant Vale.”

Vale looked up from a map.

“Sir?”

Sayeed stepped inside, leaving room for Mercer beside him.

“Corporal Mercer needs to give us an account of an incident at the eastern post.”

Mercer came in and removed his helmet.

His mouth opened, closed, then opened again.

“About twenty minutes ago,” he began, “I saw movement by the wall.”

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The elevator stopped between the eighth and ninth floors with a soft jolt.

Naomi looked up from her phone.

The man beside her pressed the button for nine again.

Nothing happened.

He pressed eight.

Nothing.

“Probably temporary,” he said.

Naomi slipped her phone into her pocket.

“Does the alarm work?”

“We may find out.”

He pressed the call button.

A bell sounded somewhere above them.

A voice crackled through the speaker.

“Security.”

The man leaned toward it.

“Elevator B has stopped.”

“Which floor?”

“Between eight and nine.”

“Anyone injured?”

“No.”

Naomi said, “No.”

The security guard’s voice returned.

“Stay inside. Maintenance is on site.”

The speaker clicked off.

The man looked at the ceiling.

“Well.”

Naomi leaned against the wall.

“You work on nine?”

“Seven. I pressed nine by accident.”

“I’m on nine.”

“I’ve seen you.”

“I started Monday.”

“That explains it.”

He put his laptop bag on the floor.

“I’m Victor.”

“Naomi.”

They shook hands awkwardly in the small space.

Victor looked at the display. It showed a frozen red 8.

“You picked a good week. The air conditioning broke yesterday.”

“I noticed.”

“People were holding meetings in the stairwell.”

“I thought that was normal.”

“No. Usually they hide in conference rooms.”

Naomi checked her phone again.

“No signal.”

“Mine never works in this lift.”

“How often do you get stuck?”

“This is my first.”

“That’s reassuring.”

“Statistically useless, though.”

She smiled.

The elevator hummed briefly.

They both looked up.

Nothing followed.

Naomi said, “I have a meeting at four.”

“What time is it?”

“Three fifty-two.”

“You may have a strong excuse.”

“It’s my second meeting with him.”

“With who?”

“Harold. Finance.”

Victor winced.

“You know Harold?”

“I’ve worked here eleven years.”

“What’s he like?”

Victor considered the question.

“He brings printed spreadsheets.”

“That bad?”

“He has a ruler.”

“For spreadsheets?”

“For following rows.”

Naomi laughed.

“I’m presenting budget assumptions.”

“He’ll enjoy himself.”

“Great.”

Victor sat on the floor.

After a second he said, “You can sit. The carpet is probably cleaner than the buttons.”

Naomi remained standing.

“I’m wearing something that creases if I look at it wrong.”

“New job clothes?”

“Yes.”

“I did that. Bought three shirts I hated.”

“Did you stop wearing them?”

“Took six years.”

Naomi looked at his shirt.

“Is that one of them?”

“No. Those are gone.”

“Good.”

A small fan above the lights continued blowing warm air.

Naomi loosened the scarf around her neck.

“This is embarrassing.”

“Being trapped?”

“Arriving late all week. Monday the train stopped. Yesterday I went into the wrong building. Today this.”

“Which wrong building?”

“The one across the plaza.”

“The insurance company?”

“I sat in their reception for ten minutes.”

“Nobody stopped you?”

“They gave me visitor Wi-Fi.”

Victor laughed hard enough that he had to take off his glasses.

“They’re very welcoming.”

“I had a coffee.”

“That’s better than our reception.”

“I considered staying.”

His laughter faded.

Naomi sat carefully on the edge of her handbag.

“Eleven years is a long time.”

“It went quickly for about eight of them.”

“And the other three?”

“More detailed.”

She nodded.

“Do you like it?”

“Some days.”

“I can’t tell yet.”

“You won’t for a while.”

“I left a place I’d been at since university.”

“That’s difficult.”

“It was getting small.”

Victor looked at her, then at the doors.

“I keep thinking about leaving.”

“Do you have somewhere else?”

“No.”

“Then I suppose it stays theoretical.”

“Mostly. My wife sends me job adverts.”

“Does she want you to leave?”

“She wants me to stop talking about leaving.”

Naomi smiled.

“That seems fair.”

“Very.”

The elevator suddenly dropped half an inch.

Naomi grabbed the handrail.

Victor stood immediately.

“Still all right?”

“Yes.”

“Security?”

He pressed the call button.

The guard returned.

“We’re working on it.”

Victor said, “The car moved.”

“We know. Please remain calm.”

The line went dead.

Naomi stared at the doors.

“I hate that phrase.”

Victor stood beside her.

“My daughter got stuck on a Ferris wheel once.”

“How long?”

“Forty minutes.”

“How old was she?”

“Seven.”

“Was she frightened?”

“She waved at us and ate all the sweets in her pocket. My wife was the one who nearly collapsed.”

Naomi breathed out slowly.

“Do you have children?”

“No.”

“You want them?”

She looked at him.

“That’s quite personal for an elevator.”

“Sorry.”

“It’s all right. Maybe. My sister has three. I visit and come home needing silence.”

“I have two. Silence becomes very valuable.”

Another hum came from above.

The car began moving.

Naomi stood quickly.

“Oh, thank God.”

The elevator rose and stopped at nine.

The doors opened.

Three maintenance workers were waiting outside.

Naomi stepped into the corridor and turned.

Victor remained inside.

“Aren’t you getting out?”

“I work on seven.”

“You could take the stairs.”

He picked up his laptop bag.

“That is an excellent idea.”

He stepped out beside her.

Naomi looked at the time.

“Four-oh-three.”

“Harold may already be measuring something.”

She began walking backward toward the glass doors.

“Which way is Conference C?”

Victor pointed left.

“Past reception, second right.”

“Thanks.”

“Good luck.”

Naomi took several steps, then stopped.

“Victor?”

“Yes?”

“If I see the insurance building again tomorrow, I may go in.”

“They have better coffee.”

“I remember.”

She hurried away.

Victor stood by the elevator for a moment, then turned toward the stairwell.
