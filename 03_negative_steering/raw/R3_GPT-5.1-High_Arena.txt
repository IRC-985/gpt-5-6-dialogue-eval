
**1. A mother and her adult son**

The kitchen smelled of garlic and burned toast. Ellen scraped at the pan with a wooden spoon.

“Don’t dig at it like that,” Mark said. “You’ll take the coating off.”

“I’ve taken the coating off everything in this house,” she said. “Your father used to complain about the pots. Take the bread out, it’s turning into roof tiles.”

Mark pulled the tray from the oven. “You keep the oven too hot.”

“I can’t see the little numbers anymore,” she said. “You have younger eyes. Sit down. I made that soup you like.”

He sat, resting his forearms on the table. “The one with the beans?”

“That’s the one.” She ladled soup into a chipped bowl. “You look thin. Do you eat where you live now? Or is it only coffee?”

“I eat,” he said. “Work’s been…busy.”

“Busy pays the rent.” She pushed the bowl toward him. “Taste it before it congeals. How long can you stay?”

“A couple of hours. I have to be back in the city tonight.”

“So soon.” She wiped her hands on a dish towel and sat opposite him. “You used to spend entire weekends here. Your friends would be in and out like pigeons.”

“I have stuff to sort out,” he said, blowing on the soup. “Paperwork. Meetings.”

“You’re always sorting.” She watched him lift the spoon. “Too much salt?”

“It’s fine.”

“You’re being polite.” She tasted her own. “All right, maybe a little too much. My hands shake when I pour. I should buy that sea salt, the fancy one. They say it’s healthier.”

He shrugged. “Salt is salt.”

“You sound like your father.” She smiled briefly. “His doctor banned the shaker from the table, he would sneak it in his pocket. Like such a secret.”

They fell quiet. Mark looked around at the familiar cabinets, the slightly crooked clock.

“You still haven’t fixed the roof,” he said. “There’s a stain over the window.”

“The man said it can wait till spring,” she replied. “I climb up there and he yells at me, that neighbor. The one with the dog. He thinks I’m going to fall.”

“He’s right to worry,” Mark said. “You’re—”

“Don’t say I’m old. I know I’m old.” She folded the towel. “Why are you here in the middle of the week? You never come on Wednesdays.”

“I wanted to see you.”

“You see me on Sundays.” She narrowed her eyes. “Something happened.”

“Nothing happened.”

“Then something is going to happen.” She leaned forward. “You get that line in your forehead when you’re holding something back.”

He rubbed the spot unconsciously. “It’s just work. There’s a change coming.”

“Are they letting people go?” she asked. “You can stay here if they do. There’s the sofa, and the little room where we put the Christmas things.”

“It’s a good change,” he said. “They offered me a promotion. Overseas.”

She blinked. “Overseas where?”

“Singapore. At first, at least. Maybe other places after.”

“That’s very far.” Her fingers tightened around the towel. “Do you even know anyone there?”

“Some colleagues. They’ll help me settle. It’s…a big step.”

“How long?” Her voice had gone flat.

“Two years. Maybe three.”

She nodded slowly. “Your father wanted to live abroad. He talked about it all the time. Paris, Buenos Aires. He never even got a passport.”

“Mom, you don’t have to—”

“You should take it,” she interrupted. “A promotion is important. Your father missed too many chances because of us. He said the factory needed him, and we needed the factory, and then there you are, almost forty, and I’m talking like this anyway.”

“I haven’t decided.”

“You already have that look.” She pointed at him with the spoon. “The one from when you left for college. You walked around this kitchen like it was a hotel lobby. Touching everything but already somewhere else.”

He stared at his soup. “I worry about you here alone.”

“The widows’ club keeps me busy. We play cards and complain about our joints.” She tried a smile. “I can learn video calls. You show me which button, and I’ll shout at the screen.”

“It’s twelve hours’ difference,” he said. “Our nights and days will be flipped.”

“So we talk at strange times.” She shrugged. “I already do that with your cousin in Toronto. I call her while she’s brushing her teeth. She hates it.”

He laughed once, without much sound.

She watched his face. “When do you have to give them an answer?”

“Friday.”

“Today is Wednesday.” She picked up his spoon and stirred his soup absentmindedly. “Stay for dinner. I’ll make something better. We’ll talk in the morning. You can sleep in your old room. The posters are still there.”

“I can’t. My boss expects me tomorrow.”

“That boss takes enough.” She set the spoon down. “Let your mother steal a little.”

He glanced at the clock. “I can stay another hour.”

“An hour.” She pushed the bread basket toward him. “Eat more. Big decisions need bread.”

**2. Two old diplomats**

The club’s leather chairs sighed when they sat down. A fire crackled, though it was hardly cold.

“Whisky?” asked Raghavan, lifting the menu with stiff fingers.

Petrov nodded. “The Islay one. My doctor would faint if he saw me.”

“Mine retired,” Raghavan said. “Or died. One of those things that happens to people who lecture for a living.”

Petrov laughed softly. “You look the same, Ravi. A little more…folded.”

“Paper folds,” Raghavan replied. “People sag. How long has it been?”

“Since that energy summit in Vienna,” Petrov said. “Seventeen years, maybe eighteen. You gave that speech about patience. Remember?”

“I remember the translator butchering my proverb,” Raghavan said. “The hall laughed at all the wrong places.”

The waiter brought their drinks and left them alone with the ticking clock.

“So,” Petrov said, lifting his glass. “To patience. It didn’t save Syria. Or Ukraine. Or anything else.”

Raghavan took a sip. “Nothing saves countries. They survive on stubbornness and luck.”

“You used to sound more optimistic,” Petrov said. “Back in Geneva, you would lecture me about ‘shared interests.’”

“Then you would leak my talking points to the press,” Raghavan said dryly.

Petrov shrugged. “It helped both of us. The leaks made you look strong at home. They made me look well-informed. Our ministers liked that.”

“You were shameless.”

“Still am.” Petrov studied the amber liquid. “How is retirement treating you?”

“My wife is excited that I cook,” Raghavan answered. “She says I chop vegetables like I’m drafting communiqués. Every piece exactly the same size.”

“She must be relieved you’re not flying everywhere,” Petrov said. “My daughter says my suitcases look lonely. She puts plants on top of them.”

“You have a granddaughter now, yes?” Raghavan asked.

“Two,” Petrov said, and his face softened. “They know me as the man who draws cats. They have no idea I spent thirty years lying professionally.”

“You call it lying,” Raghavan said. “I called it omission. It sounded cleaner.”

“We bent words till they squeaked,” Petrov said. “Do you ever read our old agreements? They look so solid on paper. Then you remember how much duct tape was behind each paragraph.”

Raghavan smiled. “I reread the river-basin treaty last month. My students think it was a masterpiece. They assign it in seminars.”

“It held for twenty-five years,” Petrov said. “That counts for something.”

“It held because the water levels stayed high.” Raghavan swirled his drink. “When the droughts came, everyone discovered creative interpretations.”

Petrov leaned back, staring at the ceiling moulding. “Do you miss the rooms?”

“Which rooms?” Raghavan asked.

“The ones with stale coffee and locked windows,” Petrov said. “The conference centers, the presidential palaces with their airless corridors. The endless waiting for some leader’s motorcade.”

“I miss the waiting, strangely,” Raghavan said. “Not the speeches. The hours outside, in side corridors, where real things moved an inch at a time.”

“The chairs with ugly fabric,” Petrov added. “I can describe every pattern from every foreign ministry guest room.”

“The carpets,” Raghavan said. “Terrible carpets. You could tell a regime’s stability by its carpet budget.”

Petrov chuckled. “Do your students believe your stories?”

“Some do,” Raghavan replied. “The rest are busy checking their phones. They care about climate, gender, cyberwar. They think we lived in the Stone Age.”

“In some ways, we did.” Petrov’s hand shook slightly as he lifted his glass. “I was watching today’s panel, these young envoys talking about ‘narratives’ and ‘platforms.’ I barely understood half of it.”

“We spoke about ‘blocks’ and ‘non-alignment,’” Raghavan said. “Different toys, same sandbox.”

“Would you go back if they asked?” Petrov’s eyes searched his old colleague’s face.

“They don’t ask old men,” Raghavan said. “We get invited to conferences like this, to sit on high chairs and tell anecdotes.”

“It’s comfortable, though,” Petrov said, glancing around at the paneled walls. “The world falls apart outside, and in here the silver is polished.”

“Comfort frightens me more than chaos,” Raghavan replied. “Chaos keeps you awake.”

Petrov raised an eyebrow. “You’re one of those who need enemies to feel alive.”

“I need problems,” Raghavan said. “My wife gives me grocery lists. I negotiate with the fishmonger about the price of salmon. It’s a form of therapy.”

“I argue with my neighbor over parking spaces,” Petrov said. “He thinks my spot extends too far. We use chalk now. It feels like drawing borders.”

“You always did enjoy red lines,” Raghavan said.

Petrov smiled. “My granddaughter asked me yesterday whether I was a good guy or a bad guy. I told her good guys don’t usually get embassies.”

“What did she say?”

“She asked if bad guys get cats.”

Raghavan laughed, genuinely. “And do they?”

“Sometimes,” Petrov said. “They sit on your lap and see through you.”

The fire crackled again. A waiter walked past with another tray.

“Will there be anything left of what we built?” Petrov asked quietly.

Raghavan looked into his glass, then at his friend. “For a while,” he said. “Long enough for someone else to blame us when it breaks.”

Petrov nodded. “Blame is also a kind of memory.”

They drank in silence for a moment, listening to the clock.

**3. Two schoolgirls**

The bus stop shelter was scuffed with old stickers. Rain streaked down the glass. Leah bounced her leg, backpack at her feet.

“You’re doing it again,” Maya said. “The leg thing.”

“I’m cold,” Leah muttered. “Why is the bus always late when it rains?”

“Because the driver hates us personally,” Maya said. She tugged her hood up. “Did you study for the math quiz?”

“Sort of,” Leah said. “I watched a video and fell asleep. Then I dreamed about triangles attacking me.”

Maya snorted. “That sounds like Sir.” She hunched her shoulders. “He yelled at me for chewing gum. It wasn’t even gum, it was this vitamin thing.”

“He yelled at Nadine for breathing too loud,” Leah said. “He hates our class.”

“He loves Max,” Maya said. “Max could set the desk on fire and Sir would call it ‘initiative.’”

Leah picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. “Are you going to the thing on Friday?”

“What thing?”

“The party at Lila’s. Her parents are away.”

Maya made a face. “There will be boys there. Boys spray deodorant like it’s holy water.”

“That’s why we go,” Leah said. “For the deodorant.”

“I’ll smell like fake ocean for a week.” Maya looked at her. “You want to go?”

“Kind of,” Leah said. “Everyone in our group is going.”

“Everyone?” Maya asked.

“Yeah. Even Hana. She begged for permission during dinner, like she was applying for a visa.”

“My mother would ask a hundred questions,” Maya said. “Who, what, where, will there be adults, will there be oxygen.” She sighed. “I haven’t asked yet.”

Leah hesitated. “We can say we’re sleeping at each other’s house.”

“Classic,” Maya said. “We invent two mothers, and then they phone each other.”

“My mom never phones,” Leah said. “She texts, and then forgets. She’s always at the restaurant.”

“Mine phones,” Maya said. “She likes hearing other mothers’ voices. It’s like a club.”

A boy from their class cycled past and raised his hand. Leah stared straight ahead.

“He waved at you,” Maya said.

“I can see,” Leah answered. “He nearly crashed into the pole.”

“He likes you,” Maya said.

“He likes his bike,” Leah said. “He talks about gears.”

“You could learn about gears,” Maya said. “Then you’d have something to talk about that isn’t homework.”

Leah kicked a pebble. “Do you ever feel like everyone else is already…started? Like they all got an email with instructions and we missed it?”

“All the time,” Maya said. “Even my little brother. He just does stuff. He signed up for drama club without even asking. I’m still deciding what pencils to buy.”

“My cousin kissed someone when she was our age,” Leah said quietly. “She told me last week. She said it was gross and also amazing.”

Maya wrinkled her nose. “I don’t even like sharing straws.”

Leah laughed. “You don’t like sharing anything.”

“I share memes,” Maya said. “That counts.”

They watched a car splash through a puddle. The water hit the edge of Leah’s shoe.

“Ugh,” she said. “Wet sock.”

“That’s the worst,” Maya said. “You’ll get trench foot and die before math.”

“Maybe that’s my only escape,” Leah said.

“Drama queen.”

Leah glanced at her. “Are you mad about something?”

Maya picked at a crack in the plastic bench. “You sat with Hana at lunch.”

“There was space at her table,” Leah said. “You were in the library.”

“I was looking for you first,” Maya said. “You weren’t there yet.”

“I came late,” Leah said. “Mrs. King held us back. I told you in the group chat.”

“I didn’t have my phone,” Maya said. “My mom took it because I left the dishes.”

Leah shifted. “I didn’t…choose Hana over you.”

Maya shrugged one shoulder. “It just looked like fun over there. With the ring light and everything.”

“They were filming that stupid dance,” Leah said. “I kept messing it up.”

“You looked happy in the video,” Maya said.

“You watched it?”

“Everyone did,” Maya said. “They posted it everywhere.”

Leah groaned. “My arms look like noodles.”

“Your arms look like arms,” Maya said. “Stop being weird.”

They sat with that for a moment. A gust of wind pushed rain inside the shelter.

“So,” Maya said. “If I get permission for Friday, will you wait for me at the corner? I don’t want to walk in alone.”

“I thought you didn’t want to go,” Leah said.

“I want to go with you,” Maya replied. “Without you, I’ll end up hiding in the bathroom reading the cleaning products.”

Leah smiled. “I’ll wait at the corner. We can smell like fake ocean together.”

“Good,” Maya said. “And if anyone tries to kiss me, you fake a call and rescue me.”

“If anyone tries to kiss me, you pretend there’s a fire drill,” Leah said.

“Deal,” Maya said.

The bus appeared at the end of the street, lights blurred in the rain.

“There,” Leah said. “Finally.”

Maya stood and swung her backpack on. “Maybe the quiz will be canceled. Maybe Sir will fall in a puddle.”

“We’re terrible people,” Leah said.

“We’re average people,” Maya replied. “That’s worse.”

They climbed onto the bus, still talking over each other.

**4. A married couple after an affair**

The dishwasher hummed in the background. Emma sat at the end of the table with a mug between her hands. Daniel stood by the sink, drying a plate that was already dry.

“You can sit down,” she said. “The plates won’t run away.”

He laid the dish on the counter and pulled out the chair opposite her. It scraped on the tiles.

“What did your therapist say?” he asked.

“She’s not my therapist.” Emma stared into her mug. “It was just a counselor. Through work.”

“What did she say?” he repeated.

“She asked questions,” Emma replied. “Mostly about time. When, how long, things like that. She wanted a timeline.”

“Did you give her one?”

“I tried.” Emma rubbed her forehead. “It all blends together. Meetings, messages, stupid hotel rooms that all looked the same.”

Daniel winced. “You don’t have to describe them.”

“I wasn’t trying to,” she said quickly. “I’m just…answering.”

He looked at the fridge, where their daughter’s drawings fluttered under magnets. “Did you talk about us?”

“She kept steering it back to me,” Emma said. “My choices, my…patterns. I kept mentioning you and the kids, and she’d say, ‘We’ll come back to them.’”

“Did you?” he asked.

“Not really.” Emma sighed. “We ran out of time.”

Daniel tapped his fingers on the table. “So what now?”

“I go again next week,” she said. “She suggested couples sessions. If you want.”

He let out a breath. “I don’t know if I can sit in a room with a stranger and rehearse all this.”

“You’re sitting in a room with me,” Emma said softly.

“You’re not a stranger,” he said, then hesitated. “At least, I keep telling myself that.”

She flinched. “I’m still—”

“I know who you are,” he said quickly. “I know your laugh and how you fall asleep during movies. I also know you lied to me for almost a year.”

Emma’s eyes filled. “I know.”

The dishwasher clicked to a new cycle, the sound rising, then settling.

“Did you love him?” Daniel asked suddenly.

Emma stared at him. “I cared about him. It felt…intense. Exciting. Like being twenty again, for a while.”

“That sounds like love,” Daniel said.

“It wasn’t the same as you,” she said. “With you it’s…daily. Groceries and bills and taking turns with the flu. With him it was all edges and stolen hours.”

Daniel pressed his palms together. “Do you miss him?”

“I miss what I felt,” she admitted. “The version of myself who didn’t worry about soccer practice schedules. It’s ugly to say that out loud.”

“I miss versions of myself too,” he said quietly. “The one who thought we were solid. The one who didn’t check your phone bill this morning like some detective in a bad show.”

“Did you find anything?” she asked.

He shook his head. “Just spam and my mother.”

“I stopped talking to him when you found out,” Emma said. “I told you that.”

“I believe you,” he said. “Mostly. Then at three a.m. I don’t.”

Emma reached for his hand, then pulled back halfway. “I hate what I did to you.”

“And to the kids,” he added.

“They don’t know,” she said quickly. “They shouldn’t have to carry this. Not yet, maybe not ever.”

“They know something,” he said. “Liam watched us at breakfast like we were a show. He keeps asking if everything is okay.”

“What do you say?” she asked.

“I say we’re having a hard week,” Daniel replied. “He asked how long weeks can last.”

Emma let out a shaky laugh. “He’s too smart.”

“He got that from you,” Daniel said.

Silence stretched between them.

“Do you want to leave?” he asked eventually. “Start over somewhere else. Without me.”

“I don’t,” she said quickly. “I thought about it, in the middle of it all. The fantasy of it. New apartment, new city. Then I’d picture you packing the kids’ backpacks alone, and I felt sick.”

“I don’t know if I can forgive you,” he said.

“I’m not asking for instant forgiveness,” she replied. “I’m asking for…time. To show up. To do the stupid, boring, everyday things with you again.”

“You’re good at everyday,” he said. “You make lists. You remember dentist appointments. You book flights six months in advance. I keep thinking, how did that person also find time for hotel rooms?”

Emma swallowed. “I carved it out of sleep. Out of lies about late trains and extra meetings. It was like living two calendars. I was always tired. I thought it was worth it until it wasn’t.”

Daniel leaned back. “Did you ever almost tell me, before I found out?”

“Yes,” she said. “A lot. In the car, especially. I’d grip the steering wheel and think, ‘Say it now, before you get home.’ And then the house would appear and there’d be laundry and spelling tests, and the moment would dissolve.”

He looked at her face, at the dark circles under her eyes. “I’m so angry,” he said. “And I still made you tea this morning on autopilot.”

“I noticed,” she whispered. “I didn’t know whether to say thank you or apologize again.”

“You said neither,” he said.

“I was afraid you’d hear both as excuses,” she said.

He rubbed his temples. “I don’t know what I want from you right now.”

“I can sleep in the guest room,” she offered.

“I already put your pillow there yesterday,” he said. “While you were at work.”

“Oh,” she said, small.

He sighed. “I didn’t throw anything out. Your sweater is still on the chair. Your mug is still here.”

She nodded. “I like this mug.”

“I know.” He glanced at the clock. “The kids will be home in twenty minutes.”

Emma wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. “I’ll start their homework folders.”

“Okay,” he said. “Later…we can talk about the counselor again. And money. And schedules.”

“And whether you want me here,” she added.

“That too,” he said.

They stood up almost at the same time, moving around each other carefully, as if the kitchen had become smaller.

**5. An officer and a subordinate**

The office smelled faintly of coffee and printer toner. Maps covered the walls, dotted with colored pins. Captain Ruiz sat behind his desk, reading from a thin folder, while Sergeant Miller stood at attention.

“Sit, Miller,” Ruiz said without looking up.

Miller obeyed, perched on the edge of the chair.

Ruiz closed the folder. “I read the report about yesterday’s patrol.”

“Yes, sir.”

“There’s a line here.” Ruiz tapped the page. “You advised against proceeding down the eastern alley. You stated ‘bad feeling.’”

Miller swallowed. “Yes, sir.”

“Explain the feeling,” Ruiz said.

Miller shifted. “It was quiet, sir. Too quiet. No kids, no vendors, no…life. There was laundry hanging, but no one near it. The doors were closed.”

Ruiz watched him. “That sector has been unstable all month. You’ve walked those streets a dozen times. What made yesterday different?”

“The cats,” Miller said, then looked embarrassed. “I know how that sounds.”

“Go on,” Ruiz said.

“They usually sit on the walls, sir. Yesterday there weren’t any. Not even one. Like someone had scooped them up. It reminded me of a village back home before a storm.”

Ruiz leaned back. “So you suggested an alternate route.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Lieutenant Harris overruled you.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You repeated your objection?”

“I did, sir. I told him I had a bad feeling. He said we had intel that the alley was clear.”

Ruiz steepled his fingers. “And then the IED went off two buildings down from where you wanted them to turn around.”

Miller stared at his knees. “Yes, sir.”

“You were pinned under debris for twenty minutes.”

“Yes, sir.”

Ruiz let the silence stretch. “Harris is in surgery.”

Miller nodded once.

“How are you?” Ruiz asked.

“My leg’s fine, sir. Bruising only. The medic cleared me for desk duty.”

“I’m not asking about your leg,” Ruiz said.

Miller blinked. “I’m…angry, sir. And also guilty. I argued with him in front of the squad. I kept thinking, what if that slowed us down just enough to hit the trigger zone.”

Ruiz shook his head slightly. “The blast radius doesn’t care about arguments.”

“Yes, sir,” Miller said mechanically.

“I asked for you specifically today,” Ruiz said. “You’ve been in this unit for three years. Your file says you have good instincts.”

“Sometimes,” Miller said. “Sometimes I’m just jumpy.”

“Have your instincts been wrong in the field?” Ruiz asked.

“Once in training,” Miller replied. “I thought the instructors had rigged the fake village a certain way. I was wrong and everyone laughed. Out here…it’s harder to tell.”

“You’re not being brought in for punishment,” Ruiz said. “I want your version before I talk to Harris, when he’s able.”

Miller exhaled. “He’ll say I was insubordinate, sir.”

“Were you?”

“I raised my voice,” Miller admitted. “I said we shouldn’t go down that alley. He told me to remember the chain of command. I shut up after that.”

“Good,” Ruiz said. “Because that chain keeps us from turning every patrol into a committee meeting.”

Miller’s jaw tightened. “With respect, sir, the chain also walked us into a bomb.”

Ruiz regarded him. “You’re not the first sergeant to feel that. You won’t be the last.”

“I keep hearing the sound,” Miller said quietly. “The boom, and then everything high-pitched. I keep thinking, what if I had said something different. Used other words.”

“Words wouldn’t have changed the wiring under the road,” Ruiz said. “Sometimes the ground is already written.”

Miller stared at the map behind Ruiz. “Are we going back there?”

“Eventually, yes,” Ruiz said. “Not this week. The engineers need to sweep it. The villagers need to walk their own streets again.”

“I don’t want to take my squad down that alley,” Miller said.

“You won’t,” Ruiz replied. “You’ll be on coordination. Radios, logistics. For a while.”

“Is that a demotion, sir?” Miller asked.

“It’s an assignment,” Ruiz said. “I need someone who knows the terrain and isn’t afraid to speak up. Just not in the middle of the street.”

Miller frowned. “Where then, sir?”

“In rooms like this,” Ruiz said. “Before and after patrols. When we plan routes, you can point to alleys and say, ‘The cats were missing there last time.’”

Miller let out a short, tired laugh. “They’ll think I’m superstitious.”

“They already think you’re alive,” Ruiz said. “That carries weight.”

Miller’s fingers gripped his knees. “I keep seeing Harris. The way he looked at me just before the blast, like he was about to say something.”

“You two worked well together before this,” Ruiz said. “He relied on your read of the streets.”

“He didn’t yesterday,” Miller said.

“He had intel,” Ruiz replied. “Intel makes people stubborn. They trust the paper more than the dust under their boots.”

“Which one do you trust, sir?” Miller asked.

“Depends on the day,” Ruiz said. “Yesterday, the paper failed. Tomorrow, your feeling might fail. That’s the job.”

Miller nodded slowly. “What do you want me to do now, sir?”

“Go see the doc again,” Ruiz said. “Tell her about the sound. The cats, too. Don’t leave that part out. Then report to Ops at fourteen hundred. They’ll have a chair and a headset for you.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Miller,” Ruiz added as the sergeant stood. “When your gut talks next time, bring it to me before you’re on the street.”

“I will, sir,” Miller said.

“Good,” Ruiz replied. “Now get out of my office before I start asking about your paperwork backlog.”

A hint of a smile flickered at the corners of Miller’s mouth as he saluted and left.

**6. Two people who barely know each other**

The elevator shuddered and stopped between floors. The light flickered once and steadied. Hannah glanced up from her tote bag; Tom pulled out one earbud.

“That didn’t sound good,” Hannah said.

Tom pressed the button for the lobby. Nothing happened. He tried the door-open button. The doors stayed shut.

“Great,” he muttered.

Hannah shifted her grocery bag to the floor. “Is there an alarm thing?”

Tom pushed the red button. A harsh bell rang for a few seconds, then cut off.

“Well,” Hannah said. “At least it makes noise.”

They waited. The numbers above the door stayed frozen between 7 and 8.

Tom cleared his throat. “I’ve seen you in the building, right? With the blue bike.”

“Oh,” she said. “Yeah. That’s me. Fourth floor.”

“I’m on six,” he said. “With the plants in the window. People keep asking if I run a jungle.”

“I like those plants,” Hannah said. “They make the stairwell less depressing.”

“Thanks,” Tom said. “I overwater them when I’m stressed. So they are very well hydrated.”

She smiled faintly. “I kill succulents. Everyone says succulents are unkillable. They haven’t met me.”

“Maybe they need your tough love,” he said.

Silence. The elevator hummed quietly, not moving.

“Should we…call someone?” Hannah asked, pulling out her phone.

Tom checked his. “No signal. Classic.”

“I have one bar,” she said. “Maybe I can text.”

“Worth a try,” Tom said. “The building manager never answers, though. I once locked myself out. I sat in the hallway for an hour.”

“Where does he live?” she asked, tapping.

“In that mysterious office on the ground floor with the blinds always half-closed,” Tom said. “I’m convinced he sleeps on a pile of spare keys.”

She laughed. “Okay, I texted my roommate. If she’s home, she can go bang on his door.”

“Good,” Tom said. “Unless she’s asleep.”

“She works nights,” Hannah said. “She’s probably watching some true crime thing in the dark.”

The air in the elevator felt thicker. Hannah loosened her scarf.

“Are you…okay?” Tom asked. “You look a little pale.”

“I just don’t like enclosed spaces,” she said. “Elevators, changing rooms, photo booths. They all feel like being swallowed.”

“I can tell you about something boring to distract you,” he offered. “Like my job. It’s extremely dull.”

“What do you do?” she asked quickly.

“I write instruction manuals,” he said. “For kitchen appliances. Mixers, blenders, that sort of thing.”

“Seriously?” Her eyebrows went up. “So those little booklets that say, ‘Do not put your hand inside while operating’—that’s you?”

“That’s my art,” he said. “Like, if a toaster exists, someone has probably poured soup into it. And then I get told to add a line so no one does it again.”

“That’s kind of amazing,” she said. “I thought those sentences just appeared.”

“I wish,” he said. “What about you?”

“I work at the bookstore on Oak,” she said. “The one with the crooked shelves.”

“I know that place,” he said. “I bought a cookbook there once. Never opened it.”

“You kept me in a job,” she said. “Cookbooks are our lifeline. People like to imagine cooking more than they like to actually cook.”

He glanced at her grocery bag. “You have a lot of onions.”

“They were on sale,” she said. “I panic-buy vegetables.”

“Onions are good company,” he said. “They last. Better than avocados.”

“Avocados are divas,” she said. “They give you a five-minute window and then turn on you.”

The elevator emitted a low mechanical groan but didn’t move.

“How long do you think we’ve been in here?” Hannah asked.

“Maybe ten minutes,” Tom said. “Feels longer.”

“Time stretches in elevators,” she said. “Like dentist waiting rooms.”

“I once got stuck in one for two hours,” he offered. “In a mall in Cleveland. There were seven of us. One guy kept pressing the button like it was a game.”

“What did you do for two hours?” she asked, clutching her mug of milk more tightly.

“We played ‘twenty questions’ with store names we could remember,” Tom said. “By the end we all knew way too much about each other’s underwear purchases.”

She made a face. “Let’s not do that version.”

“Agreed,” he said.

They fell quiet again. Somewhere in the shaft, a distant clank echoed.

“I was actually kind of rushing,” Hannah said. “I’m supposed to be making soup for my friend. She’s sick.”

“Onion soup?” he guessed.

“Potato and leek,” she said. “But they ran out of leeks, so it’ll be potato and onion pretending to be leek.”

“Your friend won’t know,” Tom said. “Unless she’s a leek expert.”

“She can barely taste anything,” Hannah said. “She has a cold. It’s more about the steam.”

“I was rushing too,” he said. “There’s this soccer game I was going to watch with my brother. He sent a thousand messages about kickoff time.”

“You’re missing it,” she said.

“He’s probably sending pictures of the TV by now,” Tom said. “With captions in all caps.”

“You seem very calm,” she observed.

“I’m freaking out a little,” he admitted. “But if I start pacing, this elevator will feel smaller.”

“I appreciate your not pacing,” she said.

The intercom crackled suddenly, making them both jump.

“Hello?” a muffled voice said. “Elevator two, can you hear me?”

Tom leaned toward the panel. “Yes. Two people stuck between seven and eight.”

“We’re aware of the situation,” the voice said. “The technician is on his way. Stay calm.”

Hannah looked at Tom and mouthed, “On his way?”

Tom shrugged. “Okay,” he said into the speaker. “Thank you.”

The intercom clicked off.

“At least someone knows we exist,” Hannah said.

“Fame at last,” Tom replied.

She smiled, then sagged against the wall. “I hate that phrase, ‘stay calm.’ It always makes me do the opposite.”

“Think about soup,” he said. “Steam, bowls, crusty bread.”

“And instruction manuals,” she added. “Page after page of tiny print. No drama.”

“Exactly,” he said. “Just diagrams with little arrows.”

Her breathing slowed. “If we’re still here in an hour, I’m eating one of these onions.”

“I’ll write a warning label,” he said. “‘In case of elevator entrapment, do not consume raw onions unless prepared for consequences.’”

She laughed, the sound bouncing off the metal walls. “Deal.”

The elevator jerked once, then began to move slowly upward. Both of them grabbed the rail.

“Oh,” Hannah said. “We’re going.”

Tom let out a long breath. “Look at that. Movement.”

The doors slid open on the eighth floor. The hallway lights seemed almost too bright.

“You go first,” Tom said, stepping aside.

She picked up her bag. “Thanks for…talking about toasters.”

“Thanks for teaching me about panic onions,” he replied.

They stepped out into the hallway, a little hesitant, then went in opposite directions, each glancing back once before turning the corner.
