
1. Mother and Adult Son

The radiator in the kitchen had been making a small metallic click every forty seconds. Daniel had counted eleven of them before his mother came in carrying a paper bag of onions.

“You’re early.”

“My train was early.”

“Trains are never early.”

“This one was.”

She set the onions on the counter and looked at his suitcase by the table.

“You brought the big one.”

“I’m staying four days.”

“You used to take that to Greece.”

“I packed badly for Greece.”

“You packed one pair of trousers for two weeks.”

“I was twenty-two.”

“You had money for three shirts and spent it on a boat.”

“It was a ferry.”

“It had cabins.”

Daniel smiled and took the onions from the bag. “Where do these go?”

“Leave them there.”

“They’ll roll.”

“They’ve been rolling around kitchens for thousands of years.”

He put them in the wooden bowl anyway.

His mother opened the refrigerator and stared inside.

“I bought chicken.”

“I saw.”

“And mushrooms.”

“I saw those too.”

“Your aunt says you don’t eat mushrooms now.”

“I eat mushrooms.”

“She said you stopped.”

“When would Aunt Miriam know what I eat?”

“She was with you in May.”

“At a wedding.”

“You pushed the mushrooms aside.”

“They were cold.”

His mother shut the refrigerator.

“So there’s no problem.”

“No problem.”

“Good.”

She filled the kettle. Daniel watched her turn the tap off with both hands, carefully, until there was no drip.

“You changed the washer?”

“The plumber came.”

“You called someone?”

“He came for upstairs.”

“And fixed yours?”

“I asked him while he was here.”

“Miracles.”

“He charged thirty-five pounds.”

“That sounds less miraculous.”

She switched on the kettle.

Daniel pulled a chair out.

“I spoke to the estate agent.”

His mother kept her back to him. “Mm.”

“She says there’s interest.”

“There’s always interest when nobody has to write a cheque.”

“She thinks you could get close to the asking price.”

“I expect she does.”

“She’s sold three houses on this street.”

“I’ve seen the boards.”

Daniel rubbed a nick in the table with his thumbnail.

“You said you wanted to move.”

“I do.”

“You could be nearer Miriam.”

“I could.”

“And there’s that flat by the park.”

“It has a tiny kitchen.”

“You don’t need a huge kitchen.”

His mother turned.

“I need somewhere to put things.”

“You could get rid of some things.”

“That seems to be everybody’s solution.”

“I’m talking about cupboards full of plates.”

“Your grandmother’s plates.”

“You use two.”

“I use six at Christmas.”

“There are thirty.”

“People broke them.”

“Apparently not enough.”

She gave him a long look, then took two mugs down.

Daniel said, “Sorry.”

“You want tea?”

“Yes.”

“Strong?”

“Normal.”

“You used to say strong.”

“I still drink strong tea. Normal strong.”

She poured the water.

Outside, someone dragged a wheelie bin over paving stones.

His mother said, “The estate agent rang yesterday.”

Daniel looked up.

“She told me.”

“She told you?”

“She said you’d asked.”

“I asked whether she’d reached you.”

“She reached me.”

“And?”

“I told her I hadn’t decided.”

“You told me last month you had.”

“I had then.”

Daniel sat back.

His mother placed his mug in front of him.

“The bedroom window sticks,” she said. “Give it a shove with your shoulder. The little room’s fine.”

“Mum.”

“And the shower takes a minute before it gets hot.”

“You’re changing the subject.”

“I’m telling you before you go upstairs and complain about the shower.”

“I’m not going to complain about the shower.”

“You complained in January.”

“It was freezing.”

“It was winter.”

Daniel wrapped both hands around the mug.

“Is this about Dad?”

She took a biscuit from a tin and broke it in half.

“What about him?”

“The house.”

“He lived here.”

“So did you.”

“I’m aware of that.”

“I’m trying to understand why you’ve changed your mind.”

His mother ate one half of the biscuit, brushed crumbs into her palm, and tipped them into the sink.

“I went to the flat.”

“With Miriam?”

“By myself.”

“When?”

“Thursday.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“You were in Brussels.”

“You can still call Brussels.”

“I went in, walked around, came home.”

“What was wrong with it?”

“There was a woman downstairs smoking in slippers.”

“People smoke.”

“I don’t want to see her every morning.”

“You might never see her again.”

“She lives there.”

“Fine. What else?”

“The park looked far away from the window.”

Daniel waited.

His mother stirred her tea although she had put no sugar in it.

“I could hear the lift.”

“That’s fixable.”

“Probably.”

“And?”

“I didn’t like it.”

He breathed through his nose.

“All right.”

“You’re annoyed.”

“I’m tired.”

“You’ve been here twenty minutes.”

“I’ve been travelling since six.”

“You said the train was good.”

“It was still a train.”

She nodded and picked up the other half of her biscuit.

After a while Daniel said, “I can stay tomorrow afternoon. We could look at another place.”

“I have the dentist.”

“When?”

“Two fifteen.”

“After.”

“I’ll be numb.”

“We can look at houses while you’re numb.”

“I might buy anything.”

“You’ll be cheap to negotiate with.”

She almost laughed. It came out as air through her nose.

Daniel drank his tea.

His mother glanced toward the suitcase.

“Four days?”

“Until Monday.”

“Are you working?”

“A little.”

“From here?”

“Yes.”

“Your father’s desk is still upstairs.”

“I know.”

“There are papers in the drawers.”

“I won’t touch them.”

“You can.”

He looked at her.

She picked up her mug and carried it to the sink though it was still half full.

“There are things in there,” she said. “Bank statements, old warranties, nonsense. If you have time.”

“You want me to sort it?”

“Some of it.”

“All right.”

“Don’t throw out the green folder.”

“What’s in the green folder?”

“I can’t remember.”

Daniel laughed.

His mother opened the refrigerator again.

“Chicken at seven?”

“Seven is good.”

“And you still eat mushrooms.”

“Yes.”

“I bought too many.”

“I’ll eat too many.”

She nodded, satisfied enough for the moment, and began taking things out onto the counter.

2. Two Old Diplomats

Sir Alistair Vale was asleep in the armchair beside the library fire when Ambassador Petrov arrived. Petrov stood over him for several seconds, removed his gloves, and dropped one onto Alistair’s lap.

Alistair opened one eye.

“You’ve become theatrical.”

“My knees hurt. I wasn’t bending down to shake you.”

“You could have coughed.”

“I did in the corridor.”

“That was you?”

“It has been me for thirty years.”

Alistair picked up the glove.

“Still smoking?”

“Only cigars.”

“So yes.”

“Where is everybody?”

“Dinner’s at eight.”

“It’s eight ten.”

“Then dinner has been delayed in your honour.”

Petrov checked the clock.

“You invited me for seven-thirty.”

“I invited you for eight.”

Petrov took a folded card from his breast pocket, put on glasses, and read.

“Seven-thirty.”

Alistair peered at it.

“My secretary’s handwriting.”

“It is printed.”

“She uses a printer.”

Petrov sat opposite him.

“Do you have whisky?”

“On the trolley.”

“I prefer yours because you don’t dilute it.”

“I’ve been accused of several things. That one is new.”

Petrov poured two glasses.

“You look terrible.”

“I had pneumonia.”

“When?”

“March.”

“Nobody told me.”

“I didn’t issue a communiqué.”

Petrov passed him a glass.

“Vera died in February.”

Alistair’s hand stopped halfway to his mouth.

“I heard.”

“From whom?”

“Eleanor.”

“Your Eleanor?”

“My Eleanor has been dead twelve years. My daughter Eleanor.”

“I always forget she has the same name.”

“So do I.”

Petrov drank.

“She died quickly,” he said. “Four days in hospital. At the end she was angry because they would not let her have her handbag.”

“What was in it?”

“Nothing useful. Receipts. Mints. A comb. She thought she needed it.”

“She carried a red one in Geneva.”

“Brown by then.”

“I remember red.”

“That was probably Vienna.”

“You had a dreadful flat in Vienna.”

“Beautiful flat.”

“No heat.”

“Historic heating.”

“There was frost inside the windows.”

Petrov looked toward the fire.

“Vera liked it.”

“I know.”

“She liked anywhere with chandeliers.”

“She nearly caused a diplomatic incident over a chandelier.”

“That chandelier was stolen.”

“The ministry said it had been relocated.”

“It was hanging in the deputy minister’s house.”

Alistair smiled into his whisky.

“Those were entertaining people.”

“Criminals.”

“Some of them.”

“Most.”

The door opened. A young woman leaned in.

“Sir Alistair, another fifteen minutes, I’m afraid.”

“Thank you, Sophie.”

“Can I bring anything?”

Petrov raised his glass.

“We have found the important supplies.”

She smiled and shut the door.

Petrov watched her go.

“Granddaughter?”

“Private secretary.”

“You have a private secretary at your age?”

“She stops me accepting invitations like this.”

“You organized this.”

“That explains why she failed.”

Petrov settled deeper into the chair.

“Do you remember Mbeki?”

“Which one?”

“Samuel.”

“Tall. Terrible ties.”

“Excellent ties.”

“Purple elephants.”

“A gift from his daughter.”

“That does not improve them.”

“He’s dead too.”

Alistair stared at him.

“When?”

“Last week.”

“I hadn’t heard.”

“His son wrote.”

“Hm.”

For a time they listened to the fire.

Petrov took off his glasses and polished them.

“I found a letter from you.”

“Destroy it.”

“September 1989.”

“Destroy it twice.”

“You thought the government would survive until Christmas.”

“I was optimistic.”

“You were usually pessimistic.”

“I was trying variety.”

“There was a paragraph about the border crossings.”

Alistair’s face changed slightly.

“What paragraph?”

“You recommended opening two of them temporarily.”

“That sounds plausible.”

“You believed the army would avoid interference if traffic remained civilian.”

Alistair stared into the fire.

“I haven’t seen those papers in years.”

“I have boxes. Vera kept everything.”

“Did it work?”

“For nine days.”

“I remember nine days.”

“You came to my office on the tenth.”

“Did I?”

“You were soaked. It had rained.”

“I remember the rain.”

“You asked whether Moscow had sent instructions.”

Alistair rotated his glass between his fingers.

“And had they?”

“No.”

“You told me they had.”

“I told you to come back after lunch.”

“That was often the same thing.”

Petrov chuckled.

“I was frightened.”

“So was everyone.”

“I did not feel like everyone. I felt like a man with a telephone that would not ring.”

Alistair looked at him.

Petrov continued, “There were people at the gate. Students mostly. Someone had painted flowers on the pavement. I kept expecting trucks.”

“I expected trucks too.”

“You left at four.”

“I had a meeting.”

“You went home.”

“My meeting was at home.”

“With whom?”

“My wife.”

Petrov laughed for a surprisingly long time and had to put his glass down.

Alistair waited.

“My God,” Petrov said. “All these years I imagined you carrying secret instructions to an emergency committee.”

“I carried haddock.”

“Haddock.”

“Eleanor had asked me to buy fish.”

Petrov wiped his eyes.

“Did you?”

“I forgot.”

“That is excellent.”

“She didn’t think so.”

The young woman returned.

“They’re ready now.”

Alistair pushed himself up slowly.

Petrov stayed seated.

“Give me a minute.”

“Your knees?”

“My knees.”

Alistair stood beside him.

“Who else is at this dinner?”

“Some people from the institute. A former minister. Two historians.”

“Historians ask terrible questions.”

“They already know the answers.”

“That makes them worse.”

Petrov reached for his cane.

“Will they ask about 1989?”

“They always do.”

“What shall we tell them?”

Alistair held out Petrov’s glove.

“Something shorter than the truth.”

Petrov took it, tucked it into his pocket, and rose.

“After dinner, remind me about the haddock.”

“There’s very little more.”

“There must be. Did Eleanor forgive you?”

“Eventually.”

“How long?”

“About nine days.”

Petrov began laughing again as they walked toward the dining room.

3. Two Schoolgirls

Mina and Jess were sitting behind the equipment shed because the far side of the hockey field was the only place the teachers rarely checked during lunch.

Jess had taken one shoe off.

“There’s mud in my sock.”

“Why did you take the shoe off?”

“To get the stone out.”

“You’re going to get more mud.”

“I already have mud.”

Mina passed her a tissue.

“This is one tissue.”

“You asked for one.”

“I need about nine.”

“That’s all I have.”

Jess rubbed at her heel and made the mud worse.

“Look.”

“I’m looking.”

“It’s disgusting.”

“You walked through the flower bed.”

“Olivia pushed me.”

“She bumped you.”

“She has arms. Arms do pushing.”

Mina opened her lunch box.

Jess leaned over.

“Is that cake?”

“Banana bread.”

“Same family.”

“You brought lunch.”

“My sandwich is wet.”

“How?”

“Tomato.”

“You asked for tomato.”

“My dad put four slices in.”

Mina tore the banana bread in half and gave her a piece.

Jess ate it in three bites.

“I think Mrs. Haines knows.”

Mina stopped chewing.

“About us being here?”

“About Friday.”

“Oh.”

“She looked at me in maths.”

“She looks at everyone in maths.”

“No. It was a long look.”

“She gave me a long look because I got seven times eight wrong.”

“You got seven times eight wrong?”

“I wrote fifty-four.”

“That’s seven times—”

“I know.”

They were quiet.

On the other side of the shed a ball bounced, followed by shouting.

Jess put her shoe back on.

“My mum found the email.”

“What email?”

“The one from school.”

Mina shut her lunch box.

“You said you deleted it.”

“I deleted it on my phone. It was on the laptop.”

“What did she do?”

“She printed it.”

“Why?”

“She likes paper when she’s angry.”

“What happened?”

“She left it on the kitchen table.”

“And?”

“And there was a yellow highlighter next to it.”

“That’s horrible.”

“It was worse when she came in.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That I was at yours.”

“You weren’t.”

“I know where I was.”

“I mean, she knows my mum.”

“She hasn’t called her yet.”

“How do you know?”

“My mum would have made a face this morning.”

“What face?”

“The face where her eyebrows go very flat.”

Jess pulled at a loose thread on her skirt.

“She asked who Leo was.”

Mina lowered her voice although no one was nearby.

“What did you say?”

“I said he was in Year Eleven.”

“You told her?”

“She asked his year.”

“You could have picked Year Ten.”

“He’s tall.”

“So?”

“My mum has seen him.”

“When?”

“Outside the shop. He was holding that stupid scooter.”

Mina frowned.

“He has a bike.”

“He had Alfie’s scooter.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know. Boys carry each other’s rubbish around.”

Mina looked across the field.

“Did you tell her I was there?”

“Not yet.”

“Jess.”

“I’m going to.”

“When?”

“I don’t know.”

“You have to, because if she calls my mum—”

“I know.”

“My mum thinks I slept at your house.”

“Mine thinks you slept at mine.”

They both considered this.

Jess said, “That part is quite impressive.”

Mina covered her face with both hands.

“We’re dead.”

“My mum isn’t going to kill us.”

“She’ll tell school.”

“She might.”

“Then Haines will call my dad.”

“Your dad will be at work.”

“He has a phone at work.”

“Everybody has a phone at work.”

Mina dropped her hands.

“I hate you.”

Jess looked at her.

“Don’t look like that. I don’t really.”

“I wasn’t doing anything.”

“You have that face.”

“My normal face?”

“Your guilty face.”

Jess picked a blade of grass.

“I thought Friday was fun.”

Mina stared at the hockey goal.

“It was fun until Callum brought the vodka.”

“We didn’t drink it.”

“You had some.”

“I had one sip.”

“You coughed for ten minutes.”

“That was because it tasted like hand sanitizer.”

“Leo drank loads.”

“Leo’s an idiot.”

“You liked him.”

“For about an hour.”

“You held his hand.”

“His hands were freezing.”

Mina began laughing.

Jess tried not to, then laughed too.

They stopped when they heard a whistle.

Mina said, “We still have to do something.”

“Tonight I’ll tell her you were there.”

“No. Tell her the actual thing.”

Jess’s smile vanished.

“She’ll take my phone until Christmas.”

“It’s August.”

“I’m aware of the calendar.”

Mina picked at the label on her water bottle.

“My mum’s going to find out anyway.”

“We could tell them together.”

“In person?”

“Maybe.”

“My house?”

“Your mum scares me.”

“Your mum prints emails.”

“Fair.”

Mina thought.

“After school. Café by the bus station.”

“With both mums?”

“If they agree.”

“They’re going to ask why we need a café.”

“We can say we want chips.”

“They’re going to know something’s wrong.”

“They already know something’s wrong.”

Jess sighed.

“I wanted to go to Zara after school.”

“You can confess and then shop.”

“If I still have money.”

“Your mum can’t confiscate money.”

“She can if she paid me to wash the car and I did a terrible job.”

Mina stood and brushed grass from her skirt.

“Come on.”

“Where?”

“Lunch ends in three minutes.”

Jess got up, limping slightly.

“My sock feels horrible.”

“You can change it in PE.”

“I don’t have another sock.”

“Take it off.”

“And wear one sock?”

“No one looks at your ankles.”

“Olivia does.”

“Olivia looks at everyone because she’s bored.”

They started toward the main building.

Halfway across the field, Jess said, “Do you still have Leo’s number?”

“Yes.”

“Delete it.”

“Why?”

“He sent me a picture this morning.”

“What picture?”

“Nothing terrible. Just his face.”

Mina walked a few steps.

“Was his hair good?”

Jess gave her a sideways look.

“Annoyingly good.”

Mina groaned.

“Fine. I’ll delete it after school.”

“Before the mums.”

“After chips.”

“Fine.”

They began to run when the second whistle went.

4. A Married Couple After an Affair

Paul was repairing the loose hinge on the hallway cupboard when Anna came downstairs with a laundry basket.

She stopped on the last stair.

“You’ve had that screwdriver out for three days.”

“The screw keeps slipping.”

“There are more in the garage.”

“Different size.”

Anna carried the basket into the living room and set it beside the sofa.

Paul remained crouched by the cupboard.

“Do you want coffee?”

“No.”

“I’m making some.”

“I had one upstairs.”

“With milk?”

“Yes.”

He tightened the screw, tested the door, and tightened it again.

Anna began folding a towel.

“The solicitor sent the forms.”

Paul’s hand stopped.

“Today?”

“Yesterday. I didn’t see the email until this morning.”

“What forms?”

“The first set. Finances, pensions, property.”

“All right.”

“You’ll have to find the mortgage statements.”

“They’re online.”

“She wants three years.”

“I can download them.”

“And the old loan paperwork.”

“That might be in the filing cabinet.”

“I looked.”

“Garage cupboard?”

“I haven’t gone through that.”

Paul stood, flexing his knees.

“I’ll check.”

Anna folded another towel.

“They ask for the date we separated.”

He leaned the screwdriver against the wall.

“What did you put?”

“I haven’t filled it in.”

“We’re still in the same house.”

“There’s a box.”

Paul walked toward the kitchen, then came back.

“June?”

Anna stared at a towel in her hands.

“June was when you told me.”

“Yes.”

“You’d been sleeping in the spare room since April.”

“Because I was snoring.”

“You slept there every night.”

“I was trying to give you space.”

Anna placed the folded towel on the sofa.

“We can put June.”

“All right.”

Paul went into the kitchen. She heard the coffee machine grinding.

He called, “There’s still some of that soup.”

“I’ll eat it later.”

“It needs using.”

“I said I’ll eat it.”

A cupboard closed.

Paul came back carrying a mug.

“I saw Claire.”

Anna’s hands went still.

“At Tesco.”

“When?”

“Saturday.”

“You went to Tesco Saturday?”

“For light bulbs.”

“You came back with light bulbs.”

“Yes.”

Anna picked up one of Ben’s T-shirts from the basket and began folding it.

“What happened?”

“She was near the pharmacy.”

“And?”

“We spoke for a minute.”

Anna folded the shirt once, unfolded it, folded it again.

“Does she still work there?”

“At the clinic? I think so.”

“You think so.”

“She didn’t talk about work.”

“What did you talk about?”

“Her mother’s operation.”

Anna looked at him for the first time.

“You stood in Tesco talking about her mother.”

“Her mother had surgery.”

“I remember.”

Paul drank coffee.

“She asked about Ben.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That he started college.”

“He hasn’t started.”

“He starts next month.”

“He got the place. He hasn’t started.”

Paul looked toward the window.

“I didn’t give her a report.”

Anna stacked the folded clothes.

“Did she ask about me?”

“Yes.”

“What did you say?”

“That you were fine.”

Anna gave a small nod and continued folding.

Paul sat in the armchair across from her.

After a while he said, “She looks older.”

Anna kept her eyes on the laundry.

“I don’t know why I said that.”

Neither spoke.

A delivery van stopped outside. Its reverse alarm sounded several times, then faded.

Anna said, “I found the hotel receipt.”

Paul put his mug down.

“Which one?”

“Bath.”

He leaned back.

“In your jacket pocket. The blue jacket.”

“I haven’t worn that in months.”

“I was checking pockets before taking clothes to the charity shop.”

“Right.”

“It was two nights.”

“Yes.”

“You told me one.”

Paul stared at his hands.

Anna waited.

“It was Friday and Saturday.”

“I thought you came home Saturday.”

“Sunday morning.”

“You called Saturday afternoon.”

“From the hotel.”

“I remember the call.”

Paul rubbed his thumb over a pale mark on his wedding ring.

Anna said, “I spent the evening making lasagne. Ben had two friends here. I left your portion in the oven until eleven.”

“I remember seeing the dish.”

“Then why tell me you’d stayed one night?”

“I was trying to get through that conversation.”

She picked up a sock, searched for its pair.

“Did you ever take her to Brighton?”

“No.”

“Oxford?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“February.”

“Hotel?”

“Day trip.”

“For work?”

“No.”

Anna found the matching sock.

“How many places?”

Paul looked at the floor.

“I don’t have a list.”

“Try.”

“Bath. Oxford. Her flat. Once at that place near Reading.”

“What place?”

“A hotel off the motorway.”

Anna’s face tightened.

“Was that when you said the conference ran late?”

“Yes.”

She put the socks down.

“December ninth?”

“I’d have to check.”

“It was the ninth. I had the school concert.”

Paul pressed his palms together.

“I’m sorry.”

Anna turned toward the window.

“You’ve been saying that for months.”

“I don’t know what else to say.”

“You don’t need to fill the room.”

He nodded.

They sat in silence.

After perhaps a minute Anna picked up another shirt.

“Ben wants the car Friday.”

“I need it until four.”

“He wants to leave at three.”

“Where’s he going?”

“Manchester.”

“He can take the train.”

“He’s taking equipment.”

“What equipment?”

“Speakers. Something for the flat.”

Paul reached for his coffee.

“I can come back early.”

“Tell him.”

“I will.”

Anna folded the shirt.

“The estate agent is coming Thursday.”

“Which one?”

“Green & Hall.”

“What time?”

“Eleven.”

“I’ll work from the office.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I’d rather not be here.”

Anna nodded.

Paul stood and picked up the screwdriver.

“The hinge is fixed.”

She glanced at the cupboard.

“It’s crooked.”

He looked at it.

“It is.”

He crouched again and loosened the screw.

Anna carried the folded towels upstairs.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

Captain Morales found Sergeant Hsu outside the vehicle bay, sitting on an ammunition crate with his helmet between his boots.

“Sergeant.”

Hsu stood.

“Ma’am.”

“Sit down.”

He remained standing until Morales sat on the crate opposite. Then he lowered himself again.

She held out a canteen.

“I have water.”

“Take it.”

He took the canteen and drank.

Morales looked past him toward the ridge. A helicopter moved low over the horizon, its sound reaching them several seconds late.

“How many?”

“Four wounded. One serious.”

“Names.”

“Dawes, Mercer, Bell, Ortiz. Ortiz is the serious one.”

“Evacuated?”

“Ten minutes ago.”

“Conscious?”

“When they loaded him.”

“And the vehicle?”

“Axle’s gone. Front left wheel assembly too. Recovery says they need a second truck.”

Morales rubbed dust from her eyebrow.

“Walk me through it.”

Hsu stared at the ground.

“We left Checkpoint Three at fourteen-twenty. Convoy interval was twenty-five metres. Visibility was poor near the southern bend.”

“Why poor?”

“Dust from the lead vehicle.”

“Speed?”

“Thirty.”

“The report from Keller says forty.”

Hsu looked up.

“We were around thirty. Maybe thirty-five going downhill.”

“Continue.”

“We cleared the bend. I saw two civilians near the wall. One crossed. Driver moved left. I told him to hold lane.”

“Who was driving?”

“Bell.”

“Then?”

“There was a flash under the front quarter. Vehicle lifted. I don’t remember the sound properly. We went sideways.”

Morales nodded toward his sleeve.

“You’re bleeding.”

Hsu looked at a cut near his elbow.

“Glass.”

“Get it cleaned.”

“I will.”

“Continue.”

“Dawes was out of the rear before I was. Mercer couldn’t move his leg. Ortiz was under the radio mount.”

“Where was your weapon?”

“Floor.”

“Secured?”

Hsu hesitated.

“No.”

Morales looked at him.

“The strap clip broke two days ago.”

“Did you report it?”

“I put in a replacement request.”

“To whom?”

“Supply.”

“When?”

“Sunday.”

“Did you tell Lieutenant Harris?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“He already had six vehicle faults waiting. I thought I could secure it with the secondary strap.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

“And it came loose.”

“Yes.”

Morales watched two mechanics cross the bay carrying a jack.

“Did it hit anyone?”

“No.”

“You’re certain?”

“Yes.”

She took the canteen back.

“Harris says you wanted the route changed this morning.”

Hsu shifted his feet.

“I asked about Route Blue.”

“Why?”

“There was traffic on Red yesterday. Two market trucks parked near the canal.”

“Anything else?”

“Kids.”

“What about them?”

“They were throwing rocks at the barriers.”

“That happens.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

Morales waited.

Hsu rubbed dust from his palms.

“One of the interpreters heard people talking about the southern road.”

“Which interpreter?”

“Rahim.”

“What did he hear?”

“Something about soldiers using the same turn every day.”

“And you told Harris?”

“I mentioned the market trucks.”

Morales’s face remained still.

“Did Rahim tell you himself?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Last night.”

“Where?”

“Dining tent.”

“What were his exact concerns?”

Hsu stared at the ridge again.

“He said his cousin heard men at a tea shop. They were talking about the convoy turning by the old pumping station. He didn’t know who they were.”

“And you decided the information wasn’t strong enough to pass on.”

“I thought I needed something firmer.”

Morales leaned forward, forearms on her knees.

“How long have you been here?”

“Five months.”

“How many patrols?”

“Seventy-three.”

“And how many times have you received vague local warnings?”

“Quite a few.”

“How many were useful?”

“Some.”

“Give me a number.”

“Maybe five. Six.”

Morales sat back.

Hsu swallowed.

“I should have passed it.”

“Yes.”

“I can write the statement now.”

“You will. Medical first.”

“I’m fine.”

“Your hands are shaking.”

Hsu looked down at them.

“They’ll stop.”

“Probably.”

The helicopter sound faded.

Hsu said, “Ortiz asked me if his legs were there.”

Morales waited.

“He couldn’t feel them. He kept asking. Dawes was holding his shoulder. I could see both legs. I told him they were there.”

Morales nodded.

“He heard you?”

“I think so.”

“What happened after?”

“He asked about his watch.”

“His watch.”

“He lost it.”

Hsu pressed his palms against his thighs.

“I kept telling him the medics were coming. He wanted the watch. I found it under the seat.”

“Did you give it to him?”

“I put it in his vest.”

“Good.”

A radio crackled inside the vehicle bay.

Morales stood.

“The board will interview you tonight or tomorrow morning.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Give them everything. Rahim, the conversation, the route request, the broken clip, your estimate of speed.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Don’t clean up the story.”

Hsu nodded.

Morales started toward the operations building.

“Captain.”

She turned.

“If Ortiz dies, will they tell us before the board?”

“Yes.”

“Could you—”

His voice failed. He looked away.

Morales waited.

“If you hear before I do.”

“I’ll find you.”

Hsu gave a short nod.

She pointed toward the medical tent.

“Go.”

He picked up his helmet and walked away, carrying it under one arm.

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The last bus had been cancelled, though the sign at the roadside still claimed it would arrive in nine minutes.

Nora had checked the sign three times. The man beside her had checked his phone more often.

He wore a dark suit under a waterproof jacket and was holding a cake box.

Nora said, “Do you think that’s leaning?”

He looked at the box.

“A little.”

“I’ve been trying not to mention it.”

“I’m aware.”

“Can you straighten it?”

He adjusted the box against his chest.

“Better?”

“Marginally.”

“It’s survived a train, a taxi and half an hour of rain.”

“What kind?”

“Cake?”

“Yes.”

“Pistachio.”

“Expensive.”

He looked at her.

“My sister chose it.”

“Birthday?”

“Engagement dinner.”

Nora checked the empty road.

“You’re late.”

“Very.”

“How late?”

“Dinner was at seven.”

She looked at her watch.

“It’s nearly ten.”

“Yes.”

“That’s beyond late.”

“I had to go back for the cake.”

“Where was it?”

“My office refrigerator.”

“Why was an engagement cake in your office?”

“I ordered it near work.”

Nora nodded as though this made enough sense.

A gust of rain blew under the shelter.

She moved closer to the glass panel.

“Your shoes are soaked.”

“So are yours.”

“Mine are cheap.”

“That probably helps.”

“It doesn’t.”

The electronic sign changed from NINE MINUTES to CANCELLED.

Nora stared at it.

“There we are.”

The man looked.

“That’s the third one.”

“You’ve been here for three?”

“Forty-five minutes.”

“You could have walked.”

“To Millbrook?”

“Oh. No.”

“Where are you going?”

“St Anne’s.”

“That’s halfway.”

“Almost.”

He put the cake carefully on the bench.

“I’m Adrian.”

“Nora.”

They shook hands awkwardly because his hand was cold and hers was wet.

Adrian took out his phone.

“No taxis.”

“I tried.”

“Did you try Falcon?”

“Yes.”

“City Cars?”

“Yes.”

“The app?”

“Two apps.”

He sighed.

“My sister is going to remember this forever.”

“Is she the kind who remembers?”

“She remembers weather from arguments we had as children.”

Nora smiled.

“My brother forgets entire years.”

“That sounds peaceful.”

“He forgets birthdays too.”

“Less peaceful.”

A car passed without slowing.

Nora tucked her hands into her coat pockets.

“What are you doing at St Anne’s this late?”

“My father’s there.”

“Hospital?”

“Yes.”

Adrian glanced at her and then away.

“Sorry.”

“He had surgery this morning.”

“How did it go?”

“They say well.”

“Are you allowed in this late?”

“The nurse said I could come. I was working.”

Nora watched rain gather on the edge of the shelter roof.

“I nearly didn’t.”

“Go?”

“Yes.”

Adrian looked down the road.

“Long day?”

“Normal day. Then I sat at my desk for an hour pretending to finish things.”

“What do you do?”

“Insurance.”

“That could take an hour.”

“It can take a lifetime.”

Adrian laughed softly.

Nora said, “What about you?”

“Architect.”

“You look like an architect.”

He turned toward her.

“How?”

“I don’t know. Black clothes. Sad cake.”

He looked at the box.

“The cake has done nothing wrong.”

“That’s true.”

They stood quietly for a while.

Adrian’s phone buzzed.

He read the message.

“My sister says they’ve eaten.”

“The cake?”

“Everything else.”

“Good.”

“She says I should go home.”

“Will you?”

“No.”

Nora looked down the road.

“How far is Millbrook from St Anne’s?”

“Five miles, maybe six.”

“Could someone from your dinner get you?”

“They’ve been drinking.”

“Ah.”

“My parents are already there. My brother-in-law’s brother came from Leeds. People have travelled.”

“And you brought the cake.”

“I was meant to bring myself as well.”

Rain rattled against the roof.

Nora said, “There’s a twenty-four-hour petrol station about a mile up.”

“I saw it from the train.”

“They might have a taxi number.”

“They might.”

“And coffee.”

“That sounds useful.”

Neither moved.

Adrian said, “You’re going to the hospital.”

“Yes.”

“I could walk there with you and keep going.”

“In this?”

“I have an umbrella.”

Nora looked at the narrow umbrella sticking from his bag.

“That thing?”

“It has eight ribs.”

“It looks like it has three.”

“It lost one in Brussels.”

“That leaves seven.”

“I may have exaggerated the original specification.”

She smiled.

“The cake won’t survive.”

Adrian looked at the box.

“You’re very concerned about it.”

“I’ve become invested.”

He lifted the lid a centimetre and looked inside.

“Oh.”

“What?”

He opened it wider.

The cake had slid sideways and pressed itself against one wall of the box. A crescent of green icing clung to the cardboard.

Nora leaned over.

“That’s bad.”

“It used to have writing.”

“What did it say?”

“Congratulations, Maya and Felix.”

“How much can you still read?”

“‘Aya and Fix.’”

Nora laughed, covering her mouth.

Adrian stared into the box, then began laughing too.

For a minute neither could stop.

When he recovered, he closed the lid.

“I carried this through Paddington above my head.”

“It had a good life.”

“I was threatened by a cyclist.”

“You protected it.”

“I protected this.”

He lifted the box.

Nora wiped rain from her cheek.

“Come on. Petrol station.”

Adrian picked up his bag.

They stepped out from under the shelter.

His umbrella opened with a violent snap and immediately turned inside out.

Nora looked at it.

Adrian wrestled with the frame.

“It usually behaves better.”

“Leave it.”

“I can fix it.”

“You’ll stab yourself.”

He collapsed it and tucked it back into the bag.

They started walking.

After a hundred metres Adrian said, “Do you want some cake?”

“Now?”

“There’s no point presenting it.”

“With our hands?”

“I have plastic forks.”

Nora looked over.

“Why?”

“The bakery gave me six.”

They walked another few steps through the rain.

“Fine,” she said. “At the petrol station.”

“Coffee too.”

“Coffee first.”

“Fair.”

Ahead of them, the petrol station sign glowed through the wet dark, blue-white and strangely welcoming.
