1. A mother and her adult son

The freezer door would not close.

Nina stood with one hand on the handle and the other braced against the refrigerator, leaning her weight into it.

“Don’t,” Pavel said from the doorway.

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re trying to force it.”

“I’m closing a door.”

“You’re seventy-three. You’re wrestling an appliance.”

Nina released the handle. The freezer door sprang open by three inches.

“There. You see? It’s spoiled.”

“It isn’t spoiled.”

“You bought me a spoiled refrigerator.”

“I bought it eight years ago.”

“Then it lasted eight years. That’s respectable.”

Pavel took off his coat and laid it over a chair. He had come straight from work, still wearing the dark trousers and blue shirt that made him look, Nina thought, like somebody waiting to give evidence in court.

He crouched beside the freezer.

“What’s in here?”

“Food.”

“Yes, I had gathered that.”

“Don’t throw anything away.”

“I haven’t touched anything.”

“You’re thinking about it.”

Pavel pulled out the top drawer. It stopped halfway.

Behind it, something wrapped in newspaper had fallen against the back wall.

“What’s this?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing has been freezing solid since approximately 1998.”

“Give it here.”

He unwrapped the parcel.

Inside was a small plastic container, yellowed with age.

Pavel looked at his mother.

“Nina Dmitrievna.”

“Don’t Nina Dmitrievna me.”

“What is it?”

“Mushrooms.”

“They’re black.”

“They’re mushrooms.”

“They’ve developed a political position.”

“Your father picked them.”

Pavel stopped.

Nina reached for the container. He gave it to her.

“When?”

“The last autumn.”

“You’ve kept frozen mushrooms for eleven years?”

“They were good mushrooms.”

“Mama.”

“What?”

“You can’t eat them.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Then why keep them?”

She put the container on the table.

The refrigerator hummed behind them. Pavel pushed the freezer drawer back in. The door closed easily.

“There,” he said. “Mystery solved.”

“You came here to repair the refrigerator.”

“I did repair it.”

“You removed your father.”

“I removed mushrooms.”

“Same thing, apparently.”

Pavel looked at her.

Nina sat down. After a moment, she said, “There’s soup.”

“I ate.”

“You always say that.”

“Because I generally have.”

“I made chicken.”

“I’ll have some.”

“That’s what I thought.”

She took a saucepan from the stove. Pavel remained crouched beside the refrigerator.

“Mama.”

“What now?”

“I spoke to the estate agent.”

The ladle stopped above the bowl.

“What estate?”

“The flat.”

“What flat?”

“This flat.”

Nina set the ladle down.

“You spoke to an estate agent about my flat?”

“You said last month you might consider moving.”

“I said the stairs were annoying.”

“You said you were tired of them.”

“I am tired of lots of things. I’m tired of television presenters. I’m not selling the television.”

“It’s four floors and no lift.”

“I know how many floors there are. I have lived on all of them.”

“You fell in March.”

“I slipped.”

“You fell in June.”

“The pavement was wet.”

“You fell here last week.”

Nina looked toward the window.

“That was a stool.”

“That was you standing on a stool to reach the top cupboard.”

“The flour was there.”

“I can move the flour.”

“And then what? Move the plates? Move the bath lower? Move the street closer?”

Pavel got up slowly.

“I found a place near me. Small building. Lift. Balcony. Grocery store downstairs.”

“Near you.”

“Yes.”

“So you can inspect me efficiently.”

“So I can help.”

“Same thing with better manners.”

He sat opposite her.

For several seconds neither spoke.

Then Nina pushed the bowl of soup toward him.

“Eat before it gets cold.”

Pavel picked up the spoon.

“You don’t have to decide now.”

“I’ve decided.”

“No, you haven’t.”

“I’m not moving.”

“All right.”

“You’re doing that thing.”

“What thing?”

“Agreeing because you plan to ask again.”

He smiled despite himself.

“You taught me persistence.”

“I taught you not to chew with your mouth open. Results were mixed.”

Pavel ate a spoonful.

“It’s good.”

“Of course.”

Nina turned the plastic container between her hands.

After a while she said, “Does it have a proper balcony?”

He looked up.

“What?”

“This place.”

“Yes.”

“Not one of those ridiculous ledges where a pigeon has to stand sideways?”

“No. A real balcony.”

“Facing where?”

“West, I think.”

“You think.”

“I can check.”

“Check.”

Pavel nodded.

Nina looked down at the mushrooms.

“And don’t tell the estate agent anything yet.”

“I won’t.”

“I’m only asking.”

“I know.”

She got up, opened the rubbish bin, and stood over it for a long moment.

Then she dropped the container inside.

Pavel said nothing.

“Eat your soup,” Nina said.

“I am.”

“And tomorrow you can move the flour.”

2. Two old diplomats

Sir Edward Vale had been waiting for fifteen minutes when Ambassador Kirov arrived.

“You’re late,” Vale said.

“I’m retired.”

“So am I.”

“Then neither of us can be late.”

Kirov removed his hat and examined the café.

It was nearly empty. Rain touched the windows in fine diagonal lines. Between them sat a chessboard with the pieces already arranged.

“You brought a board,” Kirov said.

“You complained last time.”

“I complained that you cheated.”

“I won.”

“You moved a bishop while I was ordering cognac.”

“That allegation was investigated and found unsupported.”

“By whom?”

“Me.”

Kirov sat.

“You haven’t changed.”

“I’ve become considerably more expensive to maintain.”

“That is not change. That is inflation.”

Vale pushed the white pieces toward him.

“You first.”

“Why?”

“Your country always preferred the initiative.”

“My country preferred strategic patience.”

“Your country invaded three neighboring states during my posting.”

“Strategically.”

Kirov moved a pawn.

Vale smiled.

For ten minutes they played without speaking.

Then Kirov said, “Do you remember Halberg?”

“Which one?”

“The Swedish man. Arms control.”

“Oh, God. The eyebrows.”

“They entered rooms before he did.”

“He’s dead.”

“I know.”

Vale moved a knight.

“Funeral?”

“Last Thursday.”

“You went?”

“No.”

“Neither did I.”

Kirov looked at the board.

“We are becoming the people nobody attends.”

“Nonsense. I intend to have excellent catering.”

“You think that will help?”

“People forgive almost anything for decent smoked salmon.”

Kirov snorted.

A waiter brought coffee without being asked.

Vale lifted his cup.

“He remembers us.”

“He remembers the tips.”

“At our age, motives cease to matter.”

Kirov stirred sugar into his coffee.

“Halberg told me something once.”

“That seems unlikely. Halberg mostly told himself things while others were present.”

“In Geneva. Ninety-four. He said the mistake diplomats make is believing governments want solutions.”

Vale stopped with his hand above a rook.

“Governments want advantages.”

“That was my answer.”

“And his?”

“They want problems they can survive.”

Vale leaned back.

“Not bad.”

“No.”

“Annoying man.”

“Extremely.”

The rain thickened.

Kirov moved his queen.

“Check.”

Vale stared at the board.

“That’s vulgar.”

“It’s legal.”

“Not everything legal is civilized.”

“You said precisely the opposite during the maritime negotiations.”

“I was representing national interests.”

“And now?”

“Now I’m representing a king who appears to be in danger.”

Kirov smiled.

Vale moved.

The game continued.

After several minutes, Vale said, “My granddaughter asked me what I did.”

“You lied, naturally.”

“I said I negotiated treaties.”

“That is almost true.”

“She asked whether they worked.”

Kirov’s smile disappeared.

“What did you say?”

“That some did.”

“You are becoming sentimental.”

“I’m eighty-four. It would be suspicious if I weren’t.”

Kirov turned one of his captured pawns between thumb and forefinger.

“My grandson asked me if I had ever prevented a war.”

“And?”

“I said you can never know.”

Vale nodded.

“That is true.”

“He disliked the answer.”

“Children do. They expect history to issue receipts.”

Kirov looked toward the rain.

“There was that night in Vienna.”

Vale said nothing.

“You remember.”

“I remember Vienna.”

“Three in the morning.”

“Yes.”

“Your delegation thought we were mobilizing.”

“You were mobilizing.”

“We were exercising.”

“You were exercising remarkably close to a border.”

“It was a large country. Everything was close to some border.”

Vale laughed once.

Then both men became quiet.

Kirov said, “If you had walked out—”

“I nearly did.”

“I know.”

“How?”

“I could see your briefcase.”

“My briefcase?”

“You had picked it up.”

Vale looked surprised.

“You noticed that?”

“I spent thirty-seven years noticing when British men picked up briefcases.”

Vale’s hand rested beside the chessboard.

“You asked for ten minutes.”

“Yes.”

“And we stayed six hours.”

“Yes.”

“Nothing happened.”

Kirov nodded.

“Nothing happened.”

For a moment the phrase seemed to sit between them, larger than the table.

Vale looked down.

“Your move.”

“I have moved.”

“No, you haven’t.”

Kirov pointed.

Vale stared.

“You old bastard.”

“Checkmate.”

“That bishop was not there.”

“It has been there for four moves.”

“Impossible.”

“You were reminiscing.”

“You moved it while I was reminiscing.”

Kirov put on his hat.

“Prove it.”

Vale began resetting the pieces.

“Another game.”

“I have lunch.”

“With whom?”

“No one.”

“Then cancel.”

Kirov watched him arrange the board.

“You still cannot tolerate an unfavorable outcome.”

“My dear Alexei, tolerating unfavorable outcomes is diplomacy.”

“And chess?”

Vale pushed the white pieces toward him again.

“Chess is where we are allowed not to.”

3. Two schoolgirls

Mara found Elise behind the gymnasium with a screwdriver in her hand.

“What are you doing?”

Elise jumped.

“Nothing.”

“You’re unscrewing the school.”

“I need this.”

She held up a brass plaque.

Mara read it aloud.

“‘Donated by the graduating class of 1987.’ You need that?”

“Quiet.”

“You’re stealing a plaque.”

“I’m borrowing it.”

“For what?”

Elise tucked the screwdriver into her blazer pocket.

“You won’t understand.”

“You say that whenever you’ve done something stupid.”

“I haven’t done it yet.”

“You’re holding the evidence.”

“That’s preparation.”

Mara glanced toward the corner of the building.

Lunch break ended in nine minutes.

“Elise.”

“What?”

“Put it back.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Elise stared at the plaque.

Then she said, “Because Hartmann says the time capsule is under it.”

Mara blinked.

“What?”

“My brother told me. The class of ’87 put a time capsule behind this wall.”

“Your brother also told you the headmistress used to be in the circus.”

“She did gymnastics.”

“That is not a circus.”

“It’s adjacent.”

Mara crouched beside the wall.

There were four screw holes where the plaque had been.

Behind it: bricks.

“Congratulations.”

“It’s one of these.”

“One of these what?”

“Bricks.”

“You intend to remove a brick from the gymnasium?”

“Only temporarily.”

“With your extensive masonry training?”

Elise produced a small hammer from her bag.

Mara stared at it.

“You brought a hammer to school?”

“It’s my father’s.”

“That makes this better.”

“You can leave.”

“I should.”

“Then leave.”

Mara did not move.

Elise tapped a brick.

“Not that one.”

“How do you know?”

“It sounds solid.”

“They are bricks.”

“Help me.”

“No.”

“Just listen.”

“To bricks?”

“Yes.”

Mara sighed and took the hammer.

“My life was better before I met you.”

“You met me when we were six.”

“Exactly.”

They tapped along the wall.

Solid. Solid. Solid.

Then one brick gave a slightly hollow knock.

Both girls froze.

“Do it again,” Elise whispered.

Mara tapped.

Hollow.

Elise grinned.

“Oh my God.”

“This proves nothing.”

“It proves everything.”

“It proves one brick sounds different.”

“Move.”

Using the screwdriver, Elise scraped at the old mortar.

Mara kept watch.

“This is criminal damage.”

“Only if we damage it.”

“You’re currently removing mortar.”

“It was loose.”

“You made it loose.”

“It wanted freedom.”

A bell rang inside.

“Lunch is over,” Mara said.

“Two minutes.”

“That is not how bells work.”

Elise kept digging.

The brick shifted.

They looked at each other.

“No,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“No.”

“Yes.”

Together they pulled.

The brick came free.

Inside the cavity was a rusted biscuit tin.

For several seconds neither girl spoke.

Elise reached in.

“Wait.”

“What?”

“What if it’s disgusting?”

“It’s a time capsule.”

“It’s been inside a wall for thirty-nine years. That is how disgusting begins.”

Elise pulled it out anyway.

The lid resisted, then opened with a metallic crack.

Inside were photographs, a cassette tape, several folded letters, a packet of cigarettes, and a small envelope marked:

TO WHOEVER FINDS THIS: DO NOT GIVE IT TO A TEACHER.

Mara sat down.

“Oh.”

Elise looked triumphant.

“You were saying?”

“I withdraw several objections.”

She opened the envelope.

Inside was a single sheet of paper covered in names.

At the top:

THINGS WE ARE CERTAIN WILL HAVE HAPPENED BY 2020.

Elise began reading.

“‘Flying cars.’”

Mara smiled.

“Wrong.”

“‘No more homework because computers will do it.’”

“Tragically wrong.”

“‘The Soviet Union will still exist.’”

“Very wrong.”

“‘Michael Jackson will become president of America.’”

Mara laughed so loudly Elise slapped a hand over her mouth.

“Shut up!”

“I’m sorry.”

At the bottom was one final sentence.

Elise stopped smiling.

“What?”

She handed Mara the paper.

In different handwriting, somebody had added:

I hope we are still friends when someone reads this. — Anna

Below it:

We will be. Idiot. — Sophie

Mara read it twice.

Elise looked through the photographs.

“Which ones are they?”

“We don’t know.”

“We could find out.”

“How?”

“Yearbooks.”

Mara looked at her.

“You want to investigate two schoolgirls from 1987.”

“They’d be fifty-five now.”

“Probably.”

“We could find them.”

The second bell rang.

Mara picked up the brick.

“First we repair the wall.”

“You said you wouldn’t help.”

“That was before there was a mystery.”

Elise smiled.

“Hold the plaque.”

“After this, I’m confiscating your hammer.”

“It’s my father’s.”

“Then your father can submit a written appeal.”

4. A married couple after an affair

Daniel came home carrying oranges.

Laura was sitting at the kitchen table with the bank statements spread in front of her.

He stopped.

“I bought oranges.”

“I can see that.”

“They were on sale.”

“Congratulations.”

Daniel put the bag on the counter.

One orange rolled out, crossed the worktop, and fell to the floor.

Neither of them picked it up.

Laura tapped one of the statements.

“What’s Hotel Linden?”

Daniel closed his eyes.

“When?”

“October.”

“Laura.”

“What’s Hotel Linden?”

“You know what it is.”

“I know what I think it is.”

He pulled out a chair.

“Can I sit?”

“It’s your house too.”

He sat.

Laura folded the statement neatly.

“You told me it happened twice.”

“It did.”

“September and November.”

Daniel said nothing.

“This is October.”

“I met her there.”

“You met her.”

“Yes.”

“At a hotel.”

“Yes.”

“But nothing happened.”

“No.”

Laura laughed once.

Daniel looked at the floor.

“Nothing happened.”

“Do you hear yourself?”

“Yes.”

“Because I don’t think you do.”

“I met her in the bar.”

“At the hotel where you slept with her in September.”

“Yes.”

“And then again in November.”

“Yes.”

“But October was purely administrative.”

“No.”

“What was it?”

“She wanted me to leave you.”

Laura became still.

Daniel continued before he could stop himself.

“She said she couldn’t keep doing it unless I made a decision.”

“And what did you say?”

“I said I wasn’t leaving.”

Laura looked down at the statement.

“So that was the noble month.”

“I’m not saying that.”

“You went to meet your mistress and bravely chose your wife.”

“I know how it sounds.”

“No, Daniel. You know how it sounds when I say it. I’m asking what it sounded like when you said it to yourself.”

He did not answer.

The refrigerator compressor started.

Laura got up and retrieved the orange from the floor. She rinsed it under the tap.

“I keep trying to find the version where I understand.”

“There isn’t one.”

“There has to be.”

“No.”

“People don’t detonate their lives for no reason.”

“I didn’t think I was detonating anything.”

“That is almost worse.”

“I know.”

“Stop saying that.”

“All right.”

She dried the orange.

“Did you love her?”

Daniel looked at her.

“I don’t know.”

“You’ve had six months to invent an answer.”

“I’ve had six months to realize I don’t know what I meant by half the things I was feeling.”

Laura put the orange back in the bag.

“That sounds rehearsed.”

“It probably is. I’ve thought it a thousand times.”

“And me?”

“What about you?”

“Did you love me?”

“Yes.”

“While you were sleeping with her?”

“Yes.”

She stared at him.

“That should be impossible.”

“I know.”

“Stop.”

“Sorry.”

Laura sat again.

Daniel reached toward the statements, then withdrew his hand.

“I was angry all the time,” he said.

“At me?”

“At everything. Work. My father dying. Turning forty-five. You.”

“Me.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because you knew me.”

Laura frowned.

“That’s your complaint?”

“I could come home in a bad mood and you knew why before I did. You knew when I was lying about being fine. You knew when I was scared. With her, I could be whoever I said I was.”

“And who did you say you were?”

Daniel thought.

“Someone uncomplicated.”

Laura almost smiled.

“You?”

“I know.”

She covered her face briefly with both hands.

“I hate that I can understand that.”

“I don’t want you to.”

“I know. You’d prefer to be simply monstrous.”

“It would be cleaner.”

“For you.”

“For both of us.”

“No. For me, monstrous would be easier to leave.”

Daniel looked at her.

She turned away.

“Does that mean—”

“It means nothing.”

“All right.”

“Don’t make me reassure you.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were about to.”

He nodded.

“Yes.”

Laura gathered the statements into a pile.

“I booked a room.”

Daniel’s expression changed.

“Where?”

“Not for that.”

“I didn’t—”

“Yes, you did.”

She pushed the papers aside.

“A hotel. Friday night. Alone.”

He swallowed.

“Okay.”

“I want twenty-four hours where nobody is asking me whether we’re going to survive.”

“I haven’t asked.”

“You ask every time you look at me.”

Daniel stared at his hands.

“All right.”

“I may come back and say I’m done.”

“I know.”

“And I may come back and say we try.”

He nodded.

Laura stood.

“Do you want an orange?”

Daniel looked up, startled.

“What?”

“An orange.”

“Yes.”

She took two from the bag and handed him one.

He peeled it carefully over the table.

After a moment, Laura said, “You always leave too much white stuff on them.”

Daniel looked at the orange.

“I know.”

She gave him a warning look.

He almost laughed.

Then, unexpectedly, she did too.

It lasted only a second.

But neither of them apologized for it.

5. An officer and a subordinate

Captain Sato found Corporal Venn asleep in the radio room.

He shut the door behind him.

Venn woke instantly.

“Sir.”

“Good morning.”

“Sir, I can explain.”

“It’s two in the afternoon.”

“Yes, sir.”

“So begin by explaining what happened to morning.”

Venn stood.

He was twenty-two and looked younger when frightened.

Sato picked up the logbook.

The last entry was at 11:42.

“Three hours.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You were responsible for this station.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You left the radio unattended for three hours.”

“No, sir.”

Sato looked at him.

Venn’s face changed.

“I mean—I was here.”

“Asleep.”

“Yes, sir.”

“So unattended by the technical definition that the person attending it was unconscious.”

“Yes, sir.”

Sato opened the logbook.

“Any transmissions?”

“None.”

“How do you know?”

Venn hesitated.

Sato put the book down.

“Corporal.”

“The recorder.”

Sato turned toward the field recorder.

Its indicator was blinking.

He rewound the tape.

Static filled the room.

Then a voice.

“…Seven calling Base. Seven calling Base. Do you copy?”

Sato stopped the tape.

Venn had gone pale.

“When?”

“Sir—”

Sato played it again.

A timestamp flashed: 12:18.

Another transmission followed at 12:24.

Then 12:31.

The final one was faint.

“…movement on the eastern road. Repeat, movement on—”

Static.

Sato switched off the machine.

“How long have you been awake?”

“Since you came in.”

“I mean before you fell asleep.”

Venn said nothing.

“How long?”

“Thirty-eight hours.”

Sato’s eyes narrowed.

“Why?”

“Private Ko was sick.”

“That accounts for one shift.”

“Sergeant Melnik asked me to cover the checkpoint.”

“When?”

“Last night.”

“You covered the radio post yesterday, Ko’s shift overnight, checkpoint at dawn, and this station today?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Who authorized that?”

“No one, sir.”

“That answer is incompatible with the sentence ‘Sergeant Melnik asked me.’”

Venn swallowed.

“He didn’t know about Ko.”

“And Ko?”

“He asked me not to report him.”

“Why?”

“He thinks they’ll send him back.”

“He has pneumonia.”

“He has a cough.”

“He coughed blood on my boots.”

Sato stared at him.

Venn looked down.

“Sir.”

“How many people know?”

“About Ko?”

“Yes.”

“Me. Maybe Private Ilyin.”

“And you concealed it.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Then volunteered for his duties.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Then allowed Sergeant Melnik to assign you more duties.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And finally fell asleep during an operational watch.”

“Yes, sir.”

Sato walked to the window.

Beyond the glass, the camp shimmered in the dry afternoon heat.

“Do you consider yourself responsible for what happened?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Entirely?”

Venn hesitated.

“Yes.”

Sato turned.

“Wrong.”

Venn looked up.

“You are responsible for concealing Ko’s condition. You are responsible for accepting a duty you were no longer capable of performing. You are responsible for failing to tell your sergeant you had not slept.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You are not responsible for a system in which nobody noticed a corporal had been on duty for thirty-eight hours.”

Venn said nothing.

“That part belongs to me.”

“Sir, you couldn’t have known.”

“That is a comforting sentence. Never offer it to an officer.”

Sato picked up the telephone.

Venn stiffened.

“Sir?”

“I’m sending a patrol east.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Then Ko goes to medical.”

“He’ll be furious.”

“I will attempt to endure it.”

“And me?”

Sato looked at him.

“You are confined to quarters.”

“Yes, sir.”

“For twelve hours.”

Venn blinked.

“Sir?”

“You will sleep.”

“Sir, I—”

“That is an order.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Afterward, you will report to me, and we will discuss disciplinary action.”

Venn nodded.

Sato dialed operations.

Before the call connected, Venn said, “Sir.”

“What?”

“The transmission.”

“Yes?”

“If something happened to Seven—”

“Then we deal with what happened.”

“Because I was asleep.”

“Because many things happened. One of them was that you were asleep.”

The line clicked alive.

Sato gave the patrol order, concise and calm.

When he finished, Venn was still standing there.

“Why are you here?”

“Sir?”

“I issued an order twelve seconds ago.”

Venn moved toward the door.

Then stopped.

“Captain.”

Sato waited.

“I’m sorry.”

Sato looked at the recorder, then at the young man.

“Be useful instead.”

“Yes, sir.”

Venn left.

Sato remained alone in the radio room.

After a moment, he opened the duty roster.

He began reading every name from the top.

6. Two people who barely know each other

The train stopped in the tunnel at 9:17 p.m.

At 9:26, the lights went out.

At 9:28, the emergency lights came on, bathing the carriage in a weak amber glow.

At 9:31, the woman across from Tomas said, “I think that man is dead.”

Tomas looked up from his phone.

“What man?”

She pointed three rows down.

An elderly passenger was leaning against the window, his chin on his chest.

“He’s asleep.”

“I’ve been watching him.”

“That sentence makes me less confident in your judgment.”

“He hasn’t moved.”

“People do that when asleep.”

“Not breathing.”

Tomas stared at her.

She was perhaps thirty, wearing a green raincoat and carrying a paper bag full of something that smelled strongly of coriander.

“You checked?”

“No.”

“Then how do you know?”

“I can’t see his chest moving.”

“You’re fifteen feet away.”

“Fine.”

She stood.

“Where are you going?”

“To check.”

“Why me?”

“I didn’t ask you.”

“You’ve somehow made it feel like you did.”

She walked down the carriage.

Tomas sighed, put his phone in his pocket, and followed.

The old man did look alarmingly still.

The woman touched his shoulder.

“Sir?”

No response.

She touched his neck.

Tomas said, “Do you know how to do that?”

“No.”

“Excellent.”

“You do it.”

“I don’t.”

“So we are equally qualified.”

“I’m qualified to panic quietly.”

She leaned closer.

Then the old man snored.

Both of them jumped.

The old man opened one eye.

“What?”

“Nothing,” the woman said.

“Are we there?”

“No.”

“Then why are you waking me?”

“Sorry.”

He closed his eyes again.

They returned to their seats.

Tomas said, “Strong work.”

“I was being responsible.”

“You nearly resuscitated a sleeping pensioner.”

“I touched his neck.”

“Without a license.”

She smiled.

“Tomas.”

He frowned.

“What?”

“That’s your name.”

“How do you know?”

“Your work badge.”

He looked down. His conference badge was still clipped to his jacket.

TOMAS REITER — URBAN SYSTEMS LAB.

“Right.”

“I’m Lena.”

“Do I get evidence?”

“No.”

“Convenient.”

The train remained motionless.

A speaker crackled overhead, producing three seconds of static and nothing else.

Lena looked up.

“That inspires confidence.”

“They’re probably resetting something.”

“You work in urban systems. Is that an expert opinion?”

“No. My expert opinion is that somewhere a technician is saying a very bad word.”

She laughed.

“What do urban systems people actually do?”

“Mostly presentations explaining why projects cost three times more than expected.”

“So you build trains?”

“No.”

“Stations?”

“No.”

“Tracks?”

“No.”

“What do you do?”

“I model passenger movement.”

Lena stared.

“You make imaginary people walk through imaginary stations.”

“When you phrase it correctly, yes.”

“And are they trapped in this imaginary tunnel?”

“Never. My imaginary system is excellent.”

She opened the paper bag.

“Want a dumpling?”

Tomas looked inside.

“Where did those come from?”

“My mother.”

“Are they safe?”

“You followed a stranger toward a possibly dead man, but the dumpling is where you draw the line?”

“Fair.”

He took one.

They ate.

At 9:42, the emergency lights flickered.

Lena said, “I hate tunnels.”

Tomas glanced at her.

“You seem calm.”

“I’m performing calm.”

“You’re convincing.”

“Thank you. I practiced in elevators.”

He waited.

“Actually?”

“Actually.”

She folded the paper bag shut.

“When I was fourteen, I got stuck in one for six hours.”

“Alone?”

“With my piano teacher.”

“That sounds worse.”

“She cried the entire time.”

“Oh.”

“I had to reassure her.”

“At fourteen.”

“Yes.”

“That seems backward.”

“She was having a difficult divorce.”

Tomas looked at her.

“You know a surprising amount about your piano teacher’s marriage.”

“Six hours.”

“Right.”

The carriage shook slightly.

Someone farther down muttered, “Finally.”

But the train did not move.

Lena exhaled through her nose.

Tomas said, “Do you still play?”

“Piano?”

“Yes.”

“No. You?”

“Do I still play piano?”

“Anything.”

“Badminton.”

“That is not usually described as playing an instrument.”

“You said anything.”

“I regret giving you interpretive freedom.”

Another pause.

Then Lena said, “Why were you at a conference?”

“Because my boss asked.”

“That’s depressing.”

“I presented a paper.”

“Less depressing.”

“My ex-wife wrote half of it.”

“More complicated.”

“Very.”

Lena nodded solemnly.

“My dumplings were made by a woman who hasn’t spoken to my father since February.”

“So both of us are carrying collaborative work affected by relationship failure.”

“Exactly.”

“That could be a conference panel.”

“I’d attend.”

At 9:49, the lights came fully back on.

Several passengers applauded.

The train lurched forward.

Lena looked out at the black tunnel sliding past.

“There.”

“There.”

They reached the next station two minutes later.

The doors opened.

People poured onto the platform.

Tomas stepped out, then noticed Lena was walking in the opposite direction.

“Well,” he said.

“Well.”

“Thanks for the dumpling.”

“Thanks for assisting with the dead man.”

“Sleeping man.”

“We got lucky.”

He laughed.

The crowd moved between them.

Lena raised a hand.

“Goodbye, Tomas Reiter, Urban Systems Lab.”

“Goodbye, Lena No Evidence.”

She turned away.

Tomas took three steps.

Then he stopped.

“Lena.”

She looked back.

He had absolutely no idea what he intended to say.

Apparently she saw this.

“What?”

“The imaginary stations,” he said. “They’re actually quite good.”

She smiled.

“I’m sure they are.”

Then the crowd closed again, and this time they both kept walking.
