1. The Mother and Her Adult Son — The Spare Key



When Daniel came into the kitchen, his mother was standing on a chair with a screwdriver between her teeth.

“What are you doing?”

She removed the screwdriver. “What does it look like?”

“It looks like you’re trying to die in an embarrassing way.”

“I’m taking down the clock.”

Daniel put the grocery bags on the table. “Why?”

“Because it’s ugly.”

“It’s been ugly since 1998.”

“Yes, and I’ve finally had enough.”

He reached up and steadied the chair. At sixty-eight, Marianne was still small and quick, but lately Daniel had noticed that she used furniture for balance when she thought nobody was looking.

“Get down,” he said.

“Don’t order me around in my own kitchen.”

“I’m not ordering. I’m preventing.”

“Preventing what?”

“A hip replacement.”

She gave him the screwdriver. “Fine. You do it.”

Daniel climbed onto the chair.

The clock was shaped like a sunflower, its yellow plastic petals browned by years of cooking smoke.

“You bought this,” Marianne said.

“I was nine.”

“You chose it.”

“I was nine.”

“You insisted.”

“I also insisted I could breathe underwater.”

“You were a difficult child.”

“You told everybody I was gifted.”

“I was trying to make it sound deliberate.”

The clock came away from the wall with a dry scrape. Behind it was a pale circle of wallpaper, untouched by twenty-eight years of sunlight.

Daniel looked at it.

“God,” he said.

“What?”

“Nothing. It’s just strange.”

“What is?”

“That patch. Like the wall remembers.”

Marianne took the clock from him and set it facedown beside the sink.

“Walls don’t remember.”

“You do.”

“Unfortunately.”

Daniel began unpacking groceries. Milk. Bread. Tomatoes. Her favorite blackcurrant jam.

“You bought too much,” she said.

“You always say that.”

“Because you always buy too much.”

“You eat all of it.”

“Eventually. That isn’t the point.”

He put the milk in the refrigerator and found three unopened cartons already there.

“Mother.”

“What?”

“There’s enough milk here to bathe in.”

“I forgot I had some.”

He closed the refrigerator.

She saw his face.

“Don’t.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You were about to.”

“I was about to ask when you bought them.”

“No, you weren’t.”

“When did you?”

Marianne began wiping the clean counter with a clean cloth.

“Different days.”

“All three?”

“Yes.”

“Do you know which days?”

She stopped wiping.

There was a silence long enough for the refrigerator motor to switch itself off.

“I’m not senile, Daniel.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You came here with groceries I didn’t ask for and you count my milk.”

“I come every Sunday.”

“You used to come because you wanted to.”

“I still do.”

“No. Now you come like a man checking a boiler.”

He flinched.

Marianne folded the cloth precisely in half.

Daniel said, “I worry.”

“I know.”

“That’s not a crime.”

“It can become a hobby.”

He leaned against the counter.

“Claire wants you to stay with us for a while.”

“Claire wants?”

“We both do.”

“You said Claire first.”

“Because she brought it up.”

“Of course she did.”

“Don’t make her the villain.”

“I like Claire.”

“You criticize everything she does.”

“I criticize everybody I like.”

“Yes, I’m familiar with the privilege.”

Marianne almost smiled.

Daniel reached into his pocket.

“I had another key made.”

Her eyes dropped to his hand.

A small silver key lay across his palm.

“To here?”

“Yes.”

“You have a key.”

“The old one sticks.”

“It doesn’t stick if you lift the handle.”

“Exactly.”

She looked from the key to him.

“You went and copied my key without telling me?”

“For emergencies.”

“What emergency?”

“If you fall.”

“I haven’t fallen.”

“If you don’t answer.”

“I sometimes don’t want to answer.”

“If something happens.”

“Something is always happening.”

Daniel closed his fingers around the key.

“All right.”

He put it back in his pocket.

Marianne watched him.

“You’re angry.”

“No.”

“Your ears go red.”

“They do not.”

“They have since you were four.”

He began rearranging the tomatoes.

“Stop that,” she said.

“What?”

“Pretending the tomatoes need you.”

He laughed once, unwillingly.

Then Marianne sighed.

“Put the key on the hook.”

Daniel looked at her.

“You’re sure?”

“No. But put it there.”

He hung it beside the back door.

Marianne picked up the sunflower clock.

“What should we put there instead?”

“Nothing.”

“That pale circle will show.”

“Paint over it.”

“With what?”

Daniel glanced at the wall.

“Something new.”

She nodded, though not quite in agreement.

Then she turned the clock over and ran her thumb across its cracked yellow face.

“You really loved this horrible thing.”

“I know.”

“You cried when your father said we should return it.”

“I don’t remember that.”

“I do,” Marianne said.

And for once, neither of them argued about whose memory mattered more.

2. Two Old Diplomats — The Last Reception



Sir Adrian Bell found Ambassador Kovács behind a potted palm, stealing canapés into a folded napkin.

“I see Hungary’s foreign policy remains fundamentally acquisitive.”

Kovács glanced down at the miniature salmon tarts in his hand.

“At our age, Adrian, one plans for the journey home.”

“There are waiters.”

“Waiters disappear when speeches begin. Empires have collapsed from less foreseeable mistakes.”

The reception glittered around them: chandeliers, polished shoes, glasses carried shoulder-high through the crowd. A young minister was laughing too loudly near the French windows.

Adrian accepted a champagne flute from a passing tray.

“You don’t drink champagne,” Kovács said.

“I hold champagne. It gives people confidence.”

“In you?”

“In themselves.”

“Ah. The essence of British diplomacy.”

“And yours?”

“Making people uncertain whether they have been insulted.”

Kovács tucked another canapé into his napkin.

They had first met in Vienna in 1977, when both had been junior attachés and both had possessed dark hair, narrow waists, and governments that believed in filing everything in triplicate.

Now Adrian was eighty-two. Kovács claimed to be eighty, though Adrian privately suspected this was a diplomatic age.

“Do you know anyone here?” Adrian asked.

“Everyone under forty looks like an intern.”

“Some of them are ministers.”

“That proves nothing.”

Across the room, a photographer lifted his camera.

Both old men automatically straightened.

The flash went off.

Then both relaxed again.

Kovács said, “Did you hear about Mikhail?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“February.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“I assumed you knew.”

“Everybody always assumes the retired know things. We know nothing. Nobody sends us cables.”

“Nobody sends cables at all.”

“Exactly. Civilization ended quietly.”

Adrian smiled.

Mikhail Petrov had once kept them awake for eleven consecutive hours during arms negotiations in Geneva, refusing to concede the difference between “shall” and “should.”

“He was impossible,” Adrian said.

“He was magnificent.”

“He cheated at chess.”

“That too was magnificent.”

They watched the young minister mount the little stage at the end of the room.

Someone tapped a microphone.

Kovács grimaced. “Speech.”

“We could escape.”

“No. My driver isn’t until ten.”

“I thought you planned for the journey home.”

“Food, Adrian. Not transport. I am not a Bedouin.”

The minister began thanking distinguished guests.

Adrian lowered his voice.

“Do you ever think we were wrong?”

Kovács turned.

“About?”

“Any of it.”

“That is not a diplomat’s question.”

“We’re not diplomats anymore.”

“That is precisely why one must maintain standards.”

The room applauded something neither had heard.

Adrian looked into his untouched champagne.

“There was a night in Prague,” he said. “Eighty-four. You remember?”

“No.”

“You do.”

“Then why ask?”

“You told me your government would never accept the inspection clause.”

“They wouldn’t.”

“You told me privately to propose it anyway.”

Kovács’s expression did not change.

“We eventually accepted.”

“Because you knew you could persuade them.”

“Perhaps.”

“You also told them it was my idea.”

“Naturally.”

“So you could oppose it publicly.”

“Naturally.”

“And then accept it as a concession.”

“You understand diplomacy after all.”

Adrian laughed.

“But were we doing good,” he said, “or merely preventing bad things?”

“My dear Adrian, at three in the morning those are the same profession.”

The applause came again.

Kovács studied him.

“You are thinking about your memoir.”

“I’m not writing a memoir.”

“Then you are thinking about not writing one.”

Adrian said nothing.

Kovács took one of the stolen salmon tarts and handed it to him.

“Here.”

“What’s this?”

“Your share of history.”

“It looks stale.”

“Most history is.”

Adrian ate it.

After a moment he said, “Mikhail hated salmon.”

“No, Mikhail was allergic.”

“Was he?”

“Yes. Geneva, 1981. His face swelled during dinner.”

“I thought he was furious.”

“He was furious. He was also swelling.”

They began to laugh—quietly at first, then helplessly, two old men shaking behind a palm while the minister spoke about a future full of unprecedented cooperation.

A young aide turned to stare at them.

Kovács wiped his eyes.

“Behave,” Adrian whispered.

“You started it.”

“We’re representing our countries.”

“No,” Kovács said, recovering himself. “That is the delightful part.”

He lifted two more canapés from a passing tray.

“Now we represent only ourselves.”

3. Two Schoolgirls — The Animal in the Boiler Room



“There is definitely something alive in there.”

“There’s nothing alive in there.”

“Then explain the breathing.”

“That’s the boiler.”

“Boilers don’t breathe.”

“How do you know?”

Nadia looked at Priya.

“Because they’re boilers.”

“That’s not science.”

“It’s enough science for me.”

They stood outside the basement door during lunch break, still wearing their navy school blazers. A sign above the handle read STAFF ONLY. Someone had added, in pencil, AND GHOSTS.

Priya held the stolen—or, as she insisted, temporarily borrowed—torch from the chemistry lab.

“Open it,” she said.

“You open it.”

“You found the door.”

“That doesn’t make me responsible for what’s behind it.”

“You said you heard scratching.”

“I did.”

“You said it might be a trapped cat.”

“It might.”

“So open the door.”

Nadia folded her arms.

“You’re the one who wants to be a vet.”

“I’m fourteen.”

“You’ve been telling everybody since Year Three.”

“That doesn’t make me professionally qualified for basement cats.”

The scratching came again.

Both girls froze.

Priya whispered, “That was not a boiler.”

“No.”

“Could still be pipes.”

“Pipes with claws?”

Priya grabbed Nadia’s sleeve.

“Maybe we should get Mr. Clarke.”

“And tell him what? ‘Hello, sir, we were wandering in a forbidden corridor during lunch because Nadia heard demonic scratching’?”

“We don’t have to say demonic.”

“You said demonic first.”

“I was joking.”

“You screamed.”

“I was demonstrating.”

The sound came again—scrape, scrape, thump.

Nadia reached for the handle.

“Wait,” Priya said.

“What?”

“If it jumps at us, shut the door.”

“If it’s a cat?”

“If it’s a cat, don’t.”

“And if it’s not?”

“Use judgment.”

“Excellent protocol.”

Nadia turned the handle.

The door opened three inches and stopped.

Something heavy was blocking it.

Priya switched on the torch.

They squeezed through sideways.

The basement smelled of dust, warm metal, and wet concrete. Pipes ran along the ceiling like enormous grey veins.

“Hello?” Nadia called.

Priya hissed, “Why are you saying hello?”

“In case someone’s here.”

“If someone’s here, that’s worse.”

“Hello?” Nadia called again.

Scrape.

They swung the torch toward the far wall.

Two bright eyes reflected back.

Priya made a noise like a kettle.

“Oh my God.”

“What is it?”

“I don’t know.”

“You’re holding the light!”

“I’m shaking!”

“Stop shaking!”

“I’m not doing it recreationally!”

The creature moved.

Nadia grabbed Priya’s arm.

The beam steadied.

A fox stood wedged between a stack of old chairs and a radiator.

It was smaller than Nadia expected. Thin, reddish, with one foreleg caught through the broken wooden slats of a chair.

“Oh,” Priya breathed.

The fox pulled back. The chair scraped across the concrete.

“That’s the scratching,” Nadia said.

“I know.”

“Can we get it out?”

“Don’t touch it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were absolutely going to.”

The fox bared its teeth.

They stepped backward together.

“Mr. Clarke,” Priya whispered.

“Yes.”

“Definitely Mr. Clarke.”

They had almost reached the door when Nadia stopped.

“What?”

“Look.”

On the floor beside the radiator lay a scattering of crushed biscuits.

Priya stared.

“Someone’s feeding it.”

“Who?”

There was a click behind them.

The basement light came on.

Both girls spun around.

Mrs. Fenwick, the school librarian, stood in the doorway holding a plastic container.

She looked at the girls.

The girls looked at her.

Then all three looked at the fox.

Mrs. Fenwick closed her eyes.

“Please,” she said, “tell me you did not touch him.”

Priya said, “His name is him?”

“His name is Bernard.”

Nadia’s mouth fell open.

“You named the basement fox Bernard?”

“I did not name him. He has a personality that is unmistakably Bernard.”

“But he’s trapped.”

“I can see that.”

“You’ve been feeding him?”

“For three days.”

Priya stared at the librarian with something close to reverence.

“Mrs. Fenwick.”

“Yes?”

“You are much more interesting than I thought.”

Mrs. Fenwick sighed.

“That is not a compliment you should ever give an adult.”

She handed Priya the container.

“Hold these sausages.”

Then she pointed at Nadia.

“You. Go and fetch Mr. Clarke.”

“So we’re not in trouble?”

“You are in enormous trouble.”

Nadia nodded.

“After the fox?”

“Obviously after the fox.”

Nadia grinned and ran.

Behind her, Priya said, “Why Bernard?”

And Mrs. Fenwick replied, “Look at him.”

4. A Married Couple After an Affair — Inventory



“There are six wineglasses.”

Martin stood in the kitchen doorway.

Elena did not turn around. “Yes.”

“We had eight.”

“One broke at Christmas.”

“That makes seven.”

She put another plate into the cardboard box.

“Then one broke some other time.”

“When?”

“I don’t know, Martin.”

“Was he here?”

Her hands stopped.

The question stayed between them.

Martin said, “I’m asking.”

“I know.”

“Was he?”

Elena turned.

“No.”

He studied her face as if this were still something he could do reliably.

“No?”

“No.”

“All right.”

She resumed packing.

They had agreed that Elena would take the books in the sitting room, half the kitchen things, the green armchair, and the smaller television. Martin would keep the apartment until the lease ended in November.

They had made the agreement two weeks after she told him about James.

“Take four glasses,” Martin said.

“What?”

“Four. Half.”

“I don’t need four.”

“You entertain.”

She looked at him sharply.

“That’s cheap.”

“I know.”

“Do you want me to apologize again?”

“No.”

“Because I can.”

“I know.”

“I will if you want.”

“I don’t.”

“Then stop making me do it without words.”

Martin leaned against the doorframe.

“I don’t know how to behave.”

“Neither do I.”

“You seem to.”

“I’m putting plates in a box.”

“You’ve always been good at practical things.”

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

“Nothing.”

“No, say it.”

He rubbed his face.

“I mean you found an apartment in three days. You arranged the movers. You changed the electricity account. You made a spreadsheet.”

“The spreadsheet is upsetting you?”

“The spreadsheet is magnificent.”

She gave an exhausted laugh.

“That’s ridiculous.”

“It has color coding.”

“I like color coding.”

“I know.”

Again: I know.

Eleven years of marriage compressed into those two words.

Elena sat down at the kitchen table.

“Are you going to ask me anything else?”

“I’ve asked everything.”

“No, you’ve asked dates. Hotels. How many times.”

“Those seemed relevant.”

“They were.”

“I don’t want the emotional details.”

“Fine.”

Martin pulled out the chair opposite her but didn’t sit.

Then he did.

“Did you love him?”

She looked at him.

He laughed faintly.

“Apparently I did have one left.”

“I thought I did.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

“Did you?”

“At the time? Yes.”

Martin nodded.

“And now?”

“No.”

“That’s convenient.”

“Yes.”

He looked toward the window.

A bus groaned to a stop in the street below.

Elena said, “You can be cruel if you need to.”

“I don’t want to be cruel.”

“I might deserve it.”

“That’s the problem.”

“What is?”

“I don’t want our marriage to become a courtroom.”

She stared down at her wedding ring.

She still wore it. Martin had noticed that on the first day and then every day afterward.

He said, “Why are you wearing that?”

Her thumb touched the ring.

“I don’t know.”

“You’re moving out tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“Take it off.”

She looked up.

He immediately regretted saying it.

“All right,” she said.

“Elena—”

“No. You’re right.”

She twisted the ring.

It resisted at the knuckle.

She pulled harder.

“Stop.”

“It’s stuck.”

“Use soap.”

“That feels metaphorical.”

Despite himself, Martin smiled.

She saw it and began laughing. It came out wrong—half laugh, half sob.

“Jesus,” she said.

Martin stood.

Elena went to the sink, ran cold water, rubbed dish soap around her finger.

“Here,” he said.

“I can do it.”

“I know.”

“Then let me.”

He stepped back.

She worked the ring free.

For a moment it lay in her wet palm, shining beneath the kitchen light.

“Where should I put it?” she asked.

Martin couldn’t answer.

On the counter sat two boxes. One marked ELENA. One marked MARTIN.

She looked at them too.

“Christ,” she whispered.

“Put it on the table.”

She did.

Neither of them touched it.

After a while Martin said, “Take the wineglasses.”

“All of them?”

“All six.”

“Why?”

“I hate them.”

“You chose them.”

“I made a lot of bad decisions.”

She winced.

He closed his eyes.

“Sorry.”

“No,” Elena said quietly. “That one you can have.”

He sat down again.

After another silence, she pushed the empty cardboard box aside.

“Do you want coffee?”

Martin looked at the clock.

It was nearly midnight.

“Yes,” he said.

She stood and filled the kettle.

For the next ten minutes, neither of them packed anything.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate — The Missing Minute



“Sit down, Lieutenant.”

Lieutenant Vale remained standing.

Captain Arendt looked up from the report.

“That was not a ceremonial invitation.”

Vale sat.

The office was too warm. Rain rattled against the narrow window, and somewhere beyond the walls of headquarters a telephone rang three times and stopped.

Arendt tapped the report.

“You filed this at 0400.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You wrote that your patrol reached Checkpoint Seven at 0216.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And departed at 0229.”

“Yes, sir.”

Arendt slid another sheet across the desk.

“This is the checkpoint log.”

Vale did not touch it.

“It says you arrived at 0215.”

“Then my watch was slow.”

“One minute?”

“Apparently.”

“And yet every other time in your report corresponds exactly with the radio record.”

Vale’s jaw tightened.

Arendt leaned back.

“Would you like another explanation?”

“No, sir.”

“Good. I would.”

Rain ticked against the glass.

Vale said, “It was one minute.”

“So you agree the report is false.”

“I agree the time is wrong.”

“Deliberately?”

Vale said nothing.

Arendt stood and walked to the window.

“You know what I dislike about small lies, Lieutenant?”

“No, sir.”

“They make one search for large ones.”

“It wasn’t—”

“Careful.”

Vale stopped.

Arendt turned.

“What happened at Checkpoint Seven?”

“Nothing.”

“Then why change the arrival time?”

Vale looked at the floor.

“Lieutenant.”

“There was a civilian.”

Arendt’s face remained still.

“What civilian?”

“A boy.”

“How old?”

“Sixteen, maybe seventeen.”

“At two fifteen in the morning?”

“Yes.”

“Doing what?”

“Running.”

“From whom?”

“Us.”

Arendt waited.

Vale swallowed.

“Corporal Hesse raised his rifle.”

“Did he fire?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I ordered him not to.”

“Why?”

“The boy wasn’t armed.”

“That was not your determination to make.”

“It was exactly my determination to make.”

Arendt’s eyes hardened.

Vale corrected himself.

“Sir.”

“Continue.”

“We stopped him. He had medicine.”

“What kind?”

“Insulin.”

“For whom?”

“His father. He said.”

“And you believed him.”

“Yes.”

“Did you verify it?”

“No.”

“Search him?”

“Yes.”

“Detain him?”

“No.”

“You let him pass.”

“Yes.”

Arendt returned to the desk.

“And the missing minute?”

Vale looked at the checkpoint log.

“Hesse logged us before I told him to change it.”

“Change what?”

“The official arrival.”

“Why?”

“Because the boy appeared between 0215 and 0216.”

Arendt’s expression altered by almost nothing.

Vale continued.

“If the report says 0216, then officially we weren’t there yet.”

“I understand arithmetic.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Do you understand that you have falsified an operational record?”

“Yes.”

“Do you understand that Hesse can testify to this?”

“Yes.”

“Will he?”

Vale hesitated.

“Yes.”

“Interesting confidence.”

“He thinks I should be disciplined.”

“And do you?”

Vale met the captain’s eyes.

“No.”

Arendt almost smiled, but there was no humor in it.

“At least we have reached honesty.”

He sat again.

“Was the boy telling the truth?”

“I don’t know.”

“That should trouble you.”

“It does.”

“But you would do it again.”

Vale’s silence answered.

Arendt opened a drawer and removed a thin file.

“You know why Checkpoint Seven exists?”

“To control passage into the restricted district.”

“That is the instruction. Do you know why it exists?”

Vale frowned.

Arendt pushed the file across.

Inside was a photograph of a bombed transport truck.

“Three months ago,” Arendt said. “A seventeen-year-old walked through a checkpoint carrying antibiotics. Beneath them were detonators.”

Vale stared at the photograph.

“One guard died. Four soldiers lost limbs.”

“I didn’t know.”

“No. You didn’t.”

Vale’s face drained.

Arendt let him look for several seconds.

Then he took the photograph back.

“The boy tonight,” Vale said. “Was there an incident?”

“Not that we know of.”

Vale breathed out.

Arendt closed the file.

“You made a decision based on incomplete information.”

“Yes, sir.”

“So do I, every day.”

Vale looked at him.

The captain picked up the report.

“I am ordering you to submit a corrected version.”

Vale’s shoulders stiffened.

“Yes, sir.”

“You will include the civilian.”

“Yes.”

“You will include your order to Corporal Hesse.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And you will include the correct time.”

“0215.”

“Precisely.”

Vale rose.

At the door, Arendt said, “Lieutenant.”

Vale turned.

“If you ever alter a record again, I will remove you from command.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And Vale?”

“Sir?”

Arendt looked down at the false report.

“You may have saved a sick man tonight.”

Vale waited.

“Or,” Arendt said, “you may have made a terrible mistake.”

“Yes, sir.”

“That uncertainty is part of the job. Lying about it is not.”

Vale nodded once.

Then he left.

Arendt remained at the desk, listening to the rain and looking at the missing minute.

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other — Floor Eleven



The elevator stopped between the tenth and eleventh floors.

Mara looked at the man beside her.

He looked at the ceiling.

Neither spoke.

The elevator hummed once, reconsidered its existence, and went silent.

Mara pressed eleven.

Nothing.

She pressed the door-open button.

Nothing.

The man said, “I don’t think pressing them harder improves their morale.”

“I wasn’t pressing harder.”

“You were a little.”

She looked at him.

He was vaguely familiar: forties, dark coat, wire-framed glasses. She had seen him in the building lobby, probably. Perhaps he worked on twelve.

“You have a better idea?”

“No.”

“Great.”

“I wanted to establish that early.”

Mara pressed the alarm button.

A bell rang somewhere far away.

“Comforting,” the man said.

“You’re very calm.”

“I’m not.”

“You look calm.”

“I have a face people misunderstand.”

The intercom crackled.

A voice said, “Hello?”

Mara leaned toward it. “We’re stuck.”

“Which elevator?”

She looked at the man.

He looked at her.

“The left one?” she said.

“Left from where?” the voice asked.

“The lobby.”

“All right. We’re calling maintenance.”

“How long?”

Static.

“Hello?”

Nothing.

The man took out his phone.

“No signal,” he said.

Mara checked hers.

“Same.”

They stood in silence.

Then he said, “I’m Elias.”

“Mara.”

“I think we’ve met.”

“I don’t think so.”

“You work for Halpern?”

“No.”

“Then we haven’t.”

“Compelling deduction.”

“Thank you.”

She laughed despite herself.

He loosened his scarf.

The elevator was warm.

“How long have you worked here?” he asked.

“Seven months.”

“Ah.”

“What does ‘ah’ mean?”

“Nothing. You still look at people in the elevator.”

“And after seven months?”

“You learn to stare at the numbers.”

“We don’t currently have numbers.”

“Which explains the conversation.”

Mara leaned against the mirrored wall.

“What floor are you?”

“Eleven.”

“Me too.”

He frowned.

“What company?”

“Weller & Finch.”

“You’re joking.”

“No.”

“I’m Weller & Finch.”

Now she frowned.

“Which department?”

“Accounts.”

“Of course.”

“What does that mean?”

“You look like accounts.”

“That is devastating.”

“I meant—”

“No, don’t repair it. I want to understand the damage.”

She smiled.

“I’m legal.”

“You look like legal.”

“Now you’re just retaliating.”

“Yes.”

The elevator gave a sudden jerk.

Mara grabbed the rail.

Elias grabbed her elbow.

Then everything stopped again.

“Sorry,” he said, releasing her.

“No, that was—fine.”

“Excellent. We’ve achieved physical contact before learning surnames.”

“Still time to preserve some mystery.”

He exhaled, but his face had changed.

“You really are nervous,” Mara said.

“I told you.”

“Claustrophobic?”

“A little.”

She straightened.

“How little?”

“Currently? Less little.”

“Do you want to sit down?”

“No.”

“That was very fast.”

“If I sit down, I’ll feel trapped.”

“You are trapped.”

“Yes, Mara, that information has reached me.”

“Sorry.”

He closed his eyes.

She watched him take a slow breath.

“Talk to me,” he said.

“About what?”

“Anything.”

She thought.

“I stole a duck once.”

His eyes opened.

“What?”

“You said anything.”

“You stole a duck.”

“I was six.”

“That is crucial context you deliberately withheld.”

“It lived in a park near my grandmother’s house. I decided it was lonely.”

“And?”

“I put it in my backpack.”

“A live duck?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“With difficulty.”

Elias began laughing.

“It was louder than I expected.”

“I’m astonished.”

“My grandmother discovered it in her kitchen.”

“What did she do?”

“Made me return it.”

“Sensible.”

“She also made me apologize.”

“To whom?”

“The duck.”

Elias laughed harder now.

Mara said, “Your turn.”

“I have never stolen waterfowl.”

“Something else.”

He considered.

“I’m resigning today.”

She blinked.

“From Weller & Finch?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I hate it.”

“That’s admirably concise.”

“I have hated it for four years.”

“And today?”

“My resignation letter is in my coat.”

He patted the inside pocket.

“I was going to give it to Kessler at three.”

Mara checked her dead phone instinctively.

“What time is it?”

“Two fifty-three when we stopped.”

“Oh.”

“Yes.”

“You could miss your dramatic moment.”

“I know.”

“Are you relieved?”

He looked at her.

“A little.”

The elevator suddenly shuddered and began moving.

Both went quiet.

The number 11 lit up.

The doors opened.

Beyond them stood three maintenance workers and a worried receptionist.

Mara stepped out first.

Elias followed.

The ordinary corridor seemed absurdly spacious.

“Well,” Mara said.

“Well.”

They stood there, strangers again.

Then she pointed to his coat.

“Seven minutes late.”

Elias touched the resignation letter.

“Apparently.”

“You still doing it?”

He looked toward the glass doors of Weller & Finch.

“Yes.”

Mara nodded.

“Good luck.”

“Thank you.”

She started toward legal.

“Mara?”

She turned.

Elias said, “If this goes badly, I’m stealing a duck.”

She smiled.

“Start small. Office supplies.”

Then they walked through the same doors in opposite directions.
