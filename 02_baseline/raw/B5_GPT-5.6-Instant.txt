1. A Mother and Her Adult Son

The freezer door would not close.

Nina stood with one hand on the handle and the other braced against the refrigerator, leaning her weight into it.

“Don’t,” Pavel said from the doorway.

“I’m not doing anything.”

“You’re trying to force it.”

“I’m closing a door.”

“You’re seventy-three. You’re wrestling an appliance.”

Nina released the handle. The freezer door sprang open by three inches.

“There. You see? It’s spoiled.”

“It isn’t spoiled.”

“You bought me a spoiled refrigerator.”

“I bought it eight years ago.”

“Then it lasted eight years. That’s respectable.”

Pavel took off his coat and laid it over a chair. He had come straight from work, still wearing the dark trousers and blue shirt that made him look, Nina thought, like somebody waiting to give evidence in court.

He crouched beside the freezer.

“What’s in here?”

“Food.”

“Yes, I had gathered that.”

“Don’t throw anything away.”

“I haven’t touched anything.”

“You’re thinking about it.”

Pavel pulled out the top drawer. It stopped halfway.

Behind it, something wrapped in newspaper had fallen against the back wall.

“What’s this?”

“Nothing.”

“It’s preventing the drawer from closing.”

“Then move it.”

He reached in and extracted a rectangular package, hard with frost. The newspaper had fused to the plastic underneath.

“Meat?”

“Probably.”

“What kind?”

Nina peered at it.

“How should I know?”

“You put it there.”

“I’ve put many things in many places.”

Pavel turned the package over. A date was written on the newspaper in blue pen.

“February 2019.”

“So?”

“So this meat has survived two governments.”

“Put it back.”

“Mama.”

“It’s frozen.”

“That doesn’t make it immortal.”

She held out her hand.

Pavel did not give it to her.

“What is it?”

“I said I don’t know.”

“You do.”

Nina’s hand remained extended.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Pavel looked again at the writing. Under the date, almost erased by damp, were two letters.

V.K.

His expression changed.

“Oh.”

“Give it here.”

“This was Dad’s?”

“He bought it.”

“Seven years ago?”

“He bought too much lamb for Easter.”

Pavel looked at the package, then at his mother.

“You kept Dad’s lamb?”

“I kept lamb.”

“You wrote his initials on it.”

“He wrote them. He labeled everything. You remember.”

Pavel did remember. Viktor had labeled extension cords, jars of screws, batteries, boxes of photographs. Once, inexplicably, a hammer.

Nina took the package from him.

“He said we’d make it the next Sunday,” she said.

Pavel sat back on his heels.

“Dad died that Wednesday.”

“Yes.”

“You’ve kept it since then.”

“I forgot it.”

“No, you didn’t.”

She looked sharply at him.

“You come here once a week and suddenly you know everything?”

“I know you haven’t forgotten a package you’ve moved through two freezer defrostings.”

“I didn’t ask you to investigate me.”

“I’m fixing the door.”

“Then fix the door.”

Pavel stood.

Nina placed the frozen lamb on the counter.

“You want tea?”

“No.”

“You always want tea.”

“I don’t always want tea.”

“You do in this house.”

He laughed despite himself.

Nina filled the kettle.

Behind her, Pavel picked up the package again.

“Can I throw it away?”

The tap ran.

“Not while I’m looking.”

“All right.”

“Put it in the bin after you leave.”

He was quiet.

“That’s still you throwing it away.”

“No. Then it’s already gone.”

Pavel set it beside the sink.

The kettle clicked on.

After a while Nina said, “There’s cake.”

“How old?”

“Yesterday.”

“Whose initials are on it?”

She turned and gave him a look.

Pavel smiled.

The freezer door, emptied of its obstruction, closed with a soft magnetic thump.

Neither of them looked at it.

2. Two Old Diplomats

Sir Adrian Vale found Ambassador Sokolov stealing sugar.

“You still do that,” Adrian said.

Sokolov looked up from the silver bowl.

“Do what?”

“Put three lumps in your coffee and tell everyone you take two.”

“I have never told anyone I take two.”

“You told the Finnish foreign minister in Geneva.”

“That was negotiation.”

They were alone in a reception room overlooking the rain-dark garden. Beyond the closed doors, a string quartet was playing something both men had heard too many times at too many official dinners.

Adrian lowered himself into the chair opposite.

“You’re supposed to be dead.”

“Retired.”

“Same thing in our profession.”

“Your newspapers reported me dead twice.”

“Were they wrong?”

“On both occasions.”

“Careless of them.”

Sokolov stirred his coffee.

At eighty-one, he had lost weight but not the habit of examining people as though they had arrived carrying confidential instructions.

“You limp,” he said.

“You repeat yourself.”

“I asked about the limp.”

“You didn’t ask. You observed.”

“Then explain it.”

“No.”

Sokolov nodded.

“Hip.”

Adrian poured whisky.

“Prostate.”

Sokolov stared.

“What?”

“You see? We can both diagnose.”

For the first time Sokolov smiled.

The quartet stopped. Applause came faintly through the wall.

“Do you remember Belgrade?” Sokolov asked.

“I remember several Belgrades.”

“Ninety-six.”

“I was there.”

“You lied to me.”

“Constantly.”

“About the bridge.”

Adrian’s hand stopped over his glass.

“That bridge.”

“Yes.”

Rain tapped the tall windows.

“I thought we agreed never to discuss it.”

“We agreed when we were employed.”

“And now?”

“Now nobody employs us.”

Adrian sat down.

Sokolov removed something from his inside pocket: an envelope, yellowed at the edges.

“You kept documents?”

“One.”

“You could have been prosecuted.”

“I could still be prosecuted.”

“That must be comforting.”

Sokolov slid the envelope across the table.

Adrian did not touch it.

“What is it?”

“You know.”

“No.”

“You sent me a warning at eleven forty that night.”

“I sent you nothing.”

“Precisely.”

Adrian looked at him.

Sokolov tapped the envelope.

“The message came through an intermediary. No signature. It said the eastern approach would be closed at midnight.”

“And?”

“I moved my people.”

“I remember.”

“Sixteen minutes later the bridge was hit.”

Adrian drank.

“You always assumed it was me.”

“I knew it was you.”

“Then why bring it up now?”

“Because yesterday I met the intermediary.”

Adrian’s eyes narrowed.

“He’s alive?”

“She.”

That surprised him.

Sokolov enjoyed the surprise for approximately two seconds.

“She told me you didn’t send the warning.”

“No.”

“She did.”

Adrian set down his glass.

“Why?”

“She had seen the targeting assessment.”

“And decided to warn you?”

“No. She decided to warn me because she thought I would believe the warning if I thought it came from you.”

Adrian looked toward the windows.

“That was intelligent.”

“She was twenty-four.”

“That is irritating.”

Sokolov laughed softly.

For a while they listened to chairs being moved in the next room.

“So,” Adrian said, “you spent thirty years believing I saved your people.”

“Yes.”

“And now you discover I didn’t.”

“Yes.”

“Are you disappointed?”

Sokolov considered this.

“Relieved.”

“Relieved?”

“I owed you something.”

“You never behaved as if you did.”

“I was a diplomat.”

Adrian smiled.

Sokolov pushed the envelope toward him again.

“What am I supposed to do with this?”

“Burn it.”

“Why me?”

“I don’t smoke anymore.”

“There are matches.”

“My doctor says I should avoid temptation.”

Adrian picked up the envelope.

“You know, Mikhail, this may be the first genuinely honest conversation we’ve ever had.”

Sokolov dropped a third sugar lump into his coffee.

“Don’t exaggerate.”

3. Two Schoolgirls

The frog was in Salma’s blazer pocket.

“Stop moving,” she whispered.

“I’m not moving,” Beth whispered back.

“You’re making the bench move.”

“I’m breathing.”

“Do it less.”

They sat outside the headmistress’s office beneath a framed photograph of the school’s founder, a severe Victorian woman who seemed personally disappointed in both of them.

Something moved inside Salma’s pocket.

Beth stared.

“Oh God.”

“Don’t say God outside Mrs. Wetherby’s office.”

“You stole a frog.”

“I rescued it.”

“From biology.”

“Exactly.”

“That is where frogs are supposed to be.”

“Not that one.”

The office door remained closed.

Salma put one hand over her pocket.

Beth leaned closer.

“Is it wet?”

“It’s a frog.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“It’s slightly wet.”

Beth recoiled.

“You let me carry your blazer ten minutes ago.”

“He wasn’t in it then.”

“Where was he?”

“My bag.”

Beth shut her eyes.

“You had a frog in your bag during maths?”

“He likes maths.”

“You’re insane.”

“He was very calm.”

“He’s a frog.”

“Don’t stereotype.”

The door opened.

Both girls sat upright.

Mrs. Wetherby’s secretary emerged carrying a folder.

“She’ll see you in a moment.”

“Yes, Miss,” they said together.

The door closed.

Beth turned slowly to Salma.

“We have maybe one minute. Give it to me.”

“What?”

“The frog.”

“Why?”

“I’ll put him in the plant.”

“There’s no water.”

“It’s a plant. Plants have water.”

“That is not how ponds work.”

“Salma.”

“What?”

“Your pocket is moving.”

Salma looked down.

A green head appeared beneath the flap.

Both girls froze.

“No,” Salma whispered.

The frog climbed out.

Beth cupped both hands around it just as it jumped.

She caught nothing.

The frog landed on the carpet.

“Get him.”

“You get him.”

“He likes you.”

“He doesn’t know me.”

“That’s never stopped you making friends.”

The frog hopped beneath a chair.

The girls dropped to their knees.

“Come here,” Salma murmured.

“What are you doing?”

“Encouraging him.”

“He doesn’t speak English.”

“What language do frogs speak, Beth?”

“I don’t know. Frog.”

Salma crawled under the chair.

At that exact moment, the office door opened again.

Mrs. Wetherby stood there.

She looked at Beth kneeling on the carpet.

She looked at Salma’s shoes protruding from beneath the chair.

“Miss Khan.”

“Yes, Mrs. Wetherby?”

“Would you care to explain?”

There was a pause.

“No.”

“Would you like to come out?”

“Yes.”

Salma emerged, hair disordered, holding the frog in both hands.

Mrs. Wetherby looked at it.

The frog looked at Mrs. Wetherby.

Beth stood.

“It was my idea,” she said.

Salma turned.

“No, it wasn’t.”

“It was partly my idea.”

“You told me not to.”

“Afterward.”

“Before.”

“I could have tried harder.”

Mrs. Wetherby raised one hand.

“Why,” she asked, “is there a frog in my corridor?”

Salma swallowed.

“Mr. Price was going to dissect him.”

“No, he wasn’t,” Beth said quietly.

Salma glared at her.

Mrs. Wetherby’s eyebrows rose.

“He was going to demonstrate dissection,” Salma amended.

“On a plastic model,” Beth said.

Silence.

“You said it was real,” Salma hissed.

“I thought it was.”

“You saw the box.”

“It was extremely realistic.”

Mrs. Wetherby removed her glasses.

“So you stole a live frog because you believed Mr. Price intended to dissect it.”

“Yes.”

“And where did you obtain the live frog?”

Beth looked at Salma.

Salma looked at Beth.

Mrs. Wetherby waited.

Finally Beth said, “That’s actually the more complicated part.”

4. A Married Couple After an Affair

Martin was changing the sheets when Claire came home.

She stood in the bedroom doorway with her overnight bag still over her shoulder.

“What are you doing?”

“The sheets.”

“I can see that.”

Martin pulled the fitted corner free.

“You said you’d be back tomorrow.”

“I changed my mind.”

“All right.”

“Why are you changing them?”

“They needed changing.”

“Today?”

He stopped.

“Claire.”

“No. Answer me.”

“Because I slept in them.”

“So?”

“So I wanted clean sheets.”

She put down the bag.

“Was she here?”

“No.”

“You answered quickly.”

“Because the answer is no.”

Claire watched him strip the pillowcase.

Three weeks had passed since he had told her. Eleven days since she had stopped asking for details. Four days since she had gone to her sister’s.

“Did she ever come here?”

“No.”

“Ever?”

“No.”

“Did you want her to?”

Martin sat on the edge of the bed.

“That’s a different question.”

“I know.”

“You said you didn’t want details.”

“I don’t.”

“This is a detail.”

“No. Her hair colour is a detail. Whether you imagined her in our house is not a detail.”

He looked at the bare mattress.

“Yes.”

Claire nodded once.

“Where?”

“Don’t.”

“Where?”

“Here.”

She laughed, but there was no amusement in it.

“Of course.”

“Nothing happened here.”

“You keep saying that as though geography is morality.”

“I’m not.”

“You are. Not here. Not in our bed. Not on my birthday. Not with someone I know. You keep fencing it in.”

Martin stood.

“What do you want me to say?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s the problem.”

“My problem?”

“Our problem.”

“No. Our problem was that you slept with someone else. This is what came after.”

He flinched.

Claire noticed and hated that she was pleased.

She walked to the window.

Outside, a neighbour was trying to reverse into a space too small for his car.

“How long did you stand here imagining it?”

“Claire.”

“Five minutes? A year?”

“I don’t know.”

“You know everything except the things I ask.”

“I thought about leaving.”

She turned.

Martin’s face had gone pale.

“For her?”

“No.”

“For what, then?”

“For nobody. For a different life.”

Claire stared at him.

“That’s worse.”

“I know.”

“No, I don’t think you do.”

“I do.”

“If it was her, I could hate her. I could understand that. She’s younger, or funnier, or she listens to your stories about work—”

“She’s not younger.”

“Oh, thank God.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Stop saying that.”

He nodded.

She sat on the chair beside the wardrobe.

“Were you unhappy?”

“Yes.”

“With me?”

“Sometimes.”

“And you never said.”

“I did.”

“You complained about the kitchen. You complained that we never went anywhere. You complained about my mother.”

“Those were ways of saying it.”

“No. Those were ways of making me guess.”

Martin looked down at the sheet in his hands.

After a while Claire said, “I was unhappy too.”

He looked up.

She gave a small, exhausted shrug.

“I just didn’t have an affair.”

“No.”

“That sounded cruel.”

“It was accurate.”

The neighbour outside finally abandoned the parking space.

Claire rubbed her forehead.

“Put the sheets on.”

“What?”

“I’m tired.”

“You’re staying?”

“Tonight.”

Martin unfolded the clean fitted sheet.

Claire took the opposite corners.

For a moment they stood with the mattress between them.

“Which way?” he asked.

“You’ve been changing this bed for seventeen years.”

“I always get it wrong.”

“I know.”

She turned the sheet ninety degrees.

“Like this.”

Neither of them let go.

5. An Officer and a Subordinate

Lieutenant Arendt found Corporal Weiss sitting on the ammunition crate, eating a peach.

“Where did you get that?”

Weiss looked at the peach.

“This?”

“No, the ammunition crate.”

“Village.”

“The crate?”

“The peach.”

Arendt held out his hand.

Weiss hesitated, then gave him the peach.

Arendt examined it.

“One bite.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You stole it.”

“No, sir.”

“Bought it?”

“No.”

“Then unless fruit has begun enlisting voluntarily, you stole it.”

“The woman gave it to me.”

“What woman?”

“The old one by the well.”

“There are six old women by the well.”

“The one with the red scarf.”

Arendt gave the peach back.

“Finish it.”

Weiss blinked.

“Sir?”

“You’ve already contaminated the evidence.”

Weiss took another bite.

They were sheltering in the remains of a railway office. Half the roof was gone. Beyond the wall, the road descended toward the village and then disappeared into wooded hills.

Arendt checked his watch.

“Truck’s late.”

“Yes, sir.”

“How late?”

“Forty-three minutes.”

“You were counting?”

“There’s nothing else to do.”

“Apparently there are peaches.”

Weiss chewed.

After a moment he said, “Sir?”

“What?”

“The woman asked when we’re leaving.”

Arendt looked toward the road.

“What did you tell her?”

“Nothing.”

“Good.”

“She said the others always say nothing.”

“Who are the others?”

“Us, I think.”

Arendt leaned against the wall.

“You speak her dialect?”

“My grandmother did.”

“You never mentioned that.”

“You never asked.”

“That sentence will eventually get you killed.”

“Yes, sir.”

A distant engine sounded.

Both men turned.

It faded.

Not their truck.

Weiss scraped the peach stone clean with his teeth.

“She has a son,” he said.

“The woman?”

“Yes.”

“Most old women do.”

“He disappeared last month.”

Arendt said nothing.

“She asked if we took him.”

“Weiss.”

“I said I didn’t know.”

“You should have said nothing.”

“I did know, sir.”

Arendt looked at him.

Weiss placed the peach stone carefully beside him.

“What do you mean?”

“I saw him.”

“When?”

“The checkpoint at Keller Bridge. Three weeks ago.”

“With whom?”

“Military police.”

“You’re sure it was him?”

“She had a photograph.”

Arendt’s expression hardened.

“And you told her?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I told you.”

“That was unnecessary.”

Weiss stared at him.

“With respect, sir, I thought that was what officers were for.”

Arendt almost smiled.

“You have a romantic understanding of officers.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Lose it.”

They listened to the wind moving through the broken roof.

“What happened at Keller Bridge?” Weiss asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You could find out.”

“I could ask.”

“That’s what I meant.”

“And the question would be recorded.”

Weiss nodded.

“And?”

“And somebody would ask why I was asking.”

“And?”

Arendt looked at him.

“You really are determined to make me explain the army to you.”

“No, sir. I understand the army.”

“That’s worse.”

The engine came again, louder this time.

A truck appeared around the bend.

Weiss stood and brushed dust from his trousers.

Arendt picked up his pack.

“Red scarf?”

“Sir?”

“The woman.”

“Yes.”

Arendt watched the truck approach.

“What’s the son’s name?”

Weiss said nothing for a second.

Then: “Emil Novak.”

Arendt repeated it once under his breath.

Weiss picked up the peach stone.

“What are you doing with that?”

“Keeping it.”

“Why?”

Weiss put it in his pocket.

“I might plant it.”

Arendt stared at him.

“Corporal.”

“Yes, sir?”

“Try not to become symbolic. It’s exhausting.”

The truck stopped in front of them.

Weiss climbed aboard.

Arendt followed.

6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

Daniel knew the woman’s name was Ruth because it was written on her paper cup.

RUTH.

His own cup said DAVID.

“My name isn’t David,” he said.

Ruth looked over.

They were the only two people left in the airport café. Beyond the glass, snow was blowing sideways across the runway.

“Mine isn’t Ruth,” she said.

Daniel looked at her cup.

“What is it?”

“Ruth.”

He waited.

She smiled.

“Sorry. I wanted to see what you’d do.”

“What was I supposed to do?”

“I don’t know. Accuse me of something.”

“Why?”

“It’s been a long day.”

The departure board above them changed again.

CANCELLED.

A collective groan rose from somewhere down the terminal.

Daniel checked his phone.

“No trains,” he said.

“No hotels,” Ruth replied.

“You checked?”

“Every hotel within twenty kilometres.”

“That’s efficient.”

“I have a meeting at nine.”

“Not anymore.”

She looked at him.

“You don’t know that.”

“You’re in an airport at midnight during a snowstorm.”

“The meeting still exists.”

“You may not.”

“That’s unnecessarily dark.”

“Sorry.”

Ruth stirred her coffee.

They had spoken twice before this: once when Daniel had watched her suitcase while she went to the restroom, and once when she had told him the gate had changed.

That was, he thought, already more trust than most adult relationships began with.

“What’s the meeting?” he asked.

“Divorce lawyer.”

“Oh.”

“You?”

“Funeral.”

“Oh.”

They both looked at their coffee.

Ruth laughed first.

“This is terrible.”

“Very.”

“Sorry about your—”

“Uncle.”

“Were you close?”

“No.”

She nodded.

“Are you?”

“What?”

“Sorry about my divorce?”

“I don’t know enough yet.”

“That’s fair.”

Daniel took a drink.

“Are you getting divorced?”

“I think so.”

“You think?”

“My husband thinks so.”

“And you have a lawyer.”

“He made the appointment.”

“Your husband?”

“My brother.”

“That’s a lot of men organizing your divorce.”

“Yes.”

“Sorry.”

“No, that one was accurate.”

An airport employee began distributing thin grey blankets.

Ruth watched him.

“I could just go home.”

“Can you?”

“Yes.”

“Then why don’t you?”

She looked at Daniel as though the question were intrusive, which it was.

Finally she said, “Because if I go home, he’ll be there.”

“Your husband.”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“And he’ll make tea.”

Daniel waited.

“He always makes tea when something terrible happens. Proper tea. Warms the pot. Everything.”

“That sounds nice.”

“It is nice.”

“That’s a problem?”

“It’s a huge problem.”

Daniel nodded as if he understood.

He didn’t.

Ruth studied him.

“Why are you going to your uncle’s funeral if you weren’t close?”

“My mother asked me.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s generally enough.”

“Is she going?”

“She’s dead.”

Ruth’s face changed.

“Ah.”

“She hated her brother.”

“Your uncle.”

“Yes.”

“Then why did she want you at his funeral?”

Daniel rubbed the cardboard seam of his cup.

“She asked me ten years ago. She said, ‘When Leonard dies, make sure he’s dead.’”

Ruth stared at him.

Then she laughed so suddenly she spilled coffee onto her hand.

“Oh God. Sorry.”

“No, she would have liked that.”

“Was she joking?”

“I genuinely don’t know.”

The airport employee reached them and handed over two blankets.

Ruth unfolded hers.

“So you’re crossing the country to verify a death for a dead woman.”

“Yes.”

“And I’m crossing it to discuss ending a marriage I might go home and save.”

“Apparently.”

She draped the blanket around her shoulders.

“We’re not doing very well.”

“At what?”

“Being rational adults.”

Daniel considered this.

“I’ve never seen convincing evidence that anyone is.”

Ruth smiled.

The departure board flickered.

Their cancelled flight disappeared completely.

“David,” Ruth said.

“Yes, Ruth?”

“If they reopen the bar, I’m buying.”

“My name is Daniel.”

“I know.”

“How?”

“You left your passport on the table when you went to get napkins.”

He stared at her.

“You read my passport?”

“You left me with your suitcase. I needed to know who I was committing crimes with.”

Daniel laughed.

Outside, the snow kept falling.

Neither of them checked the departure board again.
