## Scene 1: A mother and her adult son

## Scene 2: Two old diplomats

Rain tapped the awning of the café overlooking the river. Ambassador Salim stirred his tea, eyes on the current. Across from him, Ambassador Hartley unfolded a paper napkin as if it were a treaty draft.

“You always pick places near water,” Hartley said. “You know I can’t swim.”

Hartley snorted. “You tried, once.”

They smiled. The waiter set down their plates—almond cake for Salim, soup for Hartley.

“I remember the chandelier that looked like a galaxy,” Salim said. “I remember pretending not to see you rewriting our clause with a fountain pen under the table.”

“It needed a soft verb,” Hartley said. “Shall should’ve been may.”

“Shall saves lives,” Salim said.

“May gets signed,” Hartley countered. He blew on his spoon. “How many years have we been having this argument?”

“Long enough to turn shalls into shalls-we-dance,” Salim said, tapping the edge of his cup. “You retired first. Coward.”

“Strategist,” Hartley corrected. “I left before I became someone I used to despise.”

Salim leaned back. “And did it work?”

Hartley watched a gull dart under the awning. “Depends on the day.”

“On mine,” Salim said, “I wake, read the news, and reach for the phone to call a minister who hasn’t needed me in five years.”

“You still teach?”

“I do,” Salim said. “Students with bright eyes and sharper questions. Last week, one asked me how to negotiate with a person whose career depends on not agreeing.”

“What did you tell them?” Hartley asked.

“I said, ‘Offer them a different victory.’” Salim toyed with a crumb. “And make sure they can claim it without lying.”

Hartley chuckled. “You always were kind.”

“You always were clinical,” Salim replied. “Between us, we made a decent surgeon.”

“If only we’d operated on the right patient,” Hartley said. He leaned forward. “Do you regret Oslo?”

“Which part?” Salim asked. “The airport, the handshake, the flash bulbs? The clause you turned to may?”

Salim took a sip. “Sometimes I think it’s our job to buy time.”

“For what?” Hartley asked.

“Diplomat,” Hartley corrected.

“And you?”

Hartley smiled. “I would swap a few ‘never’s for ‘not yet.’”

Salim raised his cup. “To the soft verbs.”

Hartley clinked his spoon against it. “And to rivers, relentless and honest.”

They ate in companionable silence, each thinking of letters they’d written and lives they could not count, the rain writing its own preamble on the awning overhead.

## Scene 4: A married couple after an affair

The kitchen was too clean. The counters shone the way gym floors do before a game no one wants to play. Laura folded the dish towel again even though it was already folded. Daniel stood near the fridge, hands in his pockets like they could hide him.

“I know,” she said, rubbing her temple. “I believe you’re sorry. I just don’t know if you’re sorry about the right things.”

“What are the right things?” he asked.

“That you took something from me without asking,” she said. “That you wrote a story with my name in it and didn’t let me read it.”

He sat. The chair scraped too loud. “I was lonely.”

“I was too,” she said. “You didn’t ask me to be less lonely with you.”

“I thought you were tired of me,” he said, a small, miserable laugh. “That thing you do with your mouth when I tell a joke? I decided it meant contempt.”

“It means the joke needs work,” she said, and for a second they both almost smiled. Then Laura’s face changed. “Who is she?”

“Her name doesn’t help,” he said.

“It helps me to picture a person instead of a shadow,” Laura said. “It helps me to know whether she wears perfume I’ll smell at the grocery store.”

He swallowed. “Her name is Alyssa. We met at the conference in March. It was—”

“Simple,” Laura finished for him. “Easier than the work here.”

“It felt like oxygen,” he said, ashamed. “I don’t know why.”

“Because we forget to open windows,” she said flatly. “Because we confuse inconvenience with love failing.”

“I ended it,” he said. “I told her. It’s over.”

Laura stared at the kettle. “Are you asking me to forgive you?”

“I’m asking what you need,” he said.

She looked at him, really looked. “I need the password to your email,” she said. “I need you to answer questions when I wake at three a.m. I need you to go to therapy even when you’re ‘busy.’ I need—space.” Her voice wavered. “And I need you not to tell me to hurry up and heal.”

“I can do those,” he said quickly. “All of them.”

“You can offer,” she said. “Doing is different.” She took a breath. “Also, I need you to know I don’t know yet. About us.”

He nodded, tears bright. “Okay.”

“And I need to say this,” she added, fingers gripping the edge of the counter. “There was a moment today where I remembered your hands on our baby’s back when she had a fever, and I thought, there is goodness. There is also this other thing. Both are true. I hate that both are true.”

“I hate it too,” he said. “I wish I could make it simple.”

“We tried simple,” she said. “We called it later. Later we’ll talk. Later we’ll take a trip. Later we’ll be brave.” She exhaled. “Later arrived without us.”

The tea went cool. Outside, a delivery truck rattled past, and the kitchen clock clicked.

“Will you stay tonight?” Daniel asked.

“Yes,” she said. “In the guest room. I don’t want you to be far. And I don’t want you near.”

He nodded. “Okay.”

He met her eyes. “Understood.”

She watched him fill it. The sound of water was ordinary, almost kind. It made room for a future that wasn’t promised and a present that, stubbornly, still existed.

## Scene 5: An officer and a subordinate

“They’re relaxed,” Avery lied.

“Yes, sir,” Avery said.

“Yesterday’s patrol,” Ruiz began. “You called an audible at checkpoint three. Why?”

Avery’s mouth was dry. “The alley felt wrong. The vendor at the corner always whistles. He didn’t.”

“You trusted your read,” Ruiz said. “Good. What did you miss?”

Avery stared at the map pinned to the wall. “I tunneled. My focus went narrow. I didn’t see the van idling two blocks down.”

“And?”

“And we were late to intercept,” Avery said. “Sanchez had to sprint. He almost ate pavement.”

Ruiz waited. “What else?”

Avery flinched. “I raised my voice on comms.”

“You yelled,” Ruiz said, not unkind. “At your people.”

“I was scared,” Avery admitted.

“Of what?” Ruiz asked.

“Of something happening I couldn’t un-happen,” Avery said quietly. “Of being the reason someone didn’t go home.”

Ruiz let that sit. The hum of the lights was too bright. “Okay,” he said. “Here’s what I saw. You made a call based on pattern recognition. That’s leadership. You also choked the net with adrenaline. That’s fixable.”

Avery nodded, a quick jerk.

Ruiz leaned forward. “You know what my first captain told me after I blew a stop at a checkpoint and made everyone do a dumb little dance around my mistake?”

“What?” Avery asked.

“He said, ‘You’re allowed to be afraid. You’re not allowed to be alone.’” Ruiz tapped the notepad. “You didn’t have to carry the whole moment by yourself. You have NCOs. You have me. You have a team.”

Avery’s throat worked. “I didn’t want to look weak.”

“Looking stubborn is worse,” Ruiz said. “You ever seen a pilot yank the stick harder and call it courage? That’s how you stall.”

Avery rubbed a hand over his face. “Sanchez won’t look at me.”

“Sanchez is nursing a bruise on his pride, not his body,” Ruiz said. “You will apologize for the yell. Not for the call.”

“Yes, sir,” Avery said.

“Now, what would you do differently?” Ruiz asked.

“Breathe,” Avery said. “Delegate. ‘Sanchez, eyes left. Park, that van’s yours. Miller, pulse comms with HQ.’ Not try to be all of them.”

“Good,” Ruiz said. “And?”

“Use plain language,” Avery added. “Not ‘possible anomaly’ at checkpoint three. Say, ‘Alley usual vendor missing, proceed with caution.’”

Ruiz smiled a little. “You’re improving yourself in real time. That’s the job.”

Avery stared down at his hands. “When do you stop hearing the what-ifs?”

Ruiz took a breath. “You don’t. You teach them to sit in the back seat and shut up while you drive.”

Ramirez’s jaw flexed. “That wasn’t a suggestion.”

“You disobeyed a direct order,” Ramirez began.

“I saved a kid,” Cole shot back.

“You endangered your unit,” Ramirez countered.

Cole’s hands curled into fists on his knees. “With respect, sir, my unit was behind cover. That boy wasn’t.”

“And now Corporal Diaz is in the field hospital with shrapnel in his leg,” Ramirez said. “Because you broke formation and forced everyone to adjust to you.”

Cole’s gaze dropped for a second. “I didn’t know there was a second IED.”

“That’s the point.” Ramirez leaned forward. “You never know. That’s why you stick to the plan.”

“The plan didn’t include a twelve-year-old in the middle of the road,” Cole said hotly.

“No,” Ramirez said quietly. “The plan didn’t include a twelve-year-old in the middle of the road. The plan was built around minimizing risk to my people.”

“This isn’t about hearts and minds,” Ramirez snapped. “This is about command and control.”

Cole’s jaw tightened. “Is there a difference?”

“Yes.” Ramirez’s stare was steady. “One gets people killed faster.”

“I’m not asking you to be made of stone,” Ramirez said. “I’m asking you to trust that sometimes we have to walk past things we can’t fix.”

“Well, I couldn’t,” Cole said. “Not this time.”

Ramirez rubbed a hand over his face. “You’re a good soldier, Cole. One of the best. That’s why this scares the hell out of me.”

“Scares you, sir?”

“Because good soldiers with big hearts make terrible martyrs,” Ramirez said. “And I don’t have enough men to lose one to hero syndrome.”

“I wasn’t trying to be a hero,” Cole muttered.

“Then what were you trying to be?”

“Human,” Cole said. “Sir.”

“Probably,” Ramirez said. “Minimum. Loss of pay. Extra duty. It could be worse.”

“Could be court-martial,” Cole said.

“It won’t be,” Ramirez said firmly. “Because I’m not recommending it.”

“Then you follow the order,” Ramirez said. “And you hate me for it. And I hate myself for it. And we bring ten people home instead of nine.”

“I know,” Ramirez said quietly. “That’s why leadership sucks.”

### 6. Two People Who Barely Know Each Other

The airport at midnight felt like an afterthought — half the kiosks closed, announcements echoing in a mostly empty terminal.

“Gate 47,” the overhead voice droned. “Departure delayed. New estimated boarding time: 1:35 a.m.”

A few people groaned. Most just sagged further into their plastic chairs.

Nora shifted her laptop bag off the seat beside her as a man approached, balancing a paper cup and a backpack. “Anyone sitting here?”

“No,” she said. “Go ahead.”

“Thanks.” He dropped into the chair, exhaling. “They say ‘weather in Chicago,’ but I’m pretty sure that’s code for ‘the pilot is napping.’”

She half-smiled. “Or they lost the plane.”

“Comforting,” he said. He took a sip of his coffee and made a face. “This tastes like burnt cardboard.”

“You bought coffee at midnight,” Nora said. “You did this to yourself.”

“Point.” He wiped his hand on his jeans, then glanced sideways at her. “I’m Jamal, by the way. Misery seatmate for the next… what, two hours?”

“Nora,” she said.

He nodded at her closed laptop. “You look like someone who’s about to be very productive.”

“I was,” she said. “Until delay number three.”

“Ah.” He checked the departures board. “Going or coming home?”

“Going,” she said. “Conference. You?”

“Coming,” he said. “My sister’s wedding. The rehearsal dinner was… an event.”

“Bad?” she asked.

“Let’s just say when your uncle starts a speech with ‘Back in my day, women knew—’” He trailed off, grimacing.

“Oof,” she said.

“Exactly.” He eyed her carry-on. “You’re traveling light.”

“Two days, one presentation, one mildly condescending panel of senior partners,” Nora said. “Anything more than this is unnecessary suffering.”

“Law?” he guessed.

“Consulting,” she corrected. “You?”

“Software,” he said. “I build boring things that make other boring things slightly less boring.”

She smiled faintly. “Specific.”

He shrugged. “My mom still tells people I ‘do computers.’”

“Mine tells people I ‘do business,’” Nora said. “As if that covers anything.”

“Parents are vague on principle,” he said. “It’s in the handbook.”

They fell into a companionable silence, punctuated by the squeak of a cleaning cart.

“You live in Minneapolis?” he asked after a minute.

“For now,” she said. “You?”

“Chicago. Hence my deep suspicion of ‘weather in Chicago.’ I’ve seen the radar. It’s fine.”

“Maybe the pilot is genuinely napping,” she said.

“If he is, I hope he’s good and rested,” Jamal replied. “I plan to be unconscious before we even take off.”

“Lucky,” Nora said. “I can’t sleep on planes.”

“Fear of crashing?” he asked.

“Fear of drooling on strangers,” she said. “Equally tragic.”

He laughed. “Fair.”

A woman at the end of the row started a loud phone conversation. Nora winced.

“So,” Jamal said, lowering his voice conspiratorially, “what’s the presentation about? On a scale from ‘networking’ to ‘paradigm shift.’”

She rolled her eyes. “Customer segmentation in mid-tier markets.”

He placed a hand over his heart. “Be still, my beating soul.”

“It’s worse,” she said. “There are pie charts.”

“Monstrous,” he said. “You should unionize.”

“What about you?” she asked. “What boring thing do you make less boring?”

“Internal tools,” he said. “You know those forms you hate at work? I make the magic that lets them auto-fill so you hate them five percent less.”

“I appreciate your service,” she said.

“You say that now,” he replied. “Until the system goes down and everyone yells at the ‘IT guy.’”

“Do you ever… want to do something else?” she asked, then immediately frowned. “Sorry. That’s weird. Ignore me. It’s the hour.”

He considered. “Sometimes. I play music. Badly. In my head, I’m a jazz pianist in a smoky bar.”

“In reality?” she prompted.

“In reality, my downstairs neighbor leaves notes on my door that say ‘There are headphones for a reason,’” he said.

She laughed. “Brutal.”

“What about you?” he asked. “Did you always dream of… pie charts?”

“No,” she said. “I wanted to be an architect.”

“What happened?”

“My father sat me down with a spreadsheet,” she said. “Showed me average salaries. Job security. How many years of school. He made a very convincing PowerPoint.”

“And you listened,” Jamal said.

“I was eighteen,” she said. “I thought adults knew things.”

“They do,” he said. “They know how to be afraid.”

She blinked at him. “That’s… unexpectedly profound for someone drinking cardboard.”

“Don’t tell my uncle,” he said. “He’ll revoke my man card.”

They shared a small, weary smile.

“Flight 327 to Chicago now boarding all passengers,” the overhead voice announced suddenly.

Jamal looked at his boarding pass. “That’s us.”

“Finally,” Nora said.

They gathered their bags and joined the slow shuffle toward the gate.

“You window or aisle?” he asked.

“Aisle,” she said. “Easier escape.”

“You make it sound like a hostage situation,” he said.

“Have you met airline seats?” she replied.

At the jet bridge, the stream of passengers compressed. They ended up side by side again.

“So,” Jamal said, “since we’ve known each other for, what, forty-three minutes, I feel entitled to ask a deeply personal question.”

She raised an eyebrow. “Bold.”

“Is there anyone waiting for you in Chicago?” he asked. “To hear about your thrilling segmentation insights?”

She hesitated. “No. You?”

He smiled without quite reaching his eyes. “Not anymore.”

“Ah.” She didn’t push.

“Sorry,” he said. “That was… heavy.”

“It’s okay,” she said. “Airports are for existential oversharing.”

“Is that in the brochure?” he asked.

“Fine print,” she said.

They reached the plane door. The flight attendant smiled mechanically. “Welcome aboard.”

As they stepped into the narrow aisle, Jamal said, “Well, Nora-who-does-business, if we don’t end up neighbors on this metal tube, nice almost-knowing you.”

“Likewise, Jamal-who-does-computers,” she said. “May your pilot be well-rested.”

“And may your pie charts be merciful,” he replied.

They checked their seat numbers.

“18C,” she said.

“22A,” he said. “Tragic.”

“Four rows away,” she said. “A gulf between us.”

“Star-crossed,” he said dryly.

They inched apart, swallowed by strangers and overhead bins.

Later, when the cabin lights dimmed and the engines hummed them away from the wet-lit airport, Nora glanced down the aisle. She could just see the corner of Jamal’s seat, the back of his head tilted against the window.

She thought, briefly, of jazz bars and PowerPoints, of the odd intimacy of telling the truth to someone who didn’t matter and therefore, somehow, did.

Then she opened her laptop, stared at her pie charts, and began to type.

## 5) An officer and a subordinate

The station’s fluorescent lights made everyone look slightly guilty. Lieutenant Ortega stood over the briefing table, a map of the precinct spread beneath his hands. Officer Kim hovered near the doorway with a notepad, trying to look both attentive and invisible.

Ortega tapped the map. “Talk me through your report from last night. The one you filed at 03:12.”

Kim’s throat tightened. “The traffic stop on Redfield. I wrote everything down.”

## 6) Two people who barely know each other

The elevator in Building C had a personality: slow, noisy, and prone to forcing acquaintances into conversation. Nadia pressed the button for the seventh floor and immediately regretted not taking the stairs.

The man glanced at the flickering inspection certificate, then at the ceiling. “It always does that noise?”

Nadia shrugged. “It’s a feature. Keeps you humble.”

He laughed, quick and surprised. “Right. Yeah. I’m Mateo, by the way. Sixth floor. Just moved in.”

“Nadia. Seventh. Welcome to the… vertical village,” she said.

“Thanks,” Mateo replied. “Still figuring out which key is which.”

Nadia gestured at his paper bag. “That looks promising. Bakery?”

Nadia blinked. “Did she say that to everyone or just you?”

“I didn’t ask,” he said. “I took the rye. I’m impressionable.”

Nadia smiled despite herself. “Is it working?”

Mateo considered. “I’m in an elevator that sounds like it’s chewing gravel, so… mixed results.”

The elevator jolted, then slowed. The lights dimmed for a half-second.

Nadia’s hand hovered near the emergency button. “Don’t do that,” she muttered at the ceiling.

Mateo glanced at her hand. “Does it get stuck often?”

“Not *often*,” Nadia said. “Just enough to build character.”

Mateo shifted his weight. “I hate that phrase.”

“Me too,” she admitted. “My mom says it when something expensive breaks.”

The elevator made a groan and stopped between floors.

They both stared at the closed doors like staring might shame them into opening.

Mateo’s voice came out careful. “Is this… normal?”

Nadia pressed the “Door Open” button. Nothing.

She tried again. Still nothing.

“Okay,” she said, forcing calm. “Now it’s building character.”

Mateo let out a short, breathy laugh. “Perfect.”

Nadia leaned toward the panel and pressed the call button. A faint crackle answered, then silence.

She pressed again. “Come on.”

Mateo looked at the seam between the doors. “Can we pry it open?”

Nadia shot him a look. “Do you have superpowers?”

“I have rye,” he offered, lifting the bag.

“Tempting,” Nadia said. “But no.”

Mateo took a breath. “Okay. So we’re stuck.”

“For a bit,” Nadia said. She tried the call button again, holding it down longer. This time the speaker popped, and a voice answered, distorted.

“Maintenance,” the voice said.

Nadia leaned in. “Hi, we’re stuck between six and seven in Building C.”

A pause. “Again?”

“Yes,” Nadia said, dry. “Again.”

“Hold tight,” the voice said. “Ten minutes.”

The speaker clicked off.

Mateo exhaled. “Ten minutes is survivable.”

“Unless you need to pee,” Nadia said.

Mateo froze. “Do you?”

“No,” she said quickly. “I was just—making conversation.”

“Great,” Mateo said. “Because now I have to think about it.”

Nadia leaned back against the wall. “Sorry.”

Mateo looked around the small space, then at Nadia, as if deciding something. “So. New neighbor bonding activity.”

“Nothing brings people together like minor infrastructure failure,” Nadia agreed.

Mateo lifted the bag again. “Want some bread?”

Nadia hesitated. “Is it weird to share bread with a stranger in an elevator?”

“It’s only weird if we make intense eye contact,” Mateo said.

Nadia laughed. “Fair.”

He opened the bag and tore off a piece with clean fingers, offering it. Nadia accepted, careful not to touch his hand—then immediately felt childish for thinking about it.

She took a bite. The bread was dense, fragrant, faintly sour in a comforting way.

“Oh,” Nadia said. “That’s actually—wow.”

Mateo looked pleased. “The rye is doing its job.”

Nadia chewed, then said, “So you just moved in. From where?”

“Across town,” Mateo said. “After a breakup. I needed a place that didn’t have… echoes.”

Nadia nodded, understanding more than she wanted to. “Yeah. Echoes are loud.”

Mateo glanced at her. “You?”

“Divorce,” Nadia said, then immediately regretted how blunt it sounded. “Sorry. Overshare.”

Mateo shook his head. “No. That’s… real.”

They stood in a quieter silence, the kind that didn’t demand jokes.

Nadia broke it gently. “How long were you together?”

Mateo looked at the floor indicator, stuck between numbers like a metaphor. “Four years. We weren’t mean to each other. We just… stopped translating.”

Nadia swallowed another bite of bread. “That’s a good way to say it.”

Mateo gave a small shrug. “I’ve had time to workshop the pain.”

Nadia laughed softly. “I should do that. My current phrasing is mostly swearing.”

Mateo smiled. “Swearing is honest.”

The elevator lights flickered again, then brightened. A mechanical clunk sounded, followed by a slow, juddering movement.

Nadia straightened. “Oh. We’re alive.”

Mateo braced a hand against the wall. “Welcome back, vertical village.”

The elevator crawled up and stopped with a final thump. The doors opened onto the sixth floor, slightly misaligned, but open.

Mateo stepped out, then turned back. “Hey, Nadia?”

“Yeah?”

He held up the bag. “If you want, I can bring you a piece sometime when it’s not an emergency ration.”

Nadia considered him—this almost-stranger with rain on his jacket and bread in his hands and a voice that didn’t try too hard.

“I’d like that,” she said. “And I can tell you which key is which. I’m annoyingly good at keys.”

Mateo’s smile widened. “Deal.”

As the doors tried to close again, Nadia added, “And if the elevator gets stuck, we’re bringing a full meal.”

Mateo pointed at her like she’d said something wise. “Absolutely. No more character-building on an empty stomach.”

The doors shut, and the elevator continued upward—slow, noisy, but somehow less unbearable than before.

## 1. Cold Storage
*A mother and her adult son*

The chest freezer had stood in the garage since 1994, and Irene defrosted it the way other people went to church: annually, with ceremony, and against her better judgment.

"Bin bag," she said.

Martin held it open. She dropped in something grey and rectangular, furred with ice.

"What was that?"

"Beef."

"Mum, it had a *label*. It said 2019."

"It said beef."

She went back in up to the shoulders. From the doorway the freezer looked like it was eating her. Martin had been in the house eleven minutes and had not yet said the sentence he'd rehearsed on the motorway, and the longer he stood holding a bin bag the more he suspected he'd been outmanoeuvred before he arrived.

"Peas," she said. "Peas, peas. Someone's soup. Whose soup is this?"

"Yours."

"It's got a face on it." She turned the container. Frost crystals had bloomed across the lid in a rough oval. "That's a face."

"That's ice."

"You've no imagination. You get that from your father." She set the soup on the workbench, apart from the bags, and Martin noticed that and filed it.

"Mum."

"Mm."

"I wanted to talk to you about—"

"Hold the bag open, you're letting it collapse."

He held the bag open. She excavated. A bag of blackcurrants from a garden that belonged to strangers now. Four vacuum-packed mackerel. A Christmas pudding of no determinable year.

"I've been offered something," he said. "In Aberdeen."

"I know."

He looked at the back of her head. "How do you know?"

"Your sister has the discretion of a car alarm." She straightened up, breathing hard, her forearms red to the elbow. "She rang Tuesday. She was very upset for me. I had to comfort her about my own abandonment. It took forty minutes."

"I'm not abandoning—"

"I know what you're not doing, Martin. I've known since Tuesday. I've had time to get past the interesting part." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "It's a good job?"

"It's a good job."

"Then that's that." She reached in again. "Your father applied for one up there once. 1981. Didn't get it. Sulked for a fortnight and then bought a boat he couldn't sail."

"You never told me that."

"You never asked me anything until you were thirty-five."

He laughed, and it came out wrong, too loud in the garage.

She had gone still, both hands down in the cold. When she lifted the bag out it was small, and the label on it was written in blue biro in handwriting that was not hers.

They both looked at it.

"Stock," she said. "He made stock every Sunday. I've never known what to do with it."

"You could use it."

"I could." She turned it over. "Bag."

He opened the bag. She didn't move.

"Or not," he said.

"Or not." She put it on the workbench, next to the soup with the face, and went back in for the rest.

## 2. The Annexe
*Two old diplomats*

The bar of the Hôtel Bellerive had not changed, which was, Odile thought, the entire reason either of them had come.

"They've kept the chairs," said Raymond.

"They've kept the *smell*. Ashtrays and lake water. Sit down, you're swaying."

"I'm eighty-one. Swaying is my remaining posture." He lowered himself with great care. "Was it a good funeral?"

"It was an efficient funeral. Three hundred people who all knew each other and none of whom could say what he actually did."

"He'd have liked that."

"He'd have written a note about it." Odile signalled the waiter without looking at him, a gesture she had perfected in 1979 and never had cause to revise. "You've been in London."

"I've been at a desk in Sussex."

"Doing what?"

"Remembering."

"Ah." The drinks came. She let them sit. "On paper?"

"On paper. There's a publisher. Young man. Very keen. He says the period is 'having a moment.'"

"How fortunate for the period." She turned her glass a quarter turn. "How far have you got?"

"1986."

"Then you have a year of grace before it stops being a memoir."

Raymond smiled at the lake. Out past the terrace the water was doing what it always did, which was nothing, patiently.

"You could ask me directly, Odile."

"I could. But then you'd have to answer directly, and neither of us was trained for the humiliation." She drank. "The annexe."

"The annexe."

"You understand it is still not a document."

"It was never a document. It was a paragraph read aloud in a room with the windows open." He set down his glass. "Four of us in that room. Two are dead. Hendricks died Tuesday. That leaves the arithmetic rather stark."

"And you've decided the arithmetic obliges you."

"I've decided that when I am dead there will be no one left who knows why the convoys stopped for eleven days."

"There is no one left who *cares* why the convoys stopped for eleven days."

"There are perhaps four thousand people who care very much," said Raymond, "and none of them will ever be able to tell you so."

Odile was quiet. A boat went by, unhurried.

"You want absolution," she said at last, "and you have chosen to seek it from the general public, who are notoriously bad at it."

"I want the record."

"The record will not say what you did. The record will say what you were seen to have done, which is a different animal and much hungrier." She leaned forward. "Raymond. We chose. We were given a bad room and a worse clock and we chose. I have never once pretended it was clean. But I have also never handed it to a young man who is *keen*."

"You'd rather it went with us."

"I'd rather it went with us."

He looked at her for a long moment, with something in his face that was not quite affection.

"You'll read it," he said, "before."

"Of course I'll read it," said Odile. "I'll read every line, and I'll tell you exactly which of them to cut, and you'll cut none of them, and then we'll have lunch. Same as ever."

## 3. Behind the Sports Hall
*Two schoolgirls*

The wall behind the sports hall was cold through your skirt but nobody could see you from the staff room, which made it the best place in the school.

"You've got my hoodie," said Callie.

"I know." Priya didn't take it off. "It's mine now. Adverse possession."

"That's not a real thing."

"It's completely a real thing. My uncle's a solicitor. If you occupy something for long enough it becomes yours."

"You've had it nine days."

"I've had it *emotionally* for much longer."

Callie held out the crisps. Priya took four, which was more than the treaty allowed, and Callie let it go, which meant something.

They ate. Someone was doing shuttle runs on the field and being shouted at.

"You sat with Marguerite again," Priya said.

"So?"

"I'm not saying anything. I'm reporting the news."

"You're doing the voice."

"I don't have a voice."

"You have a voice," said Callie, "and it's the voice, and you're doing it."

Priya folded the crisp packet into a triangle, very precisely, corner over corner, the way her brother had taught her.

"She's got a trampoline," Callie said. "That's it. That's the whole reason. It's not even a nice one, it's got a hole in the net."

"You don't have to explain."

"I know I don't have to explain, I'm *choosing* to explain, which is worse for me, so you could be nicer about it."

"I'm being extremely nice." Priya flicked the triangle at her. It bounced off her knee. "I'm being nice in a way that's costing me a lot personally."

Callie laughed. Then she stopped laughing, because Priya hadn't.

"What," she said.

"Nothing."

"Priya."

Priya looked at the field. Shuttle runs. Shouting.

"My dad's got a flat," she said. "In Aylesbury. He got it in like — March. So he's had it since March and I found out on Sunday because there were keys."

Callie didn't say anything. Which was, Priya thought, the single most correct thing anyone had done all week.

"Keys with a tag on," she said. "A little cardboard tag. From the estate agent. Just sitting in the bowl by the door with all the normal keys. Like it was nothing."

"That's rank."

"It's so rank."

"Do you have to go there?"

"Every other weekend from September. There's a room that's supposed to be mine. He said it like it was a present. He said, *there's a room that's yours,* and I had to do a face." Priya did the face. It was terrible.

"That's a horrific face."

"I *know*. I've been doing it all week. My cheeks hurt."

Callie shuffled along the wall until their arms were touching and held the crisps out again, all of them, tipped over so Priya could reach in properly.

"You can keep the hoodie," she said.

"Obviously I can keep the hoodie. Don't make it a *gift*, that ruins it."

"Fine. It's stolen."

"Thank you," said Priya. "That's much better."

## 4. The Back Step
*A married couple, after an affair*

"Head down," Nadine said.

Tom put his head down. The comb went through, then the scissors, small metallic bites just behind his ear. She had cut his hair on this step for nineteen years, out of a habit that had started as thrift and become something neither of them had ever needed to name. She had not stopped in March. He had noticed that she had not stopped, and had never once mentioned it, because he understood roughly what it would cost him to be wrong about what it meant.

"Sarah rang," she said.

"Mm."

"Dev's fortieth. The twelfth. There's a room booked at the Lamb."

"Right."

"Don't do *right*."

"I'm not doing anything, you've got scissors by my carotid."

"Head *down*."

He put his head down. A blackbird was working the lawn over by the shed. The sun had gone off the step twenty minutes ago and it was getting properly cold, and neither of them mentioned that either.

"What did you say to her?" he asked.

"I said I'd check with you."

"You've never checked with me about anything in your life."

"I know," Nadine said. "I heard myself say it. It was disgusting." The comb again. "I bought us four days."

"So what do you want to do?"

"I want you to say what you want to do."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed, "it's a test, and you're currently failing it, and I'd like you to try harder."

He was quiet for a bit. Snip. Snip.

"They know," he said.

"They know."

"So there'll be a moment. When we come in. There's going to be a moment about a second and a half long where everyone decides how to be, and I'll be able to feel it on my face."

"Yes."

"And you'll be standing next to me while it happens."

"Yes."

"I don't want to make you stand there."

"That," said Nadine, "is the first true thing you've said since Easter." She turned his head with two fingers under his jaw, not gently. "But it's still not what I asked."

"I want to go," he said.

"Why?"

"Because if we don't go on the twelfth we won't go in November either, and then it'll be next year, and then we'll be the people who don't go to things. And I've watched that happen to other people and it looks like drowning very slowly in your own front room."

The scissors stopped.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay we go?"

"Okay we go." She started again, the same rhythm. "You'll drive. I'll want to drink and I'll want to be able to leave at eleven without a discussion."

"Fine."

"And if it's bad we leave. Not a scene. I'll say I've got a headache and you'll look sympathetic and unsurprised."

"I can do unsurprised."

"You're extraordinary at it," she said, and he decided to let that one go past.

She stepped back and looked at him.

"Done."

"Do the back again," Tom said.

She stood there a second. Then she came round behind him and did the back again.

## 5. Second Statement

The generator was off for the night, so the light in the container came from a lamp Rusk had set on its side to stop it glaring, and everything in the room had two shadows going in different directions.

"Get some sleep, Corporal," he said. "We'll do this again in the morning."

## 6. Flat-Pack
*Two people who barely know each other*

"You're the bookcase," said the man in the doorway.

"I'm the bookcase," Dawn agreed.

"Come in. Mind the — yeah. That."

*That* was a rolled carpet lying across the hall like something that had been shot. Dawn stepped over it. The flat beyond was three-quarters empty in a way that made the remaining quarter look accidental: a lamp, a stack of plates, a wall with four pale rectangles where pictures had been.

"It's through here. I should say up front, I did put in the ad that it was assembled, and I want to be honest with you, I've had a look at it now and I don't think it comes apart."

"Everything comes apart."

"That's a bleak thing to say to a man in my situation."

Dawn looked at him properly for the first time. Mid-forties, in a jumper he'd clearly been wearing for several days of this.

"Sorry," she said.

"No, it's factually correct. Just badly timed." He crouched by the bookcase. "There are these plastic — do you see, on this side?"

"Cam locks. You turn them with a flathead."

"Do I have a flathead."

"I have a flathead." She got it out of her bag. He looked at it with real feeling.

"You came prepared."

"I bought a bookcase from a stranger on the internet. I came prepared for a variety of outcomes."

They worked at it. The shelves came out easier than either had expected, and then the whole back panel gave at once with a sound like a small animal being disappointed.

"Oh," he said.

"That's fine. That's tacked on. It's meant to do that."

"Is it."

"No, but I've got a staple gun."

He laughed, once, surprised into it, and then sat back on his heels and rubbed his face with the back of his wrist.

"Sorry. Long month."

"Moving's horrible."

"It's not really moving. She's got the house. This was the — this was meant to be the flat where I wrote a book." He gestured at the pale rectangles. "It's been fourteen months. I've written eleven thousand words and bought a bookcase."

"That's not a bad ratio," said Dawn. "I've been in mine four years and I've got no shelves at all. Books in stacks. Load-bearing stacks. One of them holds up a plant."

"That's structurally interesting."

"It's structurally a disaster, which is why I'm here."

They got the sides off. She stacked the panels by the door, in order, screws bagged and taped to the top one, and he watched her do it with an expression of mild wonder.

"You've done this before."

"I move a lot." She straightened up. "Right. That's me."

At the door she stopped. The lamp was still standing on the floor, on its own, in the empty room.

"Does the lamp go with it?"

He looked at the lamp for a second longer than the question required.

"You don't want the lamp."

"I really don't," said Dawn. "But I'll take it."

"Right," he said. "Yes. Thanks."

She took the lamp.
